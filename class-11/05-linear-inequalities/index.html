Introduction to Inequalities, Notations & Fundamental Algebraic Rules

Linear Inequalities: Introduction, Notations & Fundamental Algebraic Rules

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Notations

In Euclidean algebra, an equation is a statement of exact numerical equality ($A = B$). However, physical constraints, resource limits, and geometric domains in real-world scenarios are bounded by bounds rather than exact values (e.g., maximum load, minimum speed, non-negative dimensions).

An inequality is a formal statement involving real numbers or algebraic expressions connected by order relations.

1.1 Core Definitions & Axiomatic Ordering in $\mathbb{R}$

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

The set of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ is a linearly ordered field governed by the Axiom of Trichotomy:

Axiom of Trichotomy:
For any two arbitrary real numbers $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, exactly one of the following relations holds true: 1. $a < b$ ($a$ is strictly less than $b$) 2. $a = b$ ($a$ is equal to $b$) 3. $a > b$ ($a$ is strictly greater than $b$)

Geometric Interpretation on the Real Line

Let the real number line be oriented horizontally with values increasing monotonically from left ($-\infty$) to right ($+\infty$). - $a < b$ signifies that the point representing $a$ lies strictly to the left of the point representing $b$. - $a > b$ signifies that the point representing $a$ lies strictly to the right of the point representing $b$. - The distance between $a$ and $b$ is $|b - a|$. Thus:

$$ b > a \iff b - a > 0 $$

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

1.3 Interval Notation & Set-Theoretic Equivalences

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$:

  1. Open Interval:

$$ (a, b) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x < b\} $$

  1. Closed Interval:

$$ [a, b] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \le x \le b\} $$

  1. Semi-Open / Semi-Closed Intervals:

$$ [a, b) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \le x < b\} $$

$$ (a, b] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x \le b\} $$

  1. Unbounded (Infinite) Intervals:

$$ (a, \infty) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > a\}, \quad (-\infty, b] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \le b\}, \quad (-\infty, \infty) = \mathbb{R} $$

Note on Infinity Symbols:
Symbols $\infty$ and $-\infty$ are not real numbers; they denote unbounded directional behavior. Consequently, brackets adjacent to $\pm\infty$ must always be round (open): $(-\infty, \cdot)$ or $(\cdot, \infty)$.

2. Fundamental Algebraic Rules & Step-by-Step Derivations

The algebraic manipulation of inequalities differs fundamentally from that of linear equations. The validity of each operation relies directly on the Ordered Field Axioms of $\mathbb{R}^+ = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > 0\}$.

Rule 1: Addition and Subtraction of Constants

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem 1:
Equal numbers may be added to (or subtracted from) both sides of an inequality without altering the direction of the inequality sign:

$$ a < b \iff a + c < b + c \quad \forall \, a, b, c \in \mathbb{R} $$

$$ a < b \iff a - c < b - c \quad \forall \, a, b, c \in \mathbb{R} $$

Formal Derivation:

  • Given: $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a < b$.
  • To Prove: $a + c < b + c$ and $a - c < b - c$.
  • Proof: By definition of the ordering relation on $\mathbb{R}$:

$$ a < b \iff (b - a) \in \mathbb{R}^+ \quad \text{(i.e., } b - a > 0\text{)} $$

Consider the difference between $(b + c)$ and $(a + c)$:

$$ (b + c) - (a + c) = b + c - a - c = b - a $$

Since $b - a > 0$, it follows directly that:

$$ (b + c) - (a + c) > 0 $$

By definition of inequality:

$$ a + c < b + c $$

Setting $c = -k$ gives:

$$ a - k < b - k $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Rule 2: Multiplication and Division by a Strictly Positive Scalar ($c > 0$)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem 2:
Both sides of an inequality can be multiplied (or divided) by the same strictly positive number without changing the direction of the inequality sign:

$$ a < b \text{ and } c > 0 \implies ac < bc \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{a}{c} < \frac{b}{c} $$

Formal Derivation:

  • Given: $a < b$ and $c \in \mathbb{R}^+$ ($c > 0$).
  • To Prove: $ac < bc$ and $\frac{a}{c} < \frac{b}{c}$.
  • Proof:

$$ a < b \implies (b - a) > 0 $$

Since $\mathbb{R}^+$ is closed under multiplication (Axiom of Positive Closure: if $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^+$, then $x \cdot y \in \mathbb{R}^+$):

$$ (b - a) \cdot c > 0 $$

Applying the distributive law of multiplication over addition:

$$ bc - ac > 0 $$

By the definition of strict inequality:

$$ ac < bc $$

Now consider $c > 0 \implies \frac{1}{c} > 0$. Multiplying $(b - a) > 0$ by $\frac{1}{c} \in \mathbb{R}^+$:

$$ (b - a) \cdot \frac{1}{c} > 0 \implies \frac{b}{c} - \frac{a}{c} > 0 \implies \frac{a}{c} < \frac{b}{c} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Rule 3: Multiplication and Division by a Strictly Negative Scalar ($c < 0$)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem 3 (Sign Reversal Theorem):
When both sides of an inequality are multiplied or divided by the same negative real number, the direction of the inequality sign must be reversed:

$$ a < b \text{ and } c < 0 \implies ac > bc \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{a}{c} > \frac{b}{c} $$

Formal Derivation:

  • Given: $a < b$ and $c < 0$.
  • To Prove: $ac > bc$.
  • Proof:

$$ a < b \implies (b - a) > 0 $$

Since $c < 0$, by definition of additive inverses in ordered fields, $(-c) > 0$.
Using the closure of $\mathbb{R}^+$ under multiplication, multiply $(b - a)$ by $(-c)$:

$$ (b - a)(-c) > 0 $$

Expanding using distributive axioms:

$$ -bc + ac > 0 \implies ac - bc > 0 $$

By definition of inequality, $x - y > 0 \iff x > y$:

$$ ac > bc $$

Similarly, since $c < 0 \implies -\frac{1}{c} > 0$:

$$ (b - a)\left(-\frac{1}{c}\right) > 0 \implies -\frac{b}{c} + \frac{a}{c} > 0 \implies \frac{a}{c} > \frac{b}{c} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Rule 4: Transposition of Quantities

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem 4:
Any term can be transposed from one side of an inequality to the other with its sign flipped, exactly as in equations:

$$ a + b < c \iff a < c - b $$

Formal Derivation:

  • Given: $a + b < c$.
  • To Prove: $a < c - b$.
  • Proof: Applying Rule 1, subtract $b$ from both sides:

$$ (a + b) - b < c - b $$

Using the associative and additive inverse axioms:

$$ a + (b - b) < c - b \implies a + 0 < c - b \implies a < c - b $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Rule 5: Reciprocal of Real Numbers

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem 5:
If $a$ and $b$ have the same sign (i.e., $ab > 0$):

$$ a < b \iff \frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b} $$

Caution: If $a$ and $b$ have opposite signs ($a < 0 < b$), the relation remains $\frac{1}{a} < \frac{1}{b}$.

Formal Derivation:

  • Given: $a < b$ and $ab > 0$.
  • To Prove: $\frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b}$.
  • Proof: Since $ab > 0$, its reciprocal $\frac{1}{ab} > 0$.
    Applying Rule 2, multiply both sides of $a < b$ by $\frac{1}{ab}$:

$$ a \cdot \left(\frac{1}{ab}\right) < b \cdot \left(\frac{1}{ab}\right) $$

$$ \frac{a}{ab} < \frac{b}{ab} \implies \frac{1}{b} < \frac{1}{a} \iff \frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

1. IN-DEPTH PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & MATHEMATICAL INTUITION

1.1 Fundamentals & Definitions (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐)

Definition 1.1: Statement of Inequality

An inequality is a mathematical statement involving order relations between two real numbers or algebraic expressions. The order relations are defined using the relation symbols: - $<$ : "is strictly less than" - $\le$ : "is less than or equal to" (slack/non-strict inequality) - $>$ : "is strictly greater than" - $\ge$ : "is greater than or equal to" (slack/non-strict inequality)

Definition 1.2: Linear Inequality in One Variable

An inequality is said to be a linear inequality in one variable (say $x$) if it can be transformed into one of the following canonical forms:

$$ ax + b < 0 $$

$$ ax + b \le 0 $$

$$ ax + b > 0 $$

$$ ax + b \ge 0 $$

where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $a \neq 0$. The degree of the variable $x$ is strictly $1$.

Definition 1.3: Solution and Solution Set

  • Any real value of the variable $x$ which makes the given inequality a true numerical statement is called a solution of the inequality.
  • The set of all possible solutions is termed the solution set.
  • The collection of allowed values for $x$ is known as the replacement set (or domain of the variable), typically denoted by $\mathbb{N}$ (Natural numbers), $\mathbb{W}$ (Whole numbers), $\mathbb{Z}$ (Integers), or $\mathbb{R}$ (Real numbers).

1.2 Axiomatic Foundation: Order Properties of Real Numbers (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

The algebraic manipulation of inequalities is governed by the order axioms of the real field $(\mathbb{R}, +, \cdot, \le)$.

Axiom 1: Trichotomy Law

For any two real numbers $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, exactly one of the following holds:

$$ a < b, \quad a = b, \quad \text{or} \quad a > b $$

Axiom 2: Transitivity

$$ \forall a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}, \quad (a < b \land b < c) \implies a < c $$

Axiom 3: Additive Compatibility

$$ \forall a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}, \quad a < b \implies a + c < b + c $$

Axiom 4: Multiplicative Compatibility

$$ \forall a, b \in \mathbb{R}, \quad a < b \implies \begin{cases} ac < bc & \text{if } c > 0 \\ ac > bc & \text{if } c < 0 \end{cases} $$

1.3 The Fundamental NCERT Rules of Algebraic Manipulation (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

To obtain an equivalent inequality (an inequality having the exact same solution set), two rules must be strictly adhered to:

Rule 1: Addition/Subtraction of Equal Quantities

Statement: Equal numbers may be added to (or subtracted from) both sides of an inequality without altering the sign or direction of inequality.

$$ \text{If } A \le B, \text{ then } A + k \le B + k \quad \text{and} \quad A - k \le B - k, \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{R} $$

Rule 2: Multiplication/Division by Non-Zero Constants

Statement (Part A - Positive Multiplier): Both sides of an inequality can be multiplied (or divided) by the same positive number without altering the direction of inequality.

$$ \text{If } A \le B \text{ and } k > 0, \text{ then } kA \le kB \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{A}{k} \le \frac{B}{k} $$

Statement (Part B - Negative Multiplier): When both sides are multiplied (or divided) by the same negative number, the direction of the inequality reverses (i.e., $<$ becomes $>$, $\le$ becomes $\ge$, and vice versa).

$$ \text{If } A \le B \text{ and } k < 0, \text{ then } kA \ge kB \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{A}{k} \ge \frac{B}{k} $$

2. COMPLETE STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL PROOFS & DERIVATIONS

Theorem 2.1: Additive Inversion Reverses Inequality Direction (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ \text{Prove that for all } a, b \in \mathbb{R}, \quad a < b \iff -a > -b $$

Proof:

Given: $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a < b$.
To Prove: $-a > -b$.

Step 1: By Definition of order in $\mathbb{R}$,

$$ a < b \implies b - a \in \mathbb{P} $$

where $\mathbb{P} = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x > 0\}$ is the set of strictly positive real numbers.

Step 2: Rewrite the difference $b - a$ by introducing identities:

$$ b - a = -a - (-b) $$

Step 3: Since $b - a \in \mathbb{P}$, it follows that:

$$ -a - (-b) \in \mathbb{P} $$

Step 4: By definition of the relation $>$, if $x - y \in \mathbb{P}$, then $x > y$. Taking $x = -a$ and $y = -b$:

$$ -a > -b $$

Converse:
Assume $-a > -b$. Then:

$$ -a - (-b) \in \mathbb{P} \implies -a + b \in \mathbb{P} \implies b - a \in \mathbb{P} \implies a < b $$

$$ \text{Hence, } a < b \iff -a > -b. \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 2.2: Multiplication by a Negative Real Reverses Inequality Sign (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ \text{Prove that if } a < b \text{ and } c < 0, \text{ then } ac > bc. $$

Proof:

Given: $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a < b$ and $c < 0$.
To Prove: $ac > bc$.

Step 1: Translate given conditions into the positive cone $\mathbb{P}$:

$$ a < b \implies (b - a) \in \mathbb{P} $$

$$ c < 0 \implies -c \in \mathbb{P} $$

Step 2: By the closure property of $\mathbb{P}$ under real multiplication:

$$ \forall x, y \in \mathbb{P}, \quad x \cdot y \in \mathbb{P} $$

Substitute $x = (b - a)$ and $y = -c$:

$$ (b - a)(-c) \in \mathbb{P} $$

Step 3: Apply the distributive property of multiplication over addition:

$$ (b - a)(-c) = b(-c) - a(-c) = -bc + ac = ac - bc $$

Step 4: Therefore:

$$ ac - bc \in \mathbb{P} $$

Step 5: By definition of strict inequality:

$$ ac - bc \in \mathbb{P} \implies ac > bc $$

$$ \text{Hence proved.} \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 2.3: Reciprocal Properties of Inequalities (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ \text{Prove that if } a \text{ and } b \text{ have the same sign and } a < b, \text{ then } \frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b}. $$

Proof:

Given: $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $ab > 0$ and $a < b$.
To Prove: $\frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b}$.

Step 1: Since $a < b$, we have:

$$ b - a > 0 $$

Step 2: Since $ab > 0$, its reciprocal is also strictly positive:

$$ \frac{1}{ab} > 0 $$

Step 3: By Multiplicative Compatibility (Axiom 4), multiply $(b - a) > 0$ by the positive quantity $\frac{1}{ab}$:

$$ (b - a) \cdot \frac{1}{ab} > 0 \cdot \frac{1}{ab} $$

Step 4: Distribute the left-hand side:

$$ \frac{b}{ab} - \frac{a}{ab} > 0 \implies \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b} > 0 $$

Step 5: Adding $\frac{1}{b}$ to both sides (Rule 1):

$$ \frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b} $$

$$ \text{Hence proved.} \quad \blacksquare $$

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Fundamental Definitions and NCERT Terminology

An inequality is a formal statement involving real numbers or real algebraic expressions related by the relational order symbols: - $<$ ("is strictly less than") - $\le$ ("is less than or equal to" / "at most") - $>$ ("is strictly greater than") - $\ge$ ("is greater than or equal to" / "at least")

Definition 1.1: Linear Inequality in One Variable

An algebraic inequality containing a single indeterminate $x$ with degree $1$ is termed a linear inequality in one variable. Its canonical forms are:

$$ ax + b < 0, \quad ax + b \le 0, \quad ax + b > 0, \quad ax + b \ge 0 \quad (a, b \in \mathbb{R}, \; a \neq 0) $$

  • Strict Inequality: Involves $<$ or $>$. Boundary points are excluded from the solution set.
  • Slack (or Weak) Inequality: Involves $\le$ or $\ge$. Boundary points are admitted provided they satisfy the domain restrictions.
  • Solution: Any real value of the variable which, when substituted, renders the inequality a true numerical statement.
  • Solution Set: The subset of the replacement set (usually $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{Z}$, or $\mathbb{W}$) containing all valid solutions, expressed via interval notation or set-builder notation.

1.2 The Real-Life Mathematical Modeling Framework ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Translating colloquial English into strict algebraic statements requires careful mapping of vernacular phrases to relational operators:

"At most $k$", "A maximum of $k$", "Not exceeding $k$" Less than or equal to $X \le k$

Every real-life constraint induces a ray or closed/open segment on $\mathbb{R}$: 1. Open Interval $(a, b) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a < x < b\}$: Geometrically represented by hollow circles (unshaded nodes) at $x = a$ and $x = b$, connected by a thick solid line segment. 2. Closed Interval $[a, b] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : a \le x \le b\}$: Geometrically represented by filled circles (solid shaded nodes) at $x = a$ and $x = b$. 3. Infinite Half-Lines $[a, \infty) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x \ge a\}$: Shaded solid ray directed towards $+\infty$ originating with a solid node at $a$.

1.4 The Order Axioms of Real Numbers ($\mathbb{R}$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The real number line is ordered by the following fundamental field properties:

  1. Trichotomy Property: For any two real numbers $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, exactly one of the following holds:

$$ a < b, \quad a = b, \quad \text{or} \quad a > b $$

  1. Transitivity:

$$ (a < b \;\land\; b < c) \implies a < c $$

  1. Additive Invariance:

$$ a < b \iff a + c < b + c \quad \forall a, b, c \in \mathbb{R} $$

  1. Multiplicative Order Inversion:

$$ a < b \iff \begin{cases} ac < bc, & \text{if } c > 0 \\ ac > bc, & \text{if } c < 0 \end{cases} $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: Multiplicative Inversion Under Negative Scaling ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem: Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$. If $a < b$ and $c < 0$, then $ac > bc$.

  • Given: $a < b$ and $c < 0$.
  • To Prove: $ac > bc$.
  • Axioms Utilized:
  • $x < y \iff y - x \in \mathbb{P}$ (where $\mathbb{P}$ is the set of strictly positive real numbers).
  • Closure of $\mathbb{P}$ under multiplication: If $u, v \in \mathbb{P}$, then $u \cdot v \in \mathbb{P}$.
  • Definition of negation: $c < 0 \iff -c \in \mathbb{P}$.

Proof Steps:

$$ \text{By definition of strict inequality, } a < b \implies (b - a) \in \mathbb{P} $$

$$ \text{Similarly, } c < 0 \implies (0 - c) = -c \in \mathbb{P} $$

$$ \text{By the closure property of } \mathbb{P} \text{ under multiplication:} $$

$$ (b - a) \cdot (-c) \in \mathbb{P} $$

$$ \text{Applying the distributive property of real numbers:} $$

$$ b(-c) - a(-c) \in \mathbb{P} $$

$$ -bc - (-ac) \in \mathbb{P} $$

$$ ac - bc \in \mathbb{P} $$

$$ \text{By definition of strict inequality, } (ac - bc) \in \mathbb{P} \iff ac > bc $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Transposition of Quantities Across an Inequality ⭐⭐⭐

Theorem: If $a + x \le b$, then $x \le b - a$.

  • Given: $a + x \le b$ where $a, b, x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • To Prove: $x \le b - a$.

Proof Steps:

$$ \text{Given: } a + x \le b $$

$$ \text{Add the additive inverse } (-a) \in \mathbb{R} \text{ to both sides (Additive Invariance Axiom):} $$

$$ (a + x) + (-a) \le b + (-a) $$

$$ \text{Using the Associative and Commutative properties of addition:} $$

$$ (x + a) + (-a) \le b - a $$

$$ x + (a + (-a)) \le b - a $$

$$ x + 0 \le b - a $$

$$ x \le b - a $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: Mixture Concentration Invariance Theorem ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem: Let a solution of volume $V_1$ have solute concentration $c_1\%$ (by volume). When mixed with an added solution of volume $x$ at concentration $c_2\%$ ($c_1 \neq c_2$), the concentration of the resultant solution $C(x)$ satisfies:

$$ C(x) = \frac{c_1 V_1 + c_2 x}{V_1 + x} $$

Furthermore, if $c_2 > c_1$, $C(x)$ is a strictly monotonically increasing function for $x \ge 0$, bounded above by $c_2$.

  • Given: $V_1 > 0$, $x \ge 0$, $0 \le c_1 < c_2 \le 100$.
  • To Prove: $C(x)$ is strictly increasing on $[0, \infty)$ and $\lim_{x \to \infty} C(x) = c_2$.

Proof Steps:

$$ \text{Total volume of solute in initial solution } = \left(\frac{c_1}{100}\right) V_1 $$

$$ \text{Volume of solute added } = \left(\frac{c_2}{100}\right) x $$

$$ \text{Total volume of final mixture } = V_1 + x $$

$$ \text{Final concentration percentage } C(x) = \frac{\left(\frac{c_1}{100}\right) V_1 + \left(\frac{c_2}{100}\right) x}{V_1 + x} \times 100 = \frac{c_1 V_1 + c_2 x}{V_1 + x} $$

$$ \text{Rewrite } C(x) \text{ by algebraic decomposition:} $$

$$ C(x) = \frac{c_2(V_1 + x) - c_2 V_1 + c_1 V_1}{V_1 + x} = c_2 - \frac{(c_2 - c_1)V_1}{V_1 + x} $$

$$ \text{Let } 0 \le x_1 < x_2. \text{ Since } V_1 > 0: $$

$$ V_1 + x_1 < V_1 + x_2 $$

$$ \text{Taking reciprocals on positive quantities reverses inequality:} $$

$$ \frac{1}{V_1 + x_1} > \frac{1}{V_1 + x_2} $$

$$ \text{Since } c_2 > c_1 \implies (c_2 - c_1)V_1 > 0, \text{ multiplying preserves order:} $$

$$ \frac{(c_2 - c_1)V_1}{V_1 + x_1} > \frac{(c_2 - c_1)V_1}{V_1 + x_2} $$

$$ \text{Multiplying by } -1 \text{ reverses inequality:} $$

$$ -\frac{(c_2 - c_1)V_1}{V_1 + x_1} < -\frac{(c_2 - c_1)V_1}{V_1 + x_2} $$

$$ \text{Adding } c_2 \text{ to both sides:} $$

$$ c_2 - \frac{(c_2 - c_1)V_1}{V_1 + x_1} < c_2 - \frac{(c_2 - c_1)V_1}{V_1 + x_2} \implies C(x_1) < C(x_2) $$

$$ \text{Hence, } C(x) \text{ is strictly increasing on } [0, \infty). \quad \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Problem 3.2: Chemical Dilution & Acid Solution Mixture (NCERT Exemplar) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem: A manufacturer has $600$ litres of a $12\%$ solution of acid. How many litres of a $30\%$ acid solution must be added to it so that the acid content in the resulting mixture will be more than $15\%$ but less than $18\%$?

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Set up the variable and volume expressions. Let $x$ litres of the $30\%$ acid solution be added. Physical boundary condition:

$$ x > 0 $$

$$ \text{Total volume of resulting mixture} = (600 + x) \text{ litres} $$

  • Step 2: Formulate the solute balance equation.

$$ \text{Acid from initial solution} = 12\% \text{ of } 600 = \frac{12}{100} \times 600 = 72 \text{ litres} $$

$$ \text{Acid from added solution} = 30\% \text{ of } x = \frac{30}{100} \times x = 0.30x \text{ litres} $$

$$ \text{Total acid content in mixture} = 72 + 0.30x $$

  • Step 3: Construct the simultaneous system of inequalities. The resulting concentration must be strictly between $15\%$ and $18\%$:

$$ 15\% \text{ of } (600 + x) < 72 + 0.30x < 18\% \text{ of } (600 + x) $$

$$ \frac{15}{100}(600 + x) < 72 + \frac{30}{100}x < \frac{18}{100}(600 + x) $$

  • Step 4: Solve Inequality I (Lower Bound).

$$ \frac{15}{100}(600 + x) < 72 + \frac{30}{100}x $$

Multiply the entire inequality by $100$ (since $100 > 0$):

$$ 15(600 + x) < 7200 + 30x $$

$$ 9000 + 15x < 7200 + 30x $$

Subtract $15x$ and $7200$ from both sides:

$$ 9000 - 7200 < 30x - 15x $$

$$ 1800 < 15x $$

Divide by $+15$:

$$ x > \frac{1800}{15} \implies x > 120 $$

  • Step 5: Solve Inequality II (Upper Bound).

$$ 72 + \frac{30}{100}x < \frac{18}{100}(600 + x) $$

Multiply by $100$:

$$ 7200 + 30x < 18(600 + x) $$

$$ 7200 + 30x < 10800 + 18x $$

Subtract $18x$ and $7200$ from both sides:

$$ 30x - 18x < 10800 - 7200 $$

$$ 12x < 3600 $$

Divide by $+12$:

$$ x < \frac{3600}{12} \implies x < 300 $$

  • Step 6: Determine the intersection of solution sets. Combining both constraints:

$$ (x > 120) \cap (x < 300) \implies 120 < x < 300 $$

  • Step 7: Conclusion. More than $120$ litres but less than $300$ litres of $30\%$ acid solution must be added.

$$ \text{Interval Form: } x \in (120, 300) $$

Problem 3.3: Consecutive Odd Integers Constraint ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem: Find all pairs of consecutive odd positive integers, both of which are smaller than $10$, such that their sum is more than $11$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Variable Definition. Let the smaller positive odd integer be $x$. The next consecutive odd positive integer is $x + 2$. Since they are positive integers:

$$ x > 0, \quad x \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, \dots\} $$

  • Step 2: Translate Problem Constraints into Inequalities.
  • Condition 1: "Both integers are smaller than $10$." Since $x + 2 > x$, it is sufficient to bound the larger integer:

$$ x + 2 < 10 \implies x < 8 $$

  1. Condition 2: "Their sum is more than $11$."

$$ x + (x + 2) > 11 $$

  • Step 3: Solve the compound system. From Condition 2:

$$ 2x + 2 > 11 $$

$$ 2x > 9 \implies x > 4.5 $$

Intersecting with Condition 1:

$$ 4.5 < x < 8 $$

  • Step 4: Filter by the Replacement Set ($x$ is an odd positive integer). The odd positive integers strictly between $4.5$ and $8$ are:

$$ x \in \{5, 7\} $$

  • Step 5: Construct the ordered pairs $(x, x + 2)$.
  • For $x = 5$: the pair is $(5, 5 + 2) = (5, 7)$. Check: $5 < 10$, $7 < 10$, and $5 + 7 = 12 > 11$. (Valid)

  • For $x = 7$: the pair is $(7, 7 + 2) = (7, 9)$. Check: $7 < 10$, $9 < 10$, and $7 + 9 = 16 > 11$. (Valid)

  • Conclusion: The required pairs of consecutive odd positive integers are $(5, 7)$ and $(7, 9)$.

Problem 3.4: The Multi-Cut Board Problem (Geometric Applications) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem: A carpenter wants to cut three lengths from a single piece of board of length $91\text{ cm}$. The second length is to be $3\text{ cm}$ longer than the shortest, and the third length is to be twice as long as the shortest. What are the possible lengths of the shortest board if the third piece is to be at least $5\text{ cm}$ longer than the second?

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Set up expressions in terms of a single variable. Let the length of the shortest piece be $x\text{ cm}$.
  • Shortest piece $= x$
  • Second piece $= x + 3$

  • Third piece $= 2x$ Physical constraint: $x > 0$.

  • Step 2: Formulate Inequality I (Conservation of Material). The sum of the three cut pieces cannot exceed the total board length ($91\text{ cm}$):

$$ x + (x + 3) + 2x \le 91 $$

$$ 4x + 3 \le 91 $$

$$ 4x \le 88 \implies x \le 22 $$

  • Step 3: Formulate Inequality II (Relative Length Constraint). "The third piece is to be at least $5\text{ cm}$ longer than the second":

$$ \text{Length of third piece} \ge \text{Length of second piece} + 5 $$

$$ 2x \ge (x + 3) + 5 $$

$$ 2x \ge x + 8 $$

$$ x \ge 8 $$

  • Step 4: Take the intersection.

$$ 8 \le x \le 22 $$

  • Conclusion: The shortest board length must be at least $8\text{ cm}$ and at most $22\text{ cm}$.

$$ \text{Interval Form: } x \in [8, 22]\text{ cm} $$

Trap 1: Variable Division Without Established Sign

  • The Error: A student solves an inequality like $\frac{200}{x} \le 5$ by cross-multiplying to write $200 \le 5x$.
  • Why it Fails: In abstract algebra, if $x < 0$, multiplying by $x$ reverses the inequality symbol.
  • Examiner Mandate: You may only cross-multiply a variable if the problem explicitly defines its domain as strictly positive (e.g., $x > 0$ for volume, length, or physical quantities). If $x$ can be negative, you must shift all terms to one side:

$$ \frac{200}{x} - 5 \le 0 \implies \frac{200 - 5x}{x} \le 0 $$

and solve via sign analysis (wavy curve method).

Trap 2: Omitting Physical Domain Realities

  • The Error: An inequality for volume or cut length produces $x \le 22$. The student writes the solution as $(-\infty, 22]$.
  • Why it Fails: A physical wooden board cannot have a negative or zero length ($x > 0$).
  • Examiner Deduction: Up to $1$ full mark is deducted for failing to restrict the lower bound to the physical domain:

$$ \text{Correct: } x \in (0, 22] $$

Trap 3: Strict vs. Slack Misclassification

  • The Error: Translating "minimum requirement of $80$" as $x > 80$.
  • Why it Fails: A minimum of $80$ means $80$ is permissible: $x \ge 80$.
  • Vocabulary Matrix:
  • Strict ($<, >$): "exceeds", "strictly between", "is shorter than", "less than".
  • Slack ($\le, \ge$): "not exceeding", "at least", "at most", "is not less than".

Trap 4: Sign Neglect During Negative Scalar Division

  • The Error: Solving $-4x \ge -28 \implies x \ge 7$.
  • Why it Fails: Division by $-4$ requires inverting the inequality operator:

$$ -4x \ge -28 \implies x \le \frac{-28}{-4} \implies x \le 7 $$

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Competitive Exams (JEE / CUET)

Golden Point 5.1: The Lever Rule (Alligation Formula) for Mixture Problems

For mixture problems where volume $V_1$ at concentration $c_1$ is blended with volume $V_2$ at concentration $c_2$ to achieve a target concentration $C$:

$$ \frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{|c_2 - C|}{|C - c_1|} $$

To find bounds for $V_2 = x$ when $C_{min} < C < C_{max}$: 1. Compute the lower critical volume $x_1$ by setting $C = C_{min}$:

$$ x_1 = V_1 \cdot \frac{C_{min} - c_1}{c_2 - C_{min}} $$

  1. Compute the upper critical volume $x_2$ by setting $C = C_{max}$:

$$ x_2 = V_1 \cdot \frac{C_{max} - c_1}{c_2 - C_{max}} $$

  1. The solution is immediately:

$$ x \in (x_1, x_2) $$

Verification with Problem 3.2 ($V_1 = 600$, $c_1 = 12\%$, $c_2 = 30\%$, $C \in (15\%, 18\%)$):

$$ x_1 = 600 \cdot \frac{15 - 12}{30 - 15} = 600 \cdot \frac{3}{15} = 120\text{ litres} $$

$$ x_2 = 600 \cdot \frac{18 - 12}{30 - 18} = 600 \cdot \frac{6}{12} = 300\text{ litres} $$

$$ \implies x \in (120, 300) \quad \text{(Solved in under 15 seconds!)} $$

Golden Point 5.2: The "Deficit/Surplus" Method for Target Averages

Instead of computing large products, track scores relative to the target:

$$ \text{Deviation} = \text{Score} - \text{Target} $$

For the mean to be at least $T$ across $n$ tests:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} (\text{Score}_i - T) + (x - T) \ge 0 \implies x \ge T - \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} (\text{Score}_i - T) $$

Verification with Problem 3.1 ($T = 75$, scores: $73, 67, 72$): - Deviations:

$$ 73 - 75 = -2 $$

$$ 67 - 75 = -8 $$

$$ 72 - 75 = -3 $$

  • Total Deficit $= (-2) + (-8) + (-3) = -13$
  • To offset this deficit in the 4th test:

$$ x - 75 \ge 13 \implies x \ge 75 + 13 = 88 $$

No scratch multiplication required; calculated entirely with mental arithmetic.

Golden Point 5.3: Symmetry & Dual Constraints Shortcut

When solving problems with consecutive odd/even integers: - Let the integers be symmetric about their average whenever possible. For two consecutive odd integers, using $y - 1$ and $y + 1$ (where $y$ is an even integer) yields:

$$ \text{Sum} = (y - 1) + (y + 1) = 2y > 11 \implies y > 5.5 $$

Upper bound: $y + 1 < 10 \implies y < 9$. Since $y$ must be an even integer, $y \in \{6, 8\}$. - For $y = 6 \implies (5, 7)$ - For $y = 8 \implies (7, 9)$ This eliminates algebraic carries and reduces arithmetic errors under timed conditions.

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Fundamental Definitions & Taxonomy (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

A statement involving variables and the order relation symbols $<, \le, >, \ge$ is an inequality.

Let $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a$ and $b$ are not simultaneously zero ($a^2 + b^2 \neq 0$). A Linear Inequality in Two Variables ($x$ and $y$) can take one of the following four canonical forms:

  1. Strict Inequalities:

$$ ax + by < c $$

$$ ax + by > c $$

  1. Slack / Non-Strict Inequalities:

$$ ax + by \le c $$

$$ ax + by \ge c $$

Key NCERT Definitions:

  • Solution of an Inequality: An ordered pair $(\alpha, \beta) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is said to be a solution of the inequality $ax + by < c$ if the substitution $x = \alpha$ and $y = \beta$ makes the inequality a true arithmetic statement (i.e., $a\alpha + b\beta < c$).
  • Solution Region / Feasible Region: The collection/set of all points $(x, y)$ in the Cartesian coordinate plane that satisfy the given linear inequality (or system of linear inequalities).

1.2 Geometric Axiomatics: Division of the Cartesian Plane (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Consider the straight line $L$ whose algebraic equation is:

$$ ax + by = c \quad (a^2 + b^2 \neq 0) $$

Every such line $L$ in the Euclidean plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ partitions the entire coordinate plane into three pairwise disjoint subsets: 1. The line $L$ itself:

$$ L = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid ax + by = c\} $$

  1. An open half-plane on one side of the line:

$$ H_1 = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid ax + by < c\} $$

  1. An open half-plane on the opposite side of the line:

$$ H_2 = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid ax + by > c\} $$

$$ \mathbb{R}^2 = H_1 \cup L \cup H_2 \quad \text{where } H_1 \cap L = \emptyset, \; H_2 \cap L = \emptyset, \; H_1 \cap H_2 = \emptyset $$

Terminology of Half-Planes:

  • Open Half-Plane: A region containing all points strictly on one side of the line, excluding the boundary line $L$ itself. This represents strict inequalities ($<$ or $>$).
  • Closed Half-Plane: A region containing the open half-plane together with the points on the boundary line $L$. This represents slack inequalities ($\le$ or $\ge$). Specifically:

$$ \overline{H}_1 = H_1 \cup L = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid ax + by \le c\} $$

  • Non-Vertical vs. Vertical Lines:
  • If $b \neq 0$, the line is non-vertical and divides the plane into an Upper Half-Plane and a Lower Half-Plane.
  • If $b = 0$ (and $a \neq 0$), the line $x = \frac{c}{a}$ is vertical and divides the plane into a Left Half-Plane and a Right Half-Plane.

1.3 Pedagogical Drawing Conventions (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

  1. Boundary Line Style:
  2. Strict Inequality ($<$ or $>$): Drawn as a broken / dashed line ($-\ - - - -$). This graphically communicates that points on $ax + by = c$ are strictly excluded from the solution set.
  3. Slack Inequality ($\le$ or $\ge$): Drawn as a continuous / solid line (——). This graphically communicates that points on $ax + by = c$ are included in the solution set.
  4. Shading:
  5. The valid half-plane must be shaded cleanly with parallel hatching or uniform cross-hatching.
  6. For systems of multiple inequalities, the simultaneous intersection is the region where hatchings of all inequalities overlap.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: Half-Plane Characterization Theorem (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:
Let $L: ax + by = c$ with $b > 0$ be a non-vertical line in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
1. Any point $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies in the upper half-plane of $L$ if and only if:

$$ ax_1 + by_1 > c $$

  1. Any point $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies in the lower half-plane of $L$ if and only if:

$$ ax_1 + by_1 < c $$

Proof:

Given:
A straight line $L: ax + by = c$ with $b > 0$, and an arbitrary point $P(x_1, y_1)$ in the Cartesian plane not lying on $L$.

Construction:
From $P(x_1, y_1)$, draw a line parallel to the $y$-axis (a vertical line with equation $x = x_1$).
Let this vertical line intersect the line $L$ at point $Q(x_1, y_L)$.

        y ^                 / L: ax + by = c
          |                /
          |       P(x_1, y_1)  [Upper Half-Plane: y_1 > y_L]
          |              |
          |              * Q(x_1, y_L)  [On the line]
          |              |
          |       R(x_1, y_2)  [Lower Half-Plane: y_2 < y_L]
          |              |
          +--------------+------------> x
          O             x_1

Step 1: Determine the ordinate of $Q$
Since $Q(x_1, y_L)$ lies on the line $L$, its coordinates must satisfy the line equation:

$$ ax_1 + b y_L = c $$

Solving explicitly for $y_L$ (since $b \neq 0$):

$$ y_L = \frac{c - ax_1}{b} $$

Step 2: Analysis for the Upper Half-Plane
By geometric definition, a point $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies in the upper half-plane relative to $L$ if and only if it lies vertically above the point $Q$ on the line. That is:

$$ y_1 > y_L $$

Substitute the expression for $y_L$:

$$ y_1 > \frac{c - ax_1}{b} $$

Multiply both sides by $b$. Since $b > 0$, the inequality direction is preserved:

$$ b y_1 > c - ax_1 $$

Add $ax_1$ to both sides:

$$ ax_1 + b y_1 > c $$

Step 3: Analysis for the Lower Half-Plane
Similarly, a point $R(x_1, y_2)$ lies in the lower half-plane relative to $L$ if and only if it lies vertically below $Q$. That is:

$$ y_2 < y_L $$

Substitute the expression for $y_L$:

$$ y_2 < \frac{c - ax_1}{b} $$

Multiply both sides by $b > 0$:

$$ b y_2 < c - ax_1 \implies ax_1 + b y_2 < c $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Validity of the Test-Point Method (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:
If the boundary line $ax + by = c$ does not pass through the origin $(0, 0)$, then the sign of $a(0) + b(0) - c = -c$ determines the entire half-plane containing the origin.
That is, the expression $f(x, y) = ax + by - c$ maintains a constant sign (strictly positive or strictly negative) over any connected open half-plane.

Proof:

Define the affine function:

$$ f(x, y) = ax + by - c $$

The function $f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$ is a continuous polynomial function of two variables.
Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there exist two points $A(x_A, y_A)$ and $B(x_B, y_B)$ in the same open half-plane $H$ such that:

$$ f(A) < 0 \quad \text{and} \quad f(B) > 0 $$

Consider the line segment connecting $A$ and $B$, parameterized by $t \in [0, 1]$:

$$ \mathbf{r}(t) = (1 - t)A + tB = \Big((1 - t)x_A + tx_B, \; (1 - t)y_A + ty_B\Big) $$

Define the single-variable restriction:

$$ g(t) = f(\mathbf{r}(t)) = a\big[(1 - t)x_A + tx_B\big] + b\big[(1 - t)y_A + ty_B\big] - c $$

$$ g(t) = (1 - t)f(A) + t f(B) $$

Observe that $g(t)$ is continuous on $[0, 1]$ with:

$$ g(0) = f(A) < 0 $$

$$ g(1) = f(B) > 0 $$

By the Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT), there must exist some $t^* \in (0, 1)$ such that:

$$ g(t^*) = 0 \implies f(\mathbf{r}(t^*)) = 0 $$

This implies that the point $\mathbf{r}(t^*)$ lies on the boundary line $L = \{(x, y) \mid ax + by - c = 0\}$.
However, by construction, $A$ and $B$ belong to the open half-plane $H$. Since half-planes are convex sets, the entire open line segment connecting $A$ and $B$ is contained within $H$:

$$ \mathbf{r}(t) \in H \quad \forall t \in [0, 1] $$

Because $H \cap L = \emptyset$, no point on the segment can lie on $L$. This contradicts the existence of $t^*$.

Thus, $f(x, y)$ cannot change sign without crossing $L$. Testing a single arbitrary point $(\alpha, \beta) \notin L$ conclusively establishes the algebraic sign of $f(x, y)$ for the entire half-plane containing $(\alpha, \beta)$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

$$ 2x + y < 0 $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Boundary Line Equation

$$ 2x + y = 0 \implies y = -2x $$

  • Step 2: Table of Values & Drawing Boundary
    The line passes through the origin $(0, 0)$. Find an additional reference point:
  • If $x = 1$, then $y = -2 \implies (1, -2)$.

  • If $x = -1$, then $y = 2 \implies (-1, 2)$.

    Because $(0, 0)$ lies on the line, $(0, 0)$ cannot be used as a test point.

Solve the following system of linear inequalities graphically:

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Analysis of Constraints (4): Non-negativity Constraints
    $x \ge 0$ restricts the region to the right of the $y$-axis (including the $y$-axis).
    $y \ge 0$ restricts the region above the $x$-axis (including the $x$-axis).
    Together, $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ restrict the feasible region entirely to the First Quadrant.

  • Analysis of Constraint (1): $2x + y \ge 4$

  • Boundary line $L_1$: $2x + y = 4$.
    Points: $(2, 0)$ and $(0, 4)$. Drawn as a solid line.
  • Test with $(0, 0)$:

$$ 2(0) + 0 \ge 4 \implies 0 \ge 4 \quad (\text{FALSE}) $$

Region: Closed half-plane **not** containing $(0, 0)$ (points above/to the right of $L_1$).
  • Analysis of Constraint (2): $x + y \le 3$
  • Boundary line $L_2$: $x + y = 3$.
    Points: $(3, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$. Drawn as a solid line.
  • Test with $(0, 0)$:

$$ 0 + 0 \le 3 \implies 0 \le 3 \quad (\text{TRUE}) $$

  • Analysis of Constraint (3): $2x - 3y \le 6$
  • Boundary line $L_3$: $2x - 3y = 6$.
    Points: $(3, 0)$ and $(0, -2)$. Drawn as a solid line.
  • Test with $(0, 0)$:

$$ 2(0) - 3(0) \le 6 \implies 0 \le 6 \quad (\text{TRUE}) $$

$$ 2(0) + y = 4 \implies y = 4 \implies (0, 4) $$

$$ (2x + y) - (x + y) = 4 - 3 \implies x = 1 $$

Problem 4: System with an Empty Solution Set (Inconsistent System) (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Show graphically that the following system has no solution:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + 2y &\le 3 \\ 3x + 4y &\ge 12 \\ x \ge 0, &\quad y \ge 1 \end{aligned} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • For $x + 2y \le 3$: Boundary line: passes through $(3, 0)$ and $(0, 1.5)$. Testing $(0, 0)$ yields $0 \le 3$ (True). The half-plane lies below this line.
  • For $3x + 4y \ge 12$: Boundary line: passes through $(4, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$. Testing $(0, 0)$ yields $0 \ge 12$ (False). The half-plane lies above this line.
  • Let us examine the vertical height ($y$-value) for any $x \ge 0$:
  • From inequality (1): $y \le \frac{3 - x}{2}$.
  • From inequality (2): $y \ge \frac{12 - 3x}{4}$. For both to hold simultaneously:

$$ \frac{12 - 3x}{4} \le y \le \frac{3 - x}{2} \implies \frac{12 - 3x}{4} \le \frac{6 - 2x}{4} \implies 12 - 3x \le 6 - 2x \implies x \ge 6 $$

However, check inequality $y \ge 1$: If $x \ge 6$, then $y \le \frac{3 - 6}{2} = -1.5$.
This requires $y \le -1.5$, which directly contradicts $y \ge 1$. * Conclusion:
The shaded regions of the respective half-planes do not overlap at any point in the Cartesian coordinate plane.

$$ \text{Feasible Region } = \emptyset \quad (\text{Null Set}) $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Mistakes

Trap / Pitfall Specific Mathematical Error Correct Board Exam Method
1. Origin Trap Testing $(0, 0)$ when the boundary line is $y = mx$ or $ax + by = 0$. Since $a(0) + b(0) = 0$, the inequality reduces to $0 < 0$ or $0 \le 0$, providing no distinguishing information. Never use $(0, 0)$ if the line passes through $(0, 0)$. Always pick an off-axis point like $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, or $(1, 1)$.
2. Boundary Line Style Drawing a solid line for strict inequalities ($<$ or $>$) or a dashed line for slack inequalities ($\le$ or $\ge$). Strict ($<, >$) $\to$ Dashed line ($--$). Slack ($\le, \ge$) $\to$ Solid line (——). Explicitly state: "Points on the dashed line are not included."
3. Negative Coefficient Sign Reversal Incorrectly rearranging $-2y \ge 6 - x$ into $y \ge \frac{6 - x}{-2}$. Forgetting to flip the inequality sign when dividing by a negative number. Dividing or multiplying by a negative scalar reverses the inequality: $-2y \ge 6 - x \implies y \le \frac{x - 6}{2}$.
4. Omitting Non-Negativity Boundaries Forgetting to mark that $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ restricts the solution to the First Quadrant, resulting in students shading into Quadrants II, III, and IV. Treat $x = 0$ ($y$-axis) and $y = 0$ ($x$-axis) as explicit boundary lines. State: "Restricted to the first quadrant."
5. Unbounded Region Fallacy Assuming an unbounded region has no solution. An unbounded region is a valid solution set for inequalities. Clearly extend shading to the edges of the grid with directional arrows.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

Golden Rule 1: The $y$-Coefficient "Vertical Elevation" Trick

If an inequality is solved for $y$ with a positive coefficient of $y$: * $y > mx + c$ or $y \ge mx + c$: The solution region is ALWAYS ABOVE the line. * $y < mx + c$ or $y \le mx + c$: The solution region is ALWAYS BELOW the line.

Example: $3x - 2y \le 6 \implies -2y \le 6 - 3x \implies y \ge \frac{3}{2}x - 3$.
Since $y \ge$, shade ABOVE the line. This bypasses the test-point calculation entirely.

Golden Rule 2: Intercept Form Inspection

Convert $ax + by = c$ to double-intercept form:

$$ \frac{x}{c/a} + \frac{y}{c/b} = 1 $$

  • Mark $x$-intercept $(c/a, 0)$ and $y$-intercept $(0, c/b)$ immediately.
  • Connect the dots. If $c > 0$ and the inequality is $ax + by \le c$, then $(0, 0)$ yields $0 \le c$ (True), which means you immediately shade the half-plane containing the origin.

Golden Rule 3: Vertical & Horizontal Line Invariants

  • $x \le k$: Vertical line at $k$; shade completely to the LEFT.
  • $x \ge k$: Vertical line at $k$; shade completely to the RIGHT.
  • $y \le k$: Horizontal line at $k$; shade completely BELOW.
  • $y \ge k$: Horizontal line at $k$; shade completely ABOVE.

Golden Rule 4: Corner Point Quick Check

To confirm whether your final shaded polygon is correct: Pick any point strictly interior to your shaded polygon and substitute it into every inequality in the system. If it satisfies every single inequality, your solution region is confirmed correct.

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Definition and Fundamentals of Linear Inequalities in Two Variables ⭐⭐⭐⭐

A linear inequality in two variables $x$ and $y$ involves an algebraic expression of the first degree in both variables. Formally, for real numbers $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ where $a$ and $b$ are not both zero ($a^2 + b^2 \neq 0$), a linear inequality takes one of the following four canonical forms:

  1. Strict Inequalities:

$$ ax + by < c $$

$$ ax + by > c $$

  1. Slack (or Non-Strict) Inequalities:

$$ ax + by \le c $$

$$ ax + by \ge c $$

Geometric Interpretation of a Line in the Cartesian Plane

The corresponding equation:

$$ ax + by = c $$

represents a straight line $L$ in the Cartesian plane $\mathbb{R}^2$.

According to Euclid's axioms and the topology of the Euclidean plane, any straight line $L: ax + by = c$ partitions the entire plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ into three mutually disjoint subsets: 1. The line $L$ itself:

$$ \mathcal{L} = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid ax + by = c\} $$

  1. The open half-plane $\mathcal{H}_1$:

$$ \mathcal{H}_1 = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid ax + by < c\} $$

  1. The open half-plane $\mathcal{H}_2$:

$$ \mathcal{H}_2 = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid ax + by > c\} $$

Thus:

$$ \mathbb{R}^2 = \mathcal{H}_1 \cup \mathcal{L} \cup \mathcal{H}_2 \quad \text{where } \mathcal{H}_1 \cap \mathcal{L} = \emptyset, \; \mathcal{H}_2 \cap \mathcal{L} = \emptyset, \; \mathcal{H}_1 \cap \mathcal{H}_2 = \emptyset $$

  • Open Half-Plane: An open half-plane is the set of all points satisfying a strict inequality ($ax + by < c$ or $ax + by > c$). The boundary line $L$ does not belong to the solution set. It is graphed using a dashed/broken line ($\text{-- -- --}$).
  • Closed Half-Plane: A closed half-plane is the set of all points satisfying a slack inequality ($ax + by \le c$ or $ax + by \ge c$). The boundary line $L$ belongs to the solution set. It is graphed using a solid, unbroken line (—).

1.2 System of Linear Inequalities & Common Feasible Region ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definition: System of Linear Inequalities

A collection of two or more linear inequalities involving the same variables $(x, y)$ is termed a system of linear inequalities in two variables:

$$ \begin{cases} a_1 x + b_1 y \ (\le, <, \ge, >) \ c_1 \\ a_2 x + b_2 y \ (\le, <, \ge, >) \ c_2 \\ \quad \vdots \\ a_m x + b_m y \ (\le, <, \ge, >) \ c_m \end{cases} $$

Definition: Solution of a System

An ordered pair $(\alpha, \beta) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is said to be a solution of the system if and only if $(\alpha, \beta)$ simultaneously satisfies every individual inequality in the system.

Definition: Feasible / Common Solution Region

Let $S_i \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ denote the solution set (half-plane) of the $i$-th inequality for $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, m\}$. The common region (or solution region / feasible region) of the system, denoted by $\mathcal{R}$, is the intersection of all individual solution sets:

$$ \mathcal{R} = \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} S_i = S_1 \cap S_2 \cap \dots \cap S_m $$

Classification of the Solution Region:

  1. Bounded Region: A region $\mathcal{R}$ is bounded if it can be enclosed within a circle of finite radius $R > 0$; that is, $\exists M > 0$ such that $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \le M$ for all $(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$.
  2. Unbounded Region: A region $\mathcal{R}$ is unbounded if it extends indefinitely in at least one direction (no finite circle can contain it).
  3. Inconsistent / Empty Region ($\mathcal{R} = \emptyset$): If the individual half-planes share no common points, the system has no solution.

1.3 Non-Negativity Constraints ($x \ge 0, y \ge 0$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In applied contexts (Linear Programming, resource allocation, real-world constraints): * $x \ge 0$ restricts the solution set to the right of (and including) the $y$-axis (Right Half-Plane). * $y \ge 0$ restricts the solution set above (and including) the $x$-axis (Upper Half-Plane).

The simultaneous imposition:

$$ \begin{cases} x \ge 0 \\ y \ge 0 \end{cases} $$

restricts the feasible region strictly to the First Quadrant (including the positive axes and the origin).

Theorem 1: Half-Plane Division Theorem ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: A line $ax + by = c$ ($b \neq 0$) divides the Cartesian plane into two half-planes such that: 1. If $b > 0$, the open half-plane $ax + by > c$ lies geometrically above the line, and $ax + by < c$ lies geometrically below the line. 2. If $b < 0$, the open half-plane $ax + by > c$ lies geometrically below the line, and $ax + by < c$ lies geometrically above the line.

Formal Proof:

Given: A non-vertical straight line $L: ax + by = c$ with $b \neq 0$, and an arbitrary point $P(x_1, y_1) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ not lying on $L$.

To Prove: 1. If $b > 0$: $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies above $L \iff ax_1 + by_1 > c$, and $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies below $L \iff ax_1 + by_1 < c$. 2. If $b < 0$: $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies above $L \iff ax_1 + by_1 < c$, and $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies below $L \iff ax_1 + by_1 > c$.

Construction: Draw a vertical line passing through $P(x_1, y_1)$ parallel to the $y$-axis. This line has the equation $x = x_1$. Since $L$ is non-vertical ($b \neq 0$), the vertical line $x = x_1$ intersects $L$ at a unique point $Q(x_1, y_Q)$.

Step-by-Step Algebraic Proof:

  1. Since $Q(x_1, y_Q)$ lies on $L: ax + by = c$, its coordinates satisfy:

$$ a x_1 + b y_Q = c $$

Solving for $y_Q$:

$$ y_Q = \frac{c - a x_1}{b} $$

  1. By definition of vertical orientation in $\mathbb{R}^2$:
  2. Point $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies strictly above line $L$ if and only if:

$$ y_1 > y_Q $$

  • Point $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies strictly below line $L$ if and only if:

$$ y_1 < y_Q $$

  1. Substitute the expression for $y_Q$ into the inequality $y_1 > y_Q$:

$$ y_1 > \frac{c - a x_1}{b} $$

$$ y_1 - \frac{c - a x_1}{b} > 0 $$

$$ \frac{b y_1 - (c - a x_1)}{b} > 0 $$

$$ \frac{a x_1 + b y_1 - c}{b} > 0 $$

  1. Now evaluate the sign based on the sign of $b$:
  2. Case I: $b > 0$ Multiplying both sides by $b$ (since $b > 0$, inequality direction is preserved):

$$ a x_1 + b y_1 - c > 0 \implies a x_1 + b y_1 > c $$

 Hence, $P$ lies above $L \iff a x_1 + b y_1 > c$.
 By logical negation, $P$ lies below $L \iff a x_1 + b y_1 < c$.
  • Case II: $b < 0$ Multiplying both sides by $b$ (since $b < 0$, inequality direction reverses):

$$ a x_1 + b y_1 - c < 0 \implies a x_1 + b y_1 < c $$

 Hence, $P$ lies above $L \iff a x_1 + b y_1 < c$.
 Conversely, $P$ lies below $L \iff a x_1 + b y_1 > c$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Convexity of the Solution Region of a Linear Inequality System ⭐⭐⭐

Statement: The solution set of any linear inequality in $\mathbb{R}^2$, as well as the intersection of any collection of such solution sets, is a convex set.

Definition (Convex Set): A set $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ is convex if for every pair of points $A, B \in \mathcal{C}$, the line segment connecting them is entirely contained within $\mathcal{C}$:

$$ \forall \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{C} \text{ and } \forall \lambda \in [0, 1], \quad \lambda \mathbf{u} + (1 - \lambda)\mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{C} $$

Formal Proof:

Part A: A single half-plane is convex.

Given: Let $\mathcal{H} = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid ax + by \le c\}$. Let $\mathbf{u} = (x_1, y_1) \in \mathcal{H}$ and $\mathbf{v} = (x_2, y_2) \in \mathcal{H}$.

To Prove: For any $\lambda \in [0, 1]$, the point $\mathbf{w} = \lambda \mathbf{u} + (1 - \lambda)\mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{H}$.

Proof Steps: 1. Coordinates of $\mathbf{w}$ are given by:

$$ \mathbf{w} = (\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda)x_2, \; \lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda)y_2) $$

  1. Evaluate the linear expression at $\mathbf{w}$:

$$ E(\mathbf{w}) = a(\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda)x_2) + b(\lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda)y_2) $$

  1. Group terms by parameter $\lambda$:

$$ E(\mathbf{w}) = \lambda(a x_1 + b y_1) + (1 - \lambda)(a x_2 + b y_2) $$

  1. Since $\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{H}$, $a x_1 + b y_1 \le c$. Since $\mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{H}$, $a x_2 + b y_2 \le c$.

  2. Since $\lambda \ge 0$ and $(1 - \lambda) \ge 0$:

$$ \lambda(a x_1 + b y_1) \le \lambda c $$

$$ (1 - \lambda)(a x_2 + b y_2) \le (1 - \lambda)c $$

  1. Adding both inequalities:

$$ E(\mathbf{w}) \le \lambda c + (1 - \lambda)c = c(\lambda + 1 - \lambda) = c $$

  1. Thus, $E(\mathbf{w}) \le c$, which establishes that $\mathbf{w} \in \mathcal{H}$. Therefore, $\mathcal{H}$ is a convex set.

Part B: The intersection of convex sets is convex.

Given: Let $\mathcal{R} = \bigcap_{i=1}^m S_i$, where each $S_i$ is a half-plane (and hence convex by Part A).

  1. Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathcal{R}$.
  2. By definition of set intersection:

$$ \mathbf{u} \in S_i \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{v} \in S_i \quad \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, m\} $$

  1. Since each $S_i$ is convex:

$$ \lambda \mathbf{u} + (1 - \lambda)\mathbf{v} \in S_i \quad \forall \lambda \in [0, 1], \; \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, m\} $$

  1. Since the convex combination belongs to every $S_i$, it belongs to their intersection:

$$ \lambda \mathbf{u} + (1 - \lambda)\mathbf{v} \in \bigcap_{i=1}^m S_i = \mathcal{R} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

⚠️ Trap 1: Multiplying Across by an Unknown Variable

The Error:
Given $\frac{1}{x} < 2$, students often write:

$$ 1 < 2x \implies x > \frac{1}{2} \quad \text{[INCORRECT]} $$

Why It Fails:
The sign of $x$ is unknown. - If $x > 0$, multiplying by $x$ preserves the inequality: $1 < 2x$. - If $x < 0$, multiplying by $x$ reverses the inequality: $1 > 2x$.
For instance, test $x = -1$: $\frac{1}{-1} = -1 < 2$, which is true. But the "solution" $x > \frac{1}{2}$ omits $x = -1$ entirely.

Correct Method:
Move all terms to one side and combine over a common denominator:

$$ \frac{1}{x} - 2 < 0 \implies \frac{1 - 2x}{x} < 0 $$

Now analyze using sign intervals (Wavy Curve / Critical Point Method): - Critical points: $x = \frac{1}{2}$ and $x = 0$. - Testing intervals shows the true solution: $x \in (-\infty, 0) \cup \left(\frac{1}{2}, \infty\right)$.

⚠️ Trap 2: Neglecting Sign Inversion During Step-Skipping

The Error:

$$ -3x > 12 \implies x > -4 \quad \text{[FATAL ERROR]} $$

The Correction:
Whenever dividing or multiplying by a negative coefficient, flip the relation immediately:

$$ -3x > 12 \implies x < \frac{12}{-3} \implies x < -4 $$

⚠️ Trap 3: Confusing Round $( )$ and Square $[ ]$ Brackets

The Error:
Writing the solution of $2x - 4 \ge 0$ as $(2, \infty)$ or $[2, \infty]$.

The Rule:
- Strict ($<, >$) $\implies$ Round brackets $( )$. - Slack ($\le, \ge$) $\implies$ Square brackets $[ ]$ at finite boundary values. - $\pm\infty$ always takes round brackets: $[2, \infty)$.

⚠️ Trap 4: Squaring Both Sides of an Inequality

The Error:

$$ -3 < 2 \implies (-3)^2 < 2^2 \implies 9 < 4 \quad \text{[MATHEMATICALLY FALSE]} $$

The Rule:
Both sides can be squared without reversing the inequality if and only if both sides are known to be non-negative:

$$ 0 \le a < b \implies a^2 < b^2 $$

If either side can be negative, squaring cannot be applied directly without splitting into cases.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

🚀 Golden Point 1: The Multiplication-by-Minus-One Reflection Principle

Multiplying by $-1$ reflects points across the origin on the real number line:

$$ a < b \iff -a > -b $$

   -b        -a         0         a         b
<---|---------|---------|---------|---------|--->

Since the point further to the right ($b$) is further from $0$ in the positive direction, its reflection ($-b$) lies further to the left in the negative direction. Hence, $-b < -a$.

🚀 Golden Point 2: Compound Inequality Split Hack

Any compound inequality $f(x) < g(x) < h(x)$ splits into an intersection ($\cap$) of two simultaneous conditions:

$$ f(x) < g(x) < h(x) \iff \Big(f(x) < g(x)\Big) \;\cap\; \Big(g(x) < h(x)\Big) $$

  • Solve each branch separately: $S_1$ and $S_2$.
  • The final solution set is the overlap:

$$ S_{\text{final}} = S_1 \cap S_2 $$

🚀 Golden Point 3: The Reciprocal Rule Shortcut

For quantities of the same sign:

$$ x > a > 0 \implies 0 < \frac{1}{x} < \frac{1}{a} $$

$$ x < a < 0 \implies \frac{1}{a} < \frac{1}{x} < 0 $$

For quantities of opposite signs:

$$ a < 0 < b \implies \frac{1}{a} < 0 < \frac{1}{b} \quad \text{(No inversion between } a \text{ and } b \text{)} $$

🚀 Golden Point 4: Cross-Multiplication Safety Check

You may cross-multiply terms across an inequality if and only if you are certain of the sign of the denominator:

$$ \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} > 0 $$

Instead of multiplying by $g(x)$ (which could be negative), multiply both sides by $[g(x)]^2$, which is strictly positive for all $g(x) \neq 0$:

$$ [g(x)]^2 \cdot \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} > 0 \cdot [g(x)]^2 \implies f(x) \cdot g(x) > 0 \quad (\text{provided } g(x) \neq 0) $$

This transforms rational inequalities into polynomial products while preserving the inequality direction.

1. The Variable-Positive Priority Rule

To minimize sign inversion errors, always transpose variable terms such that the coefficient of $x$ remains strictly positive:

$$ \text{Given: } 3 - 5x \le 13 $$

Benefit: Completely avoids division by negative numbers.

2. Double Inequality Decomposition Shortcut

$$ -3 \le 2x + 1 \le 7 $$

  1. Subtract $1$ from all three parts:

$$ -4 \le 2x \le 6 $$

  1. Divide all three parts by $2$:

$$ -2 \le x \le 3 \implies x \in [-2, 3] $$

Caution: If dividing by a negative coefficient, invert both inequality symbols at once:

$$ -6 < -3x \le 12 \implies \frac{-6}{-3} > x \ge \frac{12}{-3} \implies 2 > x \ge -4 \iff -4 \le x < 2 $$

3. Infinity Notation Anchor

$\infty$ and $-\infty$ are operational symbols representing unbounded growth, never real numbers. Therefore: - Never write a closed square bracket next to an infinity symbol:

$$ [a, \infty] \quad \text{[MATHEMATICALLY INVALID]} $$

$$ [a, \infty) \quad \text{[CORRECT]} $$

4. Conjunction vs. Disjunction Logic

  • "AND" ($\land$) $\implies$ Intersection ($\cap$): Simultaneous conditions must satisfy all constraints at once. Graphically, take the overlapping region of all segments.
  • "OR" ($\lor$) $\implies$ Union ($\cup$): Alternative conditions. Graphically, combine all shaded regions into one comprehensive set.

$$ \begin{aligned} x - y &< 1 \quad &\text{--- (1)} \\ x + 2y &> 4 \quad &\text{--- (2)} \end{aligned} $$

Solution:

  1. Line $L_1: x - y = 1$
  2. Points: $(1, 0)$ and $(0, -1)$.
  3. Strict inequality ($<$) $\implies$ Draw as a dashed line.
  4. Test $(0, 0)$: $0 - 0 = 0 < 1$ [TRUE].

  5. Shading goes towards the origin.

  6. Line $L_2: x + 2y = 4$

  7. Points: $(4, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$.
  8. Strict inequality ($>$) $\implies$ Draw as a dashed line.
  9. Test $(0, 0)$: $0 + 0 = 0 > 4$ [FALSE].

  10. Shading goes away from the origin.

  11. Point of Intersection:

$$ \begin{cases} x - y = 1 \\ x + 2y = 4 \end{cases} \implies 3y = 3 \implies y = 1, \quad x = 2 $$

Intersection point is $(2, 1)$.

Conclusion: The feasible region is the open infinite wedge bounded by the dashed rays of $L_1$ and $L_2$ starting from $(2, 1)$.

Important: The intersection point $(2, 1)$ does not belong to the solution set because neither dashed boundary is included in the strict inequalities.

5. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap / Error Type Incorrect Student Practice Correct Mathematical Practice Pedagogical Note
Origin Trap ($c = 0$) Testing $(0, 0)$ for lines like $y \ge 2x$, obtaining $0 \ge 0$ and getting confused. Pick any point off the line, such as $(1, 0)$ or $(0, 1)$. $(0, 0)$ lies on the line itself; it cannot distinguish between the two half-planes.
Boundary Line Style Drawing solid lines for strict inequalities ($<$ or $>$). Use dashed lines for $<$ or $>$; solid lines for $\le$ or $\ge$. In board exams, drawing a solid line for a strict inequality loses 0.5–1 mark.
Negative Coefficient Division Writing $-2x + y \le -4 \implies 2x - y \le 4$. Dividing/multiplying by $-1$ reverses the inequality: $2x - y \ge 4$. Sign reversal is the most frequent algebraic slip in Class 11.
Missing Non-Negativity Shading across into the second, third, or fourth quadrants when $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ are given. Restrict shading strictly to the first quadrant. Check the boundary constraints $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ before final shading.
Multiple Shading Smudge Shading every half-plane fully with dark pencil, creating a messy graph. Draw small directional arrows on the lines first; shade only the final common region. Improves clarity and prevents scoring errors during evaluation.

6. Speed Hacks, Mnemonics & Mental Math Shortcuts

Shortcut 1: The Sign of $b$ and Vertical Shading Direction

When an inequality is written in the form $ax + by \ (\le, \ge, <, >) \ c$: 1. Check the sign of the coefficient of $y$ ($b$): * If $b > 0$: * $ax + by > c \implies$ Shade ABOVE the line. * $ax + by < c \implies$ Shade BELOW the line. * If $b < 0$: * Multiply the entire inequality by $-1$, flip the inequality sign, and apply the rule above.

Example: $3x - 2y \le 6 \implies -2y \le -3x + 6 \implies y \ge \frac{3}{2}x - 3$. Since $y \ge \dots$, shade ABOVE the line. Takes 2 seconds mentally!

Shortcut 2: Intercept Form Inspection

To plot $ax + by = c$ in under 5 seconds:

$$ \frac{x}{c/a} + \frac{y}{c/b} = 1 $$

  • Mark $x$-intercept at $\left(\frac{c}{a}, 0\right)$
  • Mark $y$-intercept at $\left(0, \frac{c}{b}\right)$
  • Connect the two points.

Shortcut 3: The Directional Arrow Technique (Exam Strategy)

To keep graphs clean and prevent over-shading mistakes: 1. Plot the boundary line. 2. Mentally evaluate the test point. 3. Draw two tiny perpendicular arrows along the line pointing into the valid half-plane. 4. Repeat for all lines. 5. Identify the region where all arrows point inward simultaneously. 6. Shade only that final common region with neat parallel hatching lines.

Mnemonic: "S-T-A-R" for Inequality Systems

  • S — Solid or Dashed: Check $\le, \ge$ (Solid) vs $<, >$ (Dashed).
  • T — Test Point: Use $(0,0)$ if $c \ne 0$; use $(1,0)$ or $(0,1)$ if $c = 0$.
  • A — Arrows: Place directional arrows on individual lines; do not shade yet.
  • R — Region: Identify and shade the final common intersection $\mathcal{R} = \bigcap S_i$. Label all corner vertices clearly.

Question A.1: Case-Based / Passage-Integrated Question (Chemical Dilution & pH Regulation)

Tags: [CBSE Competency Pattern, 4 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Case Context:

In a biochemical manufacturing unit, an industrial chemist needs to maintain an acid buffer

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

solution within tight limits. The laboratory currently has $600\text{ L}$ of a $12\%$ acid solution. Regulations mandate that for the next synthesis phase, this solution must be diluted such that the resulting mixture contains more than $15\%$ but less than $18\%$ acid content.

The laboratory has an inventory of a $30\%$ acid solution available for blending. Let $x$ denote the volume of the $30\%$ acid solution (in litres) added to the existing $600\text{ L}$ solution.

+--------------------------+       +------------------------+
| Existing Solution        |  +    | Added Solution         |
| 600 L @ 12% Acid         |       | x L @ 30% Acid         |
+--------------------------+       +------------------------+
                     \                  /
                      \                /
                       v              v
            +------------------------------------+
            | Resulting Mixture                  |
            | Total Volume: (600 + x) L          |
            | Acid Constraint: 15% < Acid < 18%  |
            +------------------------------------+

Sub-Questions:

  1. Formulate the double inequality governing the volume $x$ of the $30\%$ solution to be added. (1 Mark)
  2. Find the minimum and maximum quantity (in litres) of the $30\%$ acid solution that can be added to strictly satisfy the regulatory specifications. (2 Marks)
  3. If the chemist accidentally adds $1000\text{ L}$ of the $30\%$ acid solution, determine whether the resulting mixture lies within, below, or above the required concentration range. (1 Mark)

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

Sub-Question 1: - Total volume of the combined mixture:

$$ (600 + x)\text{ L} $$

  • Total pure acid content present in the combined mixture:

$$ \text{Acid} = 12\% \text{ of } 600 + 30\% \text{ of } x = \frac{12}{100}(600) + \frac{30}{100}(x) = 72 + 0.3x $$

  • Given condition: The acid percentage must lie strictly between $15\%$ and $18\%$:

$$ 15\% \text{ of } (600 + x) < \text{Total Acid} < 18\% \text{ of } (600 + x) $$

$$ \frac{15}{100}(600 + x) < 72 + \frac{30}{100}x < \frac{18}{100}(600 + x) $$

$$ \mathbf{0.15(600 + x) < 72 + 0.30x < 0.18(600 + x)} $$

(CBSE Marking: [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for calculating total volume and acid content; [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for correctly setting up the double inequality)

Sub-Question 2: We solve the two simultaneous inequalities separately:

Part I: Lower Bound Inequality

$$ 0.15(600 + x) < 72 + 0.30x $$

$$ 90 + 0.15x < 72 + 0.30x $$

Transposing variable terms to the right-hand side and constant terms to the left-hand side:

$$ 90 - 72 < 0.30x - 0.15x $$

$$ 18 < 0.15x \implies x > \frac{18}{0.15} = \frac{1800}{15} = 120 $$

$$ \therefore x > 120\text{ L} $$

(CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for correctly solving the first inequality)

Part II: Upper Bound Inequality

$$ 72 + 0.30x < 0.18(600 + x) $$

$$ 72 + 0.30x < 108 + 0.18x $$

Transposing terms:

$$ 0.30x - 0.18x < 108 - 72 $$

$$ 0.12x < 36 \implies x < \frac{36}{0.12} = \frac{3600}{12} = 300 $$

$$ \therefore x < 300\text{ L} $$

(CBSE Marking: [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for correctly solving the second inequality)

Combined Interval Solution:

$$ 120 < x < 300 \quad \text{or} \quad x \in (120, 300) $$

Hence, more than $120\text{ L}$ and less than $300\text{ L}$ of the $30\%$ solution must be added. (CBSE Marking: [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for final interval interpretation)

Sub-Question 3: For $x = 1000\text{ L}$:

$$ \text{Total volume} = 600 + 1000 = 1600\text{ L} $$

$$ \text{Total pure acid} = 72 + 0.30(1000) = 72 + 300 = 372\text{ L} $$

$$ \text{Percentage concentration} = \left(\frac{372}{1600} \times 100\right)\% = \frac{372}{16}\% = 23.25\% $$

Since $23.25\% > 18\%$, the resulting mixture will be above the required concentration range (excessively acidic). (CBSE Marking: [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for calculation of concentration; [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for concluding it lies above the permitted range)

Case Context:

In thermal fatigue testing of aerospace alloys, temperatures are recorded in degrees Fahrenheit ($^\circ\text{F}$) using high-precision digital telemetry, while the structural physics formulations require temperature representations in degrees Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$) and Kelvin ($\text{K}$).

The relationship between the Fahrenheit scale ($F$) and Celsius scale ($C$) is given by:

$$ C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32) $$

The relationship between the Kelvin scale ($K$) and Celsius scale ($C$) is given by:

$$ K = C + 273.15 $$

   [ Kelvin Scale: K = C + 273.15 ]
                ^
                |
   [ Celsius Scale: C = 5/9(F - 32) ]
                ^
                |
   [ Fahrenheit Sensor Readings: F ]

Sub-Questions:

  1. An alloy specimen must be stored in an environmental chamber where the temperature in degrees Celsius is maintained strictly between $30^\circ\text{C}$ and $35^\circ\text{C}$. Set up and solve the inequality to find the permissible operating range of temperature in degrees Fahrenheit. (2 Marks)
  2. Convert the Fahrenheit temperature range $86^\circ\text{F} \le F \le 113^\circ\text{F}$ directly into an equivalent Kelvin interval. (2 Marks)
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

Sub-Question 1: - Given requirement:

$$ 30 < C < 35 $$

  • Substituting $C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32)$:

$$ 30 < \frac{5}{9}(F - 32) < 35 $$

(CBSE Marking: [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for substitution) - Multiplying the entire inequality by $9$ (since $9 > 0$, inequality signs remain unchanged):

$$ 270 < 5(F - 32) < 315 $$

  • Dividing by $5$ (since $5 > 0$):

$$ \frac{270}{5} < F - 32 < \frac{315}{5} $$

$$ 54 < F - 32 < 63 $$

(CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for intermediate simplification) - Adding $32$ across the inequality:

$$ 54 + 32 < F < 63 + 32 \implies \mathbf{86 < F < 95} $$

Thus, the temperature must be kept strictly between $86^\circ\text{F}$ and $95^\circ\text{F}$ ($F \in (86, 95)$). (CBSE Marking: [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for final range)

Sub-Question 2: - Given:

$$ 86 \le F \le 113 $$

  • Converting to Celsius first:

$$ 86 - 32 \le F - 32 \le 113 - 32 \implies 54 \le F - 32 \le 81 $$

$$ \frac{5}{9}(54) \le \frac{5}{9}(F - 32) \le \frac{5}{9}(81) $$

$$ 30 \le C \le 45 $$

(CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for finding the Celsius interval) - Converting Celsius to Kelvin using $K = C + 273.15$:

$$ 30 + 273.15 \le C + 273.15 \le 45 + 273.15 $$

$$ \mathbf{303.15 \le K \le 318.15} $$

Hence, the temperature on the Kelvin scale lies in the closed interval $[303.15\text{ K}, 318.15\text{ K}]$. (CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for converting to Kelvin with correct closed brackets)

Question A.3: Assertion-Reason Set (4 Independent Conceptual Problems)

Tags: [CBSE New Pattern, 1 Mark each] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Directions: In each of the following questions, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the correct option: - (a) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A). - (b) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A). - (c) (A) is true, but (R) is false. - (d) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Item 1:

  • Assertion (A): The
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

solution set of the linear inequality $-4x \ge 12$, where $x \in \mathbb{R}$, is $(-\infty, -3]$. - Reason (R): When both sides of an inequality are divided or multiplied by a negative real number, the direction of the inequality sign is reversed.

Answer: (a) Analysis & Marking: - Given: $-4x \ge 12$. Dividing both sides by $-4$ reverses the inequality:

$$ x \le \frac{12}{-4} \implies x \le -3 \implies x \in (-\infty, -3] $$

Thus, Assertion (A) is true. - Reason (R) states the foundational algebraic rule of inequalities: if $a \ge b$ and $c < 0$, then $\frac{a}{c} \le \frac{b}{c}$. Hence (R) is true and correctly explains (A). (CBSE Marking: Full [1 Mark] for option (a))

Item 2:

  • Assertion (A): For any real number $x$, the solution set of $|x - 3| < -2$ is $\phi$ (null set).
  • Reason (R): The absolute value of any real expression is non-negative, i.e., $|f(x)| \ge 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

Answer: (a) Analysis & Marking: - By definition of the modulus function, $|x - 3| \ge 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. - Since a non-negative quantity can never be strictly less than a negative number ($-2$), there exists no real value of $x$ satisfying the inequality. - Thus, solution set $= \phi$, making (A) true and (R) the direct, correct explanation. (CBSE Marking: Full [1 Mark] for option (a))

Item 3:

  • Assertion (A): The inequality $\frac{x - 2}{x + 3} > 0$ has the solution set $(-\infty, -3) \cup (2, \infty)$.
  • Reason (R): Multiplying both sides of the inequality $\frac{x - 2}{x + 3} > 0$ by $(x + 3)$ gives $x - 2 > 0$, hence $x > 2$.

Answer: (c) Analysis & Marking: - For $\frac{x - 2}{x + 3} > 0$, the quotient is positive if and only if both numerator and denominator share the same sign: - Case 1: $x - 2 > 0$ and $x + 3 > 0 \implies x > 2$ - Case 2: $x - 2 < 0$ and $x + 3 < 0 \implies x < -3$ Combining gives $x \in (-\infty, -3) \cup (2, \infty)$. Assertion (A) is true. - Reason (R) is mathematically fallacious because $(x + 3)$ can be negative when $x < -3$. One cannot cross-multiply by an expression containing a variable without knowing its sign; multiplication by $(x + 3)^2 > 0$ is required. Thus, Reason (R) is false. (CBSE Marking: Full [1 Mark] for option (c))

Item 4:

  • Assertion (A): If $x > y$ and $z < 0$, then $xz < yz$.
  • Reason (R): If $a < b$ and $c < d$, then $a - c < b - d$ for all real numbers $a, b, c, d$.

Answer: (c) Analysis & Marking: - For (A): By the fundamental order property of real numbers, multiplying an inequality by a negative number reverses the inequality sign. Since $x > y$ and $z < 0$, we have $xz < yz$. Thus, Assertion (A) is true. - For (R): Counterexample: Let $a = 2, b = 10 \implies 2 < 10$. Let $c = 1, d = 20 \implies 1 < 20$. Now evaluate: $a - c = 2 - 1 = 1$; and $b - d = 10 - 20 = -10$. Here, $1 < -10$ is false ($1 > -10$). Inequalities cannot be subtracted directly. Hence, Reason (R) is completely false. (CBSE Marking: Full [1 Mark] for option (c))

Question A.4: Higher Order Thinking Skills (HOTS) – Nested Modulus Inequality

Tags: [CBSE Competency / HOTS, 5 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem:

Solve the following double-modulus inequality analytically for $x \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$ \left| \frac{2x - 1}{x - 1} \right| > 2, \quad x \ne 1 $$

Represent the complete

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

solution set using interval notation and graph it on the real number line.

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

Step 1: Application of Modulus Inequality Theorem Recall the standard real property: For any $a > 0$,

$$ |u| > a \iff u < -a \quad \text{or} \quad u > a $$

Here, $u = \frac{2x - 1}{x - 1}$ and $a = 2$. Therefore:

$$ \frac{2x - 1}{x - 1} < -2 \quad \text{OR} \quad \frac{2x - 1}{x - 1} > 2 $$

(CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for splitting into two distinct non-modulus cases)

Step 2: Solving Branch I ($\frac{2x - 1}{x - 1} < -2$) Do not cross-multiply by $(x - 1)$. Transpose the constant to the left:

$$ \frac{2x - 1}{x - 1} + 2 < 0 $$

Finding a common denominator:

$$ \frac{(2x - 1) + 2(x - 1)}{x - 1} < 0 $$

$$ \frac{2x - 1 + 2x - 2}{x - 1} < 0 \implies \frac{4x - 3}{x - 1} < 0 $$

To find the sign change points, determine the critical numbers:

$$ \text{Numerator: } 4x - 3 = 0 \implies x = \frac{3}{4} $$

$$ \text{Denominator: } x - 1 = 0 \implies x = 1 $$

Testing intervals on the number line for $\frac{4x - 3}{x - 1}$: - For $x < \frac{3}{4}$ (e.g., $x = 0$): $\frac{-3}{-1} = 3 > 0$ - For $\frac{3}{4} < x < 1$ (e.g., $x = 0.8$): $\frac{3.2 - 3}{0.8 - 1} = \frac{0.2}{-0.2} = -1 < 0$ (Satisfied) - For $x > 1$ (e.g., $x = 2$): $\frac{8 - 3}{2 - 1} = 5 > 0$

Thus, the solution to Branch I is:

$$ x \in \left(\frac{3}{4}, 1\right) $$

(CBSE Marking: [1.5 Marks] for algebraic simplification and critical point testing)

Step 3: Solving Branch II ($\frac{2x - 1}{x - 1} > 2$) Transpose $2$ to the left:

$$ \frac{2x - 1}{x - 1} - 2 > 0 $$

$$ \frac{(2x - 1) - 2(x - 1)}{x - 1} > 0 $$

$$ \frac{2x - 1 - 2x + 2}{x - 1} > 0 \implies \frac{1}{x - 1} > 0 $$

Since the numerator is strictly positive ($1 > 0$), the fraction is positive if and only if the denominator is strictly positive:

$$ x - 1 > 0 \implies x > 1 $$

Thus, the solution to Branch II is:

$$ x \in (1, \infty) $$

(CBSE Marking: [1.5 Marks] for correct algebraic steps yielding $x > 1$)

Step 4: Union of Branches and Final Representation The total solution set is the union of Branch I and Branch II:

$$ S = \left(\frac{3}{4}, 1\right) \cup (1, \infty) $$

Number line:
---------o=========o---------o==================>
        3/4        1         1
         (         )         (

(Notice an open circle exists at $x = 1$ because the rational expression is undefined at $x = 1$.)

Final Answer:

$$ \mathbf{x \in \left(\frac{3}{4}, 1\right) \cup (1, \infty)} $$

(CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for correctly taking the union, excluding $x = 1$, and drawing/stating the interval)

Question A.5: HOTS – Multi-Variable Perimeter & Geometry Optimization

Tags: [CBSE Competency / HOTS, 4 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Problem:

The longest side of a triangle is three times the shortest side, and the third side is $2\text{ cm}$ shorter than the longest side. If the perimeter of the triangle is at least $61\text{ cm}$, find the minimum length of the shortest side. Furthermore, verify whether such a triangle satisfies the fundamental Triangle Inequality Theorem.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

Step 1: Variable Definition and Formulation - Let the length of the shortest side be $x\text{ cm}$, where $x > 0$. - Longest side $= 3x\text{ cm}$. - Third side $= (3x - 2)\text{ cm}$. (CBSE Marking: [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for correctly setting up side expressions)

Step 2: Perimeter Inequality Formulation - The perimeter $P$ of the triangle is:

$$ P = x + 3x + (3x - 2) = 7x - 2 $$

  • Given condition: $P \ge 61$:

$$ 7x - 2 \ge 61 $$

(CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for setting up the inequality)

Step 3: Algebraic Solution - Adding $2$ to both sides:

$$ 7x \ge 61 + 2 \implies 7x \ge 63 $$

  • Dividing both sides by $7$ (since $7 > 0$):

$$ x \ge \frac{63}{7} \implies x \ge 9 $$

Therefore, the minimum length of the shortest side is $9\text{ cm}$. (CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for solving to get $x \ge 9$)

Step 4: Verification of Triangle Inequality Theorem For $x = 9\text{ cm}$: - Shortest side $a = 9\text{ cm}$ - Longest side $b = 3(9) = 27\text{ cm}$ - Third side $c = 3(9) - 2 = 25\text{ cm}$

Checking whether the sum of any two sides is strictly greater than the third side: 1. $a + c = 9 + 25 = 34 > 27$ ($a + c > b$) $\checkmark$ 2. $a + b = 9 + 27 = 36 > 25$ ($a + b > c$) $\checkmark$ 3. $b + c = 27 + 25 = 52 > 9$ ($b + c > a$) $\checkmark$

The condition holds for all $x \ge 9$ because:

$$ \text{Sum of two smaller sides} = x + (3x - 2) = 4x - 2 $$

We require $4x - 2 > 3x \implies x > 2$, which is satisfied for all $x \ge 9$. (CBSE Marking: [1.5 Marks] for complete analytical verification of the Triangle Inequality)

$$ \frac{x - 1}{2x - 1} < 0 $$

Problem:

Solve the following inequality for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$ \frac{|x + 3| + x}{x + 2} > 1 $$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

Step 1: Domain Restriction and Definition of Modulus The denominator cannot be zero:

$$ x + 2 \ne 0 \implies x \ne -2 $$

Domain: $x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{-2\}$.

The modulus function $|x + 3|$ has its critical point at $x = -3$:

$$ |x + 3| = \begin{cases} x + 3, & \text{if } x \ge -3 \\ -(x + 3), & \text{if } x < -3 \end{cases} $$

We partition the real number line into two distinct cases based on this critical value. (CBSE Marking: [1 Mark] for domain identification and splitting modulus function)

Step 2: Case 1: When $x \ge -3$ (with $x \ne -2$) Here, $|x + 3| = x + 3$. The inequality becomes:

$$ \frac{(x + 3) + x}{x + 2} > 1 $$

$$ \frac{2x + 3}{x + 2} > 1 $$

Transpose $1$ to LHS:

$$ \frac{2x + 3}{x + 2} - 1 > 0 $$

$$ \frac{(2x + 3) - (x + 2)}{x + 2} > 0 $$

$$ \frac{x + 1}{x + 2} > 0 $$

Critical points: $x = -2$ and $x = -1$. Sign test on intervals: - If $x < -2$ (within this case: $-3 \le x < -2$): Let $x = -2.5 \implies \frac{-2.5 + 1}{-2.5 + 2} = \frac{-1.5}{-0.5} = +3 > 0$ (Satisfied) So, $[-3, -2)$ is part of the solution. - If $-2 < x < -1$: Let $x = -1.5 \implies \frac{-1.5 + 1}{-1.5 + 2} = \frac{-0.5}{0.5} = -1 < 0$ (Not satisfied) - If $x > -1$: Let $x = 0 \implies \frac{0 + 1}{0 + 2} = \frac{1}{2} > 0$ (Satisfied) So, $(-1, \infty)$ is part of the solution.

Intersecting with the Case 1 condition ($x \ge -3, x \ne -2$):

$$ \text{Solution for Case 1} = [-3, -2) \cup (-1, \infty) $$

(CBSE Marking: [2 Marks] for complete simplification and sign analysis of Case 1)

Step 3: Case 2: When $x < -3$ Here, $|x + 3| = -(x + 3) = -x - 3$. The inequality becomes:

$$ \frac{(-x - 3) + x}{x + 2} > 1 $$

$$ \frac{-3}{x + 2} > 1 $$

Transpose $1$ to LHS:

$$ \frac{-3}{x + 2} - 1 > 0 $$

$$ \frac{-3 - (x + 2)}{x + 2} > 0 $$

$$ \frac{-x - 5}{x + 2} > 0 $$

Multiply numerator by $-1$ and reverse the inequality sign:

$$ \frac{x + 5}{x + 2} < 0 $$

Critical points: $x = -5$ and $x = -2$. For the quotient $\frac{x + 5}{x + 2}$ to be strictly negative, $x$ must lie strictly between the roots:

$$ -5 < x < -2 $$

Now, intersect this with the Case 2 condition ($x < -3$):

$$ (-5, -2) \cap (-\infty, -3) = (-5, -3) $$

$$ \text{Solution for Case 2} = (-5, -3) $$

(CBSE Marking: [1.5 Marks] for solving Case 2 and taking the correct intersection)

Step 4: Union of Solutions from Both Cases The complete solution set is the union of solutions from Case 1 and Case 2:

$$ S = \text{Solution (Case 2)} \cup \text{Solution (Case 1)} $$

$$ S = (-5, -3) \cup [-3, -2) \cup (-1, \infty) $$

Notice that at $x = -3$: - In Case 2, the interval ends at $-3$ (open). - In Case 1, $x = -3$ is included ($[-3, -2)$). Testing $x = -3$ in the original inequality:

$$ \frac{|-3 + 3| + (-3)}{-3 + 2} = \frac{0 - 3}{-1} = 3 > 1 \quad \text{(True)} $$

Therefore, the two intervals $(-5, -3)$ and $[-3, -2)$ merge continuously into $(-5, -2)$.

Final Solution:

$$ \mathbf{x \in (-5, -2) \cup (-1, \infty)} $$

(CBSE Marking: [$\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] for correctly merging the boundary point $x = -3$ and stating the final solution set)

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 The "LCM Cross-Multiplication Bypass" Trick

In rational linear inequalities such as:

$$ \frac{ax + b}{c} < \frac{dx + e}{f} \quad (c, f \in \mathbb{R}^+) $$

  • Mental Shortcut: When denominators are purely positive constants, cross-multiply immediately without computing common denominators on both sides.
  • Algebraic Rule: If the denominator contains a variable, e.g., $\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \le k$:
  • Never cross-multiply $Q(x)$ across the inequality sign directly.
  • Shortcut: Transpose $k$ to the left: $\frac{P(x) - k Q(x)}{Q(x)} \le 0$, then multiply both sides by $[Q(x)]^2$ (which is strictly positive for $Q(x) \neq 0$):

$$ (P(x) - k Q(x)) \cdot Q(x) \le 0 \quad \text{with } Q(x) \neq 0 $$

  • This converts rational expressions into a single standard product of linear factors in one step.

1.2 The Generalized Wavy Curve (Sign Scheme) Rapid Method

For factored polynomial/rational inequalities of the form $\frac{(x - a_1)^{k_1} (x - a_2)^{k_2} \cdots}{(x - b_1)^{m_1} (x - b_2)^{m_2} \cdots} \gtrless 0$: 1. Coefficient Normalization: Ensure the coefficient of $x$ in every linear bracket is strictly $+1$. If any term is $(a - x)$, factor out $-1$ and flip the inequality sign. 2. Critical Points (Roots & Poles): Mark all roots (numerator) and poles (denominator) on the real number line in increasing order. 3. Multiplicity Rule (Bounce vs. Cross): * Start from the extreme right with a $+$ sign. * Moving right to left across a critical point $c$: * If the exponent of $(x - c)$ is odd, the sign alternates ($+ \to -$ or $- \to +$). * If the exponent of $(x - c)$ is even, the sign remains the same (no sign change). 4. Interval Read-off: Read positive intervals for $>0$ and negative intervals for $<0$. Automatically exclude all poles (denominator roots) using open round brackets ) or (.

1.3 Absolute Value (Modulus) Shortcut Templates

Avoid splitting into piecewise cases ($x \ge 0$ vs $x < 0$) when simple modulus forms appear: * Form 1: $|ax + b| \le c \iff -c \le ax + b \le c \iff \frac{-c - b}{a} \le x \le \frac{c - b}{a} \quad (c \ge 0, a > 0)$ * Form 2: $|ax + b| \ge c \iff ax + b \le -c \quad \text{or} \quad ax + b \ge c$ * Form 3 (Two Absolute Values): $|f(x)| \le |g(x)| \iff [f(x)]^2 \le [g(x)]^2 \iff (f(x) - g(x))(f(x) + g(x)) \le 0$ * Eliminates 4 piecewise cases into a single factorized quadratic inequality.

1.4 Test Point Shortcut for 2D Half-Planes (System of Linear Inequalities)

When graphing $ax + by + c \gtrless 0$: * If the boundary line does not pass through the origin $(0,0)$, evaluate $E(0,0) = a(0) + b(0) + c = c$. * If $c$ satisfies the inequality, shade the region containing $(0,0)$. * If $c$ violates it, shade the opposite half-plane. * If the line passes through $(0,0)$ ($c = 0$), pick $(1, 0)$ or $(0, 1)$ as the ultra-fast test point.

1.5 Replacement Set Restrictor (Discrete Sets)

When solving over $x \in \mathbb{W}, \mathbb{N},$ or $\mathbb{Z}$: 1. Solve the inequality completely over $\mathbb{R}$ first to obtain the continuous interval (e.g., $x < 4.6$). 2. Apply the discrete projection: * $x \in \mathbb{N} \implies \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ * $x \in \mathbb{W} \implies \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$ * $x \in \mathbb{Z}^- \implies \{-1, -2, -3, \dots\}$ * Examiner Hack: Never write discrete set solutions in interval notation like $[1, 4]$. Always use roster notation: $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

  1. Fundamental Axiom of Order Inversion: For $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $k \in \mathbb{R}$:
  2. If $a < b$ and $k > 0 \implies ka < kb$ and $\frac{a}{k} < \frac{b}{k}$.
  3. If $a < b$ and $k < 0 \implies ka > kb$ and $\frac{a}{k} > \frac{b}{k}$.

  4. If $a < b \implies -a > -b$.

  5. Reciprocal Property:

  6. If $a$ and $b$ have the same sign (i.e., $ab > 0$):

$$ a < b \iff \frac{1}{a} > \frac{1}{b} $$

  • If $a$ and $b$ have opposite signs (i.e., $a < 0 < b$):

$$ a < b \implies \frac{1}{a} < 0 < \frac{1}{b} \iff \frac{1}{a} < \frac{1}{b} \quad \text{(Order does NOT reverse!)} $$

  1. Double Inequalities Splitting Rule:

$$ A < B < C \iff (A < B) \;\mathbf{AND}\; (B < C) $$

The final solution is strictly the intersection of both solution sets:

$$ S = S_1 \cap S_2 $$

(Writing OR instead of AND results in immediate 0 for the logic step).

  1. Strict vs. Slack Inequalities:
  2. $ax + by < c$ or $ax + by > c$ represents an Open Half-Plane (boundary line excluded; graph using dashed lines).

  3. $ax + by \le c$ or $ax + by \ge c$ represents a Closed Half-Plane (boundary line included; graph using solid continuous lines).

  4. Absolute Value Triangular Inequalities (NCERT Highlights):

  5. $|a + b| \le |a| + |b|$ (Equality holds if and only if $ab \ge 0$).

  6. $|a - b| \ge ||a| - |b||$ (Equality holds if and only if $ab \ge 0$ and $|a| \ge |b|$).

  7. Mixture Problem Balance Rule: When adding $x$ litres of a $p_1\%$ solution to $V$ litres of a $p_2\%$ solution to obtain a mixture between $A\%$ and $B\%$:

$$ \frac{A}{100} (V + x) \le \frac{p_2}{100} V + \frac{p_1}{100} x \le \frac{B}{100} (V + x) $$

Always eliminate the factor of $100$ across all terms before expanding.

4.1 Governing Algebraic Rules for Inequalities

$$ \begin{array}{|l|l|l|} \hline Standard Form & Algebraic Condition & Solution Set x \in \mathbb{R} \\ \hline x + c \le d & c \in \mathbb{R} & x \in (-\infty, d - c] \\ \hline kx \le d \quad (k > 0) & k > 0 & x \in \left(-\infty, \frac{d}{k}\right] \\ \hline kx \le d \quad (k < 0) & k < 0 \text{ (Sign Inverts)} & x \in \left[\frac{d}{k}, \infty\right) \\ \hline |x| \le a & a > 0 & -a \le x \le a \implies x \in [-a, a] \\ \hline |x| < a & a \le 0 & x \in \emptyset \text{ (No solution)} \\ \hline |x| \ge a & a > 0 & x \le -a \text{ or } x \ge a \implies x \in (-\infty, -a] \cup [a, \infty) \\ \hline |x| > a & a \le 0 & x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\} \text{ (if } a=0 \text{ and strict)} \text{ or } \mathbb{R} \text{ (if } a < 0) \\ \hline a \le |x - c| \le b & 0 < a < b & x \in [c - b, c - a] \cup [c + a, c + b] \\ \hline \frac{x - a}{x - b} \le 0 & a < b & x \in [a, b) \quad \mathbf{(Pole } b \text{ strictly excluded)} \\ \hline \frac{x - a}{x - b} \ge 0 & a < b & x \in (-\infty, a] \cup (b, \infty) \\ \hline (x - a)(x - b) \le 0 & a \le b & x \in [a, b] \\ \hline (x - a)(x - b) \ge 0 & a \le b & x \in (-\infty, a] \cup [b, \infty) \\ \hline \end{array} $$

4.3 High-Value AM-GM Inequality Bounds (Applied Extremal Forms)

While formal AM-GM is treated in Sequences & Series, applied inequality questions frequently assess:

$$ \begin{array}{|l|l|l|} \hline Expression & Constraint & Standard Result / Bound \\ \hline x + \frac{1}{x} & x > 0 & x + \frac{1}{x} \ge 2 \quad (\text{Equality at } x = 1) \\ \hline x + \frac{1}{x} & x < 0 & x + \frac{1}{x} \le -2 \quad (\text{Equality at } x = -1) \\ \hline \frac{a + b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab} & a, b > 0 & \text{Equality holds if and only if } a = b \\ \hline a^2 + b^2 \ge 2ab & a, b \in \mathbb{R} & (a - b)^2 \ge 0 \quad (\text{Universal for all real numbers}) \\ \hline \end{array} $$
