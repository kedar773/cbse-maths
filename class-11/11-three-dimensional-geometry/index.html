1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Transition from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐)

In two-dimensional coordinate geometry ($\mathbb{R}^2$), the position of any point in a plane is specified by an ordered pair of real numbers $(x, y)$ relative to two mutually perpendicular directed lines (the $x$-axis and $y$-axis).

To pinpoint an object in real-world three-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^3$)—such as a ceiling fan hanging in a room or an aeroplane in flight—a third dimension (height, depth, or altitude) is indispensable. Hence, space is structured using an ordered triplet of real numbers:

$$ (x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3 = \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} $$

1.2 Coordinate Axes and Origin (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Formal Definition

Fix a point $O$ in space called the Origin. Through $O$, construct three mutually perpendicular directed straight lines, conventionally denoted as: 1. $X'OX$ (The $x$-axis) 2. $Y'OY$ (The $y$-axis) 3. $Z'OZ$ (The $z$-axis)

These three lines taken together are termed the rectangular coordinate axes (or simply coordinate axes).

Orientation & The Right-Handed Cartesian System

The standard Cartesian convention used in NCERT and advanced mathematics is the Right-Handed Coordinate System: - If the fingers of the right hand are curled from the positive direction of the $x$-axis ($OX$) towards the positive direction of the $y$-axis ($OY$) through an angle of $90^\circ$, the outstretched thumb points in the positive direction of the $z$-axis ($OZ$).

1.3 Coordinate Planes (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

The three coordinate axes taken in pairs determine three fundamental mutually perpendicular planes called the coordinate planes:

  1. $xy$-plane (Plane $XOY$): The flat surface spanned by the $x$-axis and $y$-axis.
  2. $yz$-plane (Plane $YOZ$): The flat surface spanned by the $y$-axis and $z$-axis.
  3. $zx$-plane (Plane $ZOX$): The flat surface spanned by the $z$-axis and $x$-axis.

Analytical Equations of the Coordinate Planes

  • Equation of the $xy$-plane: Any point lying on the $xy$-plane has zero vertical displacement along the $z$-axis.

$$ \Pi_{xy}: z = 0 $$

  • Equation of the $yz$-plane: Any point lying on the $yz$-plane has zero perpendicular displacement from the $yz$-plane along the $x$-axis.

$$ \Pi_{yz}: x = 0 $$

  • Equation of the $zx$-plane: Any point lying on the $zx$-plane has zero displacement along the $y$-axis.

$$ \Pi_{zx}: y = 0 $$

1.4 Coordinate Axes Equations & Parametric Forms (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

A coordinate axis is the line of intersection of two coordinate planes: 1. $x$-axis: Intersection of $xy$-plane ($z = 0$) and $zx$-plane ($y = 0$).

$$ \text{Equations of } x\text{-axis}: \quad y = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad z = 0 $$

$$ \text{General coordinates of a point on } x\text{-axis}: \quad (x, 0, 0), \quad x \in \mathbb{R} $$

  1. $y$-axis: Intersection of $xy$-plane ($z = 0$) and $yz$-plane ($x = 0$).

$$ \text{Equations of } y\text{-axis}: \quad x = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad z = 0 $$

$$ \text{General coordinates of a point on } y\text{-axis}: \quad (0, y, 0), \quad y \in \mathbb{R} $$

  1. $z$-axis: Intersection of $yz$-plane ($x = 0$) and $zx$-plane ($y = 0$).

$$ \text{Equations of } z\text{-axis}: \quad x = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad y = 0 $$

$$ \text{General coordinates of a point on } z\text{-axis}: \quad (0, 0, z), \quad z \in \mathbb{R} $$

1.5 Geometric Meaning of the Coordinates of a Point (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $P$ be any arbitrary point in space. Pass three planes through $P$ parallel to the coordinate planes: - Plane parallel to $yz$-plane intersecting the $x$-axis at $A(x, 0, 0)$. - Plane parallel to $zx$-plane intersecting the $y$-axis at $B(0, y, 0)$. - Plane parallel to $xy$-plane intersecting the $z$-axis at $C(0, 0, z)$.

These three planes, along with the three coordinate planes, form a rectangular parallelopiped (box) with the origin $O(0,0,0)$ and $P(x, y, z)$ as diagonally opposite vertices.

Interpretations of Coordinates $(x, y, z)$:

  • $x$-coordinate: Directed perpendicular distance of point $P$ from the $yz$-plane.
  • $y$-coordinate: Directed perpendicular distance of point $P$ from the $zx$-plane.
  • $z$-coordinate: Directed perpendicular distance of point $P$ from the $xy$-plane.

1.6 The Eight Octants (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Just as the two coordinate axes divide the $\mathbb{R}^2$ plane into $4$ quadrants, the three coordinate planes divide space ($\mathbb{R}^3$) into $8$ regions called Octants.

The signs of the coordinates of a point $P(x, y, z)$ determine the octant in which it lies.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivations & Proofs

Derivation 2.1: Perpendicular Distances of a Point from the Coordinate Planes (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem

Let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ be any point in space. The perpendicular distances from $P$ to the $xy$-plane, $yz$-plane, and $zx$-plane are $|z_1|$, $|x_1|$, and $|y_1|$ respectively.

Geometric Proof

Given:
A point $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ in Cartesian space relative to origin $O(0, 0, 0)$ and axes $X, Y, Z$.

To Prove:
1. $d(P, \Pi_{xy}) = |z_1|$ 2. $d(P, \Pi_{yz}) = |x_1|$ 3. $d(P, \Pi_{zx}) = |y_1|$

Construction:
Drop a perpendicular from $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ onto the $xy$-plane meeting the plane at foot $M$.

Step-by-step Derivation: 1. Since $M$ lies in the $xy$-plane, its $z$-coordinate is identically zero:

$$ z_M = 0 $$

  1. Because the segment $PM$ is perpendicular to the $xy$-plane, it is parallel to the $z$-axis. Hence, the projection of $PM$ onto both the $x$-axis and $y$-axis is zero, preserving the $x$ and $y$ coordinates of $P$:

$$ x_M = x_1, \quad y_M = y_1 $$

  1. Therefore, the coordinates of the foot of the perpendicular $M$ are:

$$ M = (x_1, y_1, 0) $$

  1. By the 3D Euclidean distance formula between $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $M(x_1, y_1, 0)$:

$$ d(P, M) = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_1)^2 + (y_1 - y_1)^2 + (z_1 - 0)^2} $$

$$ d(P, M) = \sqrt{0 + 0 + z_1^2} = \sqrt{z_1^2} = |z_1| $$

  1. Similarly, let $L$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ onto the $yz$-plane ($x = 0$):

$$ L = (0, y_1, z_1) $$

$$ d(P, \Pi_{yz}) = d(P, L) = \sqrt{(x_1 - 0)^2 + (y_1 - y_1)^2 + (z_1 - z_1)^2} = \sqrt{x_1^2} = |x_1| $$

  1. Let $N$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ onto the $zx$-plane ($y = 0$):

$$ N = (x_1, 0, z_1) $$

$$ d(P, \Pi_{zx}) = d(P, N) = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_1)^2 + (y_1 - 0)^2 + (z_1 - z_1)^2} = \sqrt{y_1^2} = |y_1| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2.2: Perpendicular Distances of a Point from the Coordinate Axes (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem

Let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ be a point in space. The shortest perpendicular distances of $P$ from the $x$-axis, $y$-axis, and $z$-axis are given by:

$$ d_x = \sqrt{y_1^2 + z_1^2}, \quad d_y = \sqrt{x_1^2 + z_1^2}, \quad d_z = \sqrt{x_1^2 + y_1^2} $$

Geometric Proof

Given:
Point $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$.

To Prove:
The perpendicular distance of $P$ from the $x$-axis is $\sqrt{y_1^2 + z_1^2}$.

Construction:
Drop a perpendicular from $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ onto the $x$-axis meeting the $x$-axis at point $A$.

Step-by-step Derivation: 1. Any point on the $x$-axis has $y = 0$ and $z = 0$. 2. The plane passing through $P$ perpendicular to the $x$-axis has the equation:

$$ x = x_1 $$

  1. The intersection of this perpendicular plane $x = x_1$ with the $x$-axis ($y = 0, z = 0$) gives the unique foot of the perpendicular:

$$ A = (x_1, 0, 0) $$

  1. The distance $PA$ is the perpendicular distance from $P$ to the $x$-axis:

$$ d(P, A) = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_1)^2 + (y_1 - 0)^2 + (z_1 - 0)^2} $$

$$ d(P, A) = \sqrt{0^2 + y_1^2 + z_1^2} = \sqrt{y_1^2 + z_1^2} $$

  1. Applying identical geometric steps for the $y$-axis and $z$-axis:
  2. Foot on $y$-axis: $B(0, y_1, 0) \implies d(P, B) = \sqrt{x_1^2 + z_1^2}$
  3. Foot on $z$-axis: $C(0, 0, z_1) \implies d(P, C) = \sqrt{x_1^2 + y_1^2}$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2.3: Distance of a Point from the Origin (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem

The Euclidean distance of any point $P(x, y, z)$ from the origin $O(0,0,0)$ is:

$$ OP = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

Proof via Double Pythagorean Theorem

           Z
           |        P(x,y,z)
           |       /|
           |      / |
           |     /  | z
           |    /   |
           O---/----+------- Y
            \ /    /
           x \    / y
              \  /
               M(x,y,0)
  1. Drop a perpendicular from $P(x, y, z)$ to the $xy$-plane at $M(x, y, 0)$.
  2. Thus, the line segment $PM$ is perpendicular to the $xy$-plane.
  3. Since $OM$ lies in the $xy$-plane, $PM \perp OM$. Therefore, $\triangle OMP$ is a right-angled triangle with the right angle at $M$.
  4. By the Pythagorean Theorem on $\triangle OMP$:

$$ OP^2 = OM^2 + PM^2 $$

  1. In the $xy$-plane, drop a perpendicular from $M(x, y, 0)$ to the $x$-axis at $A(x, 0, 0)$. Then $\triangle OAM$ is a right-angled triangle in the $xy$-plane with the right angle at $A$:

$$ OM^2 = OA^2 + AM^2 $$

Where $OA = |x|$ and $AM = |y|$.

$$ OM^2 = x^2 + y^2 $$

  1. Clearly, the vertical length $PM = |z| \implies PM^2 = z^2$.
  2. Substituting $OM^2$ into the primary equation:

$$ OP^2 = (x^2 + y^2) + z^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 $$

$$ OP = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

SECTION 1: IN-DEPTH PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & MATHEMATICAL INTUITION

1.1 Transition from 2D ($\mathbb{R}^2$) to 3D ($\mathbb{R}^3$) $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐]

In two-dimensional Cartesian geometry ($\mathbb{R}^2$), the position of any point is uniquely determined by an ordered pair $(x, y)$ relative to two mutually perpendicular directed lines called the $X$-axis and $Y$-axis. These axes partition the plane into four disjoint regions called quadrants.

However, physical reality and spatial objects cannot be constrained to a single plane. To locate an object suspended in space (e.g., an airplane, a ceiling fan, or a vertex of a crystal lattice), a third spatial dimension—height or depth—must be introduced.

In three-dimensional Cartesian geometry ($\mathbb{R}^3$): 1. Three mutually perpendicular lines intersecting at a common fixed point $O$ (the origin) form the Cartesian coordinate axes: - $X'OX$ ($X$-axis) - $Y'OY$ ($Y$-axis) - $Z'OZ$ ($Z$-axis) 2. By convention, the orientation follows the Right-Handed Cartesian Coordinate System: - If the fingers of the right hand curl from the positive direction of the $X$-axis toward the positive direction of the $Y$-axis through the smaller angle ($90^\circ$), the outstretched thumb points along the positive direction of the $Z$-axis.

1.2 Coordinate Planes and Spatial Partitioning $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

The three mutually perpendicular coordinate axes taken in pairs determine three fundamental mutually perpendicular planes known as the Coordinate Planes: 1. $XY$-Plane: The plane containing the $X$-axis and $Y$-axis. Every point on this plane has height zero:

$$ z = 0 $$

  1. $YZ$-Plane: The plane containing the $Y$-axis and $Z$-axis. Every point on this plane has zero displacement along the $x$-direction:

$$ x = 0 $$

  1. $ZX$-Plane (or $XZ$-Plane): The plane containing the $Z$-axis and $X$-axis. Every point on this plane has zero displacement along the $y$-direction:

$$ y = 0 $$

The Eight Octants

The three coordinate planes divide the entire infinite three-dimensional space into eight disjoint open regions called Octants.

An octant is designated by the signs of the coordinates of its points. The convention adopted by NCERT identifies the octants using Roman numerals $\text{I}$ through $\text{VIII}$, defined by the signs of $(x, y, z)$:

Octant Standard Notation Sign of $x$ Sign of $y$ Sign of $z$ Geometric Region
I $OXYZ$ $+$ $+$ $+$ All coordinates positive
II $OX'YZ$ $-$ $+$ $+$ Negative $x$, positive $y$ and $z$
III $OX'Y'Z$ $-$ $-$ $+$ Negative $x$ and $y$, positive $z$
IV $OXY'Z$ $+$ $-$ $+$ Positive $x$, negative $y$, positive $z$
V $OXYZ'$ $+$ $+$ $-$ Positive $x$ and $y$, negative $z$
VI $OX'YZ'$ $-$ $+$ $-$ Negative $x$, positive $y$, negative $z$
VII $OX'Y'Z'$ $-$ $-$ $-$ All coordinates negative
VIII $OXY'Z'$ $+$ $-$ $-$ Positive $x$, negative $y$ and $z$

Pedagogical Note on Signs: Notice that octants $\text{I}$ to $\text{IV}$ lie completely above the $XY$-plane ($z > 0$). Their $(x, y)$ signs replicate the standard 2D quadrants $\text{I}$, $\text{II}$, $\text{III}$, and $\text{IV}$. Octants $\text{V}$ to $\text{VIII}$ lie completely below the $XY$-plane ($z < 0$), with their $(x, y)$ signs repeating the identical sequence of quadrants $\text{I}$ through $\text{IV}$.

1.3 Coordinates of a Point in Space: The Cuboidal Framework $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Let $P$ be any arbitrary point in space. To define the coordinates of $P$, construct planes through $P$ parallel to the coordinate planes: 1. A plane parallel to the $YZ$-plane intersecting the $X$-axis at $A$. 2. A plane parallel to the $ZX$-plane intersecting the $Y$-axis at $B$. 3. A plane parallel to the $XY$-plane intersecting the $Z$-axis at $C$.

These three planes, together with the coordinate planes, form a rectangular parallelepiped (cuboid) having $O(0,0,0)$ and $P(x,y,z)$ as diagonally opposite vertices.

  • $OA = x$ (directed distance of $P$ from the $YZ$-plane)
  • $OB = y$ (directed distance of $P$ from the $ZX$-plane)
  • $OC = z$ (directed distance of $P$ from the $XY$-plane)

The ordered triplet $(x, y, z)$ is called the Cartesian coordinates of the point $P$.

1.4 Orthogonal Projections $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Definition of Orthogonal Projection

The orthogonal projection (or the foot of the perpendicular) of a point $P$ onto a given line (axis) or plane is the point of intersection of that line or plane with the perpendicular drawn from $P$ onto it.

For any point $P(x, y, z)$:

1. Projections onto Coordinate Planes:

  • Foot of perpendicular onto $XY$-plane: The line perpendicular to the $XY$-plane through $P(x, y, z)$ is parallel to the $Z$-axis. The point of intersection with $z = 0$ is:

$$ M_{xy} = (x, y, 0) $$

  • Foot of perpendicular onto $YZ$-plane: The line perpendicular to the $YZ$-plane through $P(x, y, z)$ is parallel to the $X$-axis. The point of intersection with $x = 0$ is:

$$ M_{yz} = (0, y, z) $$

  • Foot of perpendicular onto $ZX$-plane: The line perpendicular to the $ZX$-plane through $P(x, y, z)$ is parallel to the $Y$-axis. The point of intersection with $y = 0$ is:

$$ M_{zx} = (x, 0, z) $$

2. Projections onto Coordinate Axes:

  • Foot of perpendicular onto $X$-axis: The perpendicular from $P(x, y, z)$ meets the $X$-axis ($y = 0, z = 0$) at:

$$ L_x = (x, 0, 0) $$

  • Foot of perpendicular onto $Y$-axis: The perpendicular from $P(x, y, z)$ meets the $Y$-axis ($x = 0, z = 0$) at:

$$ L_y = (0, y, 0) $$

  • Foot of perpendicular onto $Z$-axis: The perpendicular from $P(x, y, z)$ meets the $Z$-axis ($x = 0, y = 0$) at:

$$ L_z = (0, 0, z) $$

THEOREM 1: Perpendicular Distance of a Point from Coordinate Planes $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Statement

Let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ be a point in three-dimensional space. The perpendicular distances of $P$ from the coordinate planes $XY$, $YZ$, and $ZX$ are $|z_1|$, $|x_1|$, and $|y_1|$ respectively.

Proof

1. Distance from the $XY$-plane

  • Given: A point $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and the $XY$-plane defined by the equation $z = 0$.
  • To Prove: Perpendicular distance $d(P, \text{XY-plane}) = |z_1|$.
  • Construction: Let $M$ be the foot of the perpendicular drawn from $P$ onto the $XY$-plane.

Algebraic Derivation: Since $PM$ is perpendicular to the $XY$-plane, $PM$ is parallel to the $Z$-axis. Therefore, the $x$- and $y$-coordinates of $M$ are identical to those of $P$, and the $z$-coordinate of $M$ is zero because $M$ lies on the $XY$-plane. Thus, the coordinates of $M$ are:

$$ M = (x_1, y_1, 0) $$

Using the Euclidean distance formula between $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $M(x_1, y_1, 0)$:

$$ PM = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_1)^2 + (y_1 - y_1)^2 + (z_1 - 0)^2} $$

$$ PM = \sqrt{0 + 0 + z_1^2} = \sqrt{z_1^2} = |z_1| $$

2. Distance from the $YZ$-plane

Let $N$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to the $YZ$-plane ($x = 0$).

$$ N = (0, y_1, z_1) $$

$$ PN = \sqrt{(x_1 - 0)^2 + (y_1 - y_1)^2 + (z_1 - z_1)^2} = \sqrt{x_1^2} = |x_1| $$

3. Distance from the $ZX$-plane

Let $K$ be the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ to the $ZX$-plane ($y = 0$).

$$ K = (x_1, 0, z_1) $$

$$ PK = \sqrt{(x_1 - x_1)^2 + (y_1 - 0)^2 + (z_1 - z_1)^2} = \sqrt{y_1^2} = |y_1| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

THEOREM 2: Perpendicular Distance of a Point from Coordinate Axes $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Statement

Let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ be a point in three-dimensional space. The perpendicular distances of $P$ from the $X$-axis, $Y$-axis, and $Z$-axis are:

$$ d_x = \sqrt{y_1^2 + z_1^2}, \quad d_y = \sqrt{x_1^2 + z_1^2}, \quad d_z = \sqrt{x_1^2 + y_1^2} $$

Proof

1. Perpendicular Distance from the $X$-axis

  • Given: Point $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and the $X$-axis ($y = 0, z = 0$).
  • To Prove: The perpendicular distance from $P$ to the $X$-axis is $\sqrt{y_1^2 + z_1^2}$.
  • Construction:
  • Drop a perpendicular from $P$ to the $XY$-plane meeting it at $M(x_1, y_1, 0)$.
  • From $M$, drop a perpendicular to the $X$-axis meeting it at $L(x_1, 0, 0)$.
  • Join $P$ to $L$.

Geometric & Algebraic Argument: 1. $PM$ is perpendicular to the $XY$-plane by construction. 2. Therefore, $PM$ is perpendicular to every line lying in the $XY$-plane passing through $M$. Since the line $ML$ lies entirely in the $XY$-plane, we have:

$$ PM \perp ML \implies \angle PML = 90^\circ $$

  1. By the Theorem of Three Perpendiculars (or simple vector orthogonality), since $ML \perp X\text{-axis}$ in the $XY$-plane and $PM \perp (XY\text{-plane})$, the line $PL$ is perpendicular to the $X$-axis:

$$ PL \perp X\text{-axis} $$

Thus, $L(x_1, 0, 0)$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ onto the $X$-axis, and $PL$ is the required perpendicular distance. 4. In right-angled triangle $\triangle PML$, by Pythagoras' Theorem:

$$ PL^2 = PM^2 + ML^2 $$

  1. From the coordinates:

$$ PM = |z_1| \implies PM^2 = z_1^2 $$

$$ ML = \text{distance between } (x_1, y_1, 0) \text{ and } (x_1, 0, 0) = |y_1| \implies ML^2 = y_1^2 $$

  1. Substituting these values:

$$ PL^2 = z_1^2 + y_1^2 \implies PL = \sqrt{y_1^2 + z_1^2} $$

2. Perpendicular Distance from the $Y$-axis

Let the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ onto the $Y$-axis be $Q(0, y_1, 0)$. The distance $d_y$ is:

$$ d_y = PQ = \sqrt{(x_1 - 0)^2 + (y_1 - y_1)^2 + (z_1 - 0)^2} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + z_1^2} $$

3. Perpendicular Distance from the $Z$-axis

Let the foot of the perpendicular from $P$ onto the $Z$-axis be $R(0, 0, z_1)$. The distance $d_z$ is:

$$ d_z = PR = \sqrt{(x_1 - 0)^2 + (y_1 - 0)^2 + (z_1 - z_1)^2} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + y_1^2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

THEOREM 3: The Distance Formula Between Two Points in $\mathbb{R}^3$ $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Statement

The distance $d$ between two points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ is given by:

$$ PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

Proof

  • Given: Two points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$.
  • To Prove: $PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}$.
  • Construction: Pass planes through $P$ and $Q$ parallel to the coordinate planes to construct a rectangular cuboid such that $PQ$ is its body diagonal.
  • Let the plane through $P$ parallel to the $XY$-plane and the line through $Q$ parallel to the $Z$-axis intersect at point $R$.
  • Let the orthogonal projection of $P$ onto the $XY$-plane be $P'(x_1, y_1, 0)$ and that of $Q$ be $Q'(x_2, y_2, 0)$.
  • Through $P$, draw a line parallel to the $X$-axis, and through $R$, draw a line parallel to the $Y$-axis, meeting at $S$.
  • Thus, $\triangle PSR$ lies in a plane parallel to the $XY$-plane, and $QR$ is perpendicular to this plane.

Step-by-Step Algebraic Derivation: 1. The line segment $QR$ is parallel to the $Z$-axis. Therefore, the $x$- and $y$-coordinates of $R$ are equal to those of $Q$, while its $z$-coordinate is equal to that of $P$:

$$ R = (x_2, y_2, z_1) $$

  1. The coordinates of vertex $S$ are:

$$ S = (x_2, y_1, z_1) $$

  1. In $\triangle PSR$, the angle $\angle PSR = 90^\circ$ because $PS \parallel X\text{-axis}$ and $SR \parallel Y\text{-axis}$. By the Pythagorean theorem:

$$ PR^2 = PS^2 + SR^2 $$

  1. Evaluating $PS$ and $SR$:

$$ PS = |x_2 - x_1| \implies PS^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 $$

$$ SR = |y_2 - y_1| \implies SR^2 = (y_2 - y_1)^2 $$

Therefore:

$$ PR^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 $$

  1. Since $QR$ is parallel to the $Z$-axis and the plane containing $\triangle PSR$ is parallel to the $XY$-plane:

$$ QR \perp PR \implies \angle PRQ = 90^\circ $$

  1. In right-angled triangle $\triangle PRQ$, by the Pythagorean theorem:

$$ PQ^2 = PR^2 + RQ^2 $$

  1. Evaluating $RQ$:

$$ RQ = |z_2 - z_1| \implies RQ^2 = (z_2 - z_1)^2 $$

  1. Substituting $PR^2$ and $RQ^2$ into the equation:

$$ PQ^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2 $$

  1. Taking the positive square root:

$$ PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Problem 4: Advanced Locus Problem $\quad$ [4 Marks]

Question: Find the equation of the set of points $P$ such that the sum of the squares of its distances from the coordinate planes is equal to its squared distance from the origin. What geometric entity does this represent?

Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Scheme:

Step 1: Define distances to coordinate planes [1.5 Marks] Let the coordinates of point $P$ be $(x, y, z)$. - Distance of $P$ from the $XY$-plane $= |z|$ $\implies$ Square of distance $= z^2$. - Distance of $P$ from the $YZ$-plane $= |x|$ $\implies$ Square of distance $= x^2$. - Distance of $P$ from the $ZX$-plane $= |y|$ $\implies$ Square of distance $= y^2$.

Sum of squares of distances from coordinate planes:

$$ S_1 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 $$

Step 2: Define distance to the origin [1 Mark] The distance of $P(x, y, z)$ from the origin $O(0, 0, 0)$ is:

$$ OP = \sqrt{(x - 0)^2 + (y - 0)^2 + (z - 0)^2} = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

Square of distance from the origin:

$$ S_2 = OP^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 $$

Step 3: Equating and Interpretation [1.5 Marks] According to the given condition:

$$ S_1 = S_2 \implies x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 $$

$$ 0 = 0 $$

Conclusion: This equation is an identity that holds true for every point $(x, y, z) \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Hence, the locus is the entire three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$.

SECTION 4: EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

1. Distance from an Axis vs. Distance from a Plane

  • The Error: Writing the distance of $P(x, y, z)$ from the $X$-axis as $|x|$ or $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$.
  • The Correction:
  • Distance from the $X$-axis depends on $y$ and $z$: $d_x = \sqrt{y^2 + z^2}$.
  • Distance from the $YZ$-plane depends only on $x$: $d_{yz} = |x|$. Mnemonic: The axis is a 1D line; you need both remaining dimensions to specify the distance to it. The plane is a 2D surface; only the one missing orthogonal coordinate determines the distance.

2. Sign Errors in Octant Assignment

  • The Error: Forgetting that octants $\text{V}$–$\text{VIII}$ correspond to negative $z$ while $(x, y)$ cycle through quadrants $\text{I}$–$\text{IV}$.
  • Example: A student marks $(-2, 3, -4)$ as Octant VI because $x$ is negative, but confuses whether it is II or VI.
  • Correction: $(-2, 3)$ is Quadrant II. Since $z = -4 < 0$, add 4 to the quadrant number: $\text{II} + 4 = \text{VI}$.

3. Foot of Perpendicular vs. Distance

  • The Error: When asked for the "projection on the $Y$-axis", providing a scalar length $|y|$ instead of the coordinates $(0, y, 0)$.
  • The Correction:
  • "Projection" or "Foot of perpendicular" is a point $\implies (0, y, 0)$.
  • "Length of projection" or "Perpendicular distance" is a scalar $\implies \sqrt{x^2 + z^2}$.

4. Forgetting the Absolute Value Sign

  • The Error: Writing the distance from $(2, -5, 3)$ to the $ZX$-plane as $-5$.
  • The Correction: Distance is strictly a non-negative geometric quantity:

$$ \text{Distance} = |-5| = 5 $$

PART 1: Pedagogical Foundations & Mathematical Intuition

1. The Transition from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}^3$

In two-dimensional Cartesian geometry ($\mathbb{R}^2$), the position of a point is uniquely fixed by an ordered pair of real numbers $(x, y)$ representing directed perpendicular distances from two mutually perpendicular axes ($Y$-axis and $X$-axis respectively).

In three-dimensional space ($\mathbb{R}^3$), a point is located using an ordered triplet of real numbers $(x, y, z)$. These numbers denote the directed perpendicular distances of the point from three mutually perpendicular coordinate planes: - $x$ is the directed distance from the $YZ$-plane. - $y$ is the directed distance from the $ZX$-plane. - $z$ is the directed distance from the $XY$-plane.

2. Geometric Intuition of Spatial Distance

The straight-line Euclidean distance between two points in space corresponds to the length of the space diagonal of a rectangular cuboid (or rectangular parallelepiped) whose faces are parallel to the principal coordinate planes. The calculation applies the Pythagorean Theorem across two orthogonal planes.

Proof 1: Distance Formula in 3D Space (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem Statement

Let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ be two distinct arbitrary points referred to a right-handed rectangular Cartesian coordinate system in $\mathbb{R}^3$. The Euclidean distance $d(P, Q) = PQ$ is given by:

$$ PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

Step-by-Step Geometric Derivation

1. Given:

Two points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ in three-dimensional space relative to origin $O(0, 0, 0)$ and axes $OX, OY, OZ$.

2. Construction:

  • Through the points $P$ and $Q$, construct planes parallel to the coordinate planes ($XY$, $YZ$, $ZX$) to form a rectangular parallelepiped.
  • The segment $PQ$ forms a space diagonal of this cuboid.
  • Let the faces through $P$ and $Q$ intersect such that:
  • $P$ has coordinates $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$.
  • $A$ is a vertex with coordinates $(x_2, y_1, z_1)$, so that edge $PA$ is parallel to the $X$-axis.
  • $N$ is a vertex on the same bottom face with coordinates $(x_2, y_2, z_1)$, so that edge $AN$ is parallel to the $Y$-axis.
  • Vertex $Q$ lies directly above $N$ with coordinates $(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, so that edge $NQ$ is parallel to the $Z$-axis.

3. Plane Analysis & Metric Evaluation:

The lengths of the three edges meeting along orthogonal directions are:

$$ PA = |x_2 - x_1| $$

$$ AN = |y_2 - y_1| $$

$$ NQ = |z_2 - z_1| $$

4. Applying the Pythagorean Theorem in Two Steps:

Step A: Consider $\triangle PAN$ in the horizontal plane $z = z_1$. Because the $X$-axis and $Y$-axis are mutually perpendicular, $PA \perp AN$. Therefore, $\triangle PAN$ is a right-angled triangle at vertex $A$.

By the Pythagorean theorem:

$$ PN^2 = PA^2 + AN^2 $$

Substituting the side lengths:

$$ PN^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)} $$

Step B: Consider $\triangle PNQ$. The edge $NQ$ is parallel to the $Z$-axis and is perpendicular to the plane containing $\triangle PAN$. Hence, $NQ$ is perpendicular to line segment $PN$, making $\triangle PNQ$ a right-angled triangle at vertex $N$.

By the Pythagorean theorem:

$$ PQ^2 = PN^2 + NQ^2 \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)} $$

5. Algebraic Substitution & Resolution:

Substitute Equation (1) into Equation (2):

$$ PQ^2 = \left[ (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 \right] + NQ^2 $$

Substitute $NQ = |z_2 - z_1|$:

$$ PQ^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2 $$

Because distance is a non-negative scalar metric ($PQ \ge 0$):

$$ PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Corollary: Distance of a Point from Origin (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement

The distance of point $P(x, y, z)$ from the origin $O(0, 0, 0)$ is:

$$ OP = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

Proof

Set $(x_1, y_1, z_1) = (0, 0, 0)$ and $(x_2, y_2, z_2) = (x, y, z)$ in the general formula:

$$ OP = \sqrt{(x - 0)^2 + (y - 0)^2 + (z - 0)^2} = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

PART 4: Collinearity of Three Points in $\mathbb{R}^3$

Three distinct spatial points $A, B,$ and $C$ are said to be collinear if and only if they lie on the same straight line. In Class 11 introductory 3D geometry, two methods establish collinearity without using direction cosines or vector products:

                  A                     B                   C
                  o---------------------o-------------------o
                  |<------- AB -------->|<------ BC ------->|
                  |<-------------------- AC --------------->|

Method 1: The Metric / Triangle Inequality Degeneracy Method (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theoretical Principle

For any three points in a metric space, the Triangle Inequality states:

$$ AB + BC \ge AC $$

Equality holds ($AB + BC = AC$) if and only if $B$ lies on the straight-line segment joining $A$ and $C$.

Algorithmic Execution:

  1. Compute the three pairwise distances using the distance formula:

$$ d_1 = AB, \quad d_2 = BC, \quad d_3 = CA $$

  1. Identify the maximum of the three distances, say $d_{\max} = \max(d_1, d_2, d_3)$.
  2. Check whether the sum of the remaining two smaller distances equals the maximum distance:

$$ d_{\text{small}_1} + d_{\text{small}_2} = d_{\max} $$

  1. If verified, the points are collinear, and the point shared by the two smaller segments lies strictly between the other two points.

Method 2: The Section Formula Equivalence Method (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theoretical Principle

Three points $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, and $C(x_3, y_3, z_3)$ are collinear if there exists a single unique real scalar $k \in \mathbb{R}$ ($k \ne -1$) such that point $C$ divides segment $AB$ in the ratio $k : 1$.

Algebraic Test:

  1. Equate the coordinates of $C$ to the section formula coordinates for ratio $k:1$:

$$ x_3 = \frac{k x_2 + x_1}{k + 1} \implies k_x = \frac{x_3 - x_1}{x_2 - x_3} $$

  1. Solve independently for $k$ using the $x$-coordinates ($k_x$), $y$-coordinates ($k_y$), and $z$-coordinates ($k_z$):

$$ k_y = \frac{y_3 - y_1}{y_2 - y_3}, \qquad k_z = \frac{z_3 - z_1}{z_2 - z_3} $$

  1. Condition for Collinearity:

$$ k_x = k_y = k_z = k $$

If all three ratios yield the exact same value of $k$, the three points are collinear. If $k > 0$, division is internal; if $k < 0$, division is external.

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Definitions

1.1 Division of a Line Segment by Coordinate Planes

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Extremely High Yield in CBSE Board Exams & Competitive Objective Tests)

Core Geometric Intuition

In a 3-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system, space is divided into eight octants by three mutually perpendicular coordinate planes intersecting at the origin $O(0, 0, 0)$: 1. $xy$-plane: Formed by the $x$ and $y$ axes. Every point on this plane has zero elevation along the $z$-axis. Hence, its equation is:

$$ z = 0 $$

  1. $yz$-plane: Formed by the $y$ and $z$ axes. Every point on this plane has zero displacement along the $x$-axis. Hence, its equation is:

$$ x = 0 $$

  1. $zx$-plane (or $xz$-plane): Formed by the $z$ and $x$ axes. Every point on this plane has zero displacement along the $y$-axis. Hence, its equation is:

$$ y = 0 $$

When a directed line segment joining two distinct points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ intersects any coordinate plane at a point $R$, $R$ lies simultaneously on the segment $PQ$ and on that coordinate plane.

To determine the ratio in which the plane divides the directed segment $PQ$: - Let the required ratio be $k : 1$ (where $k = \frac{m}{n}$, $n \neq 0$). - Using the Section Formula, express the coordinates of the point of division $R$ in terms of the scalar parameter $k$. - Equate the coordinate corresponding to the plane's defining equation to zero (e.g., equate the $z$-coordinate to $0$ for the $xy$-plane).

Nature of Division:

  • If $k > 0$, the division is internal; the segment strictly intersects the plane between $P$ and $Q$. Thus, $P$ and $Q$ lie on opposite sides of the coordinate plane.
  • If $k < 0$, the division is external; the plane intersects the extended line containing $P$ and $Q$. Thus, $P$ and $Q$ lie on the same side of the coordinate plane.
  • If $k = -1$, the segment is parallel to the coordinate plane ($x_1 = x_2$, $y_1 = y_2$, or $z_1 = z_2$), and no finite division exists (the line is either parallel or lies completely in the plane).

1.2 Centroid of a Triangle in 3D Space

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Foundational Concept for 3D Vectors & Analytical Geometry)

Definition

The centroid of a triangle is the unique point of concurrency of its three medians.

A median is a line segment joining any vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side.

Fundamental Properties:

  1. Centroid Division Property: The centroid divides each median internally in the ratio $2 : 1$, measuring from the vertex to the base midpoint.
  2. Mean Position Property: In 3-space, the centroid represents the arithmetic mean (centre of mass of three equal particles placed at the vertices) of the positional coordinates of the three vertices:

$$ G = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3}, \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3}, \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3}\right) $$

  1. Invariance: The centroid is independent of the choice of coordinate axes and orientation of the coordinate frame.

2. Rigorous Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: Ratio of Division of Segment $PQ$ by Coordinate Planes

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ be two distinct points in $\mathbb{R}^3$. 1. The $yz$-plane ($x = 0$) divides the segment $PQ$ in the ratio $-x_1 : x_2$. 2. The $zx$-plane ($y = 0$) divides the segment $PQ$ in the ratio $-y_1 : y_2$. 3. The $xy$-plane ($z = 0$) divides the segment $PQ$ in the ratio $-z_1 : z_2$.

Proof (Detailed for $xy$-plane):

  • Given: Two points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ such that $z_1 \neq z_2$.
  • To Prove: The $xy$-plane divides the line segment $PQ$ in the ratio $-\frac{z_1}{z_2} : 1$ (or $-z_1 : z_2$).
  • Derivation:
  • Let the point of intersection of the line segment $PQ$ with the $xy$-plane be $R(x, y, z)$.
  • Let $R$ divide $PQ$ in the ratio $k : 1$ ($k \neq -1$).
  • By the 3D Section Formula for internal division, the coordinates of $R$ are given by:

$$ R(x, y, z) = \left(\frac{k x_2 + x_1}{k + 1}, \frac{k y_2 + y_1}{k + 1}, \frac{k z_2 + z_1}{k + 1}\right) $$

  1. Since the point $R$ lies on the $xy$-plane, its $z$-coordinate must be identically zero:

$$ z = 0 $$

  1. Substituting the $z$-coordinate of $R$:

$$ \frac{k z_2 + z_1}{k + 1} = 0 $$

  1. Since $k \neq -1$, multiplying both sides by $(k + 1)$ gives:

$$ k z_2 + z_1 = 0 \implies k z_2 = -z_1 \implies k = -\frac{z_1}{z_2} $$

  1. Therefore, the ratio $k : 1$ is:

$$ k : 1 = -\frac{z_1}{z_2} : 1 \equiv -z_1 : z_2 $$

  • Deduction of Coordinates of Point of Intersection $R$: Substituting $k = -\frac{z_1}{z_2}$ into $x$ and $y$:

$$ x = \frac{\left(-\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)x_2 + x_1}{-\frac{z_1}{z_2} + 1} = \frac{\frac{-x_2 z_1 + x_1 z_2}{z_2}}{\frac{z_2 - z_1}{z_2}} = \frac{x_1 z_2 - x_2 z_1}{z_2 - z_1} $$

$$ y = \frac{\left(-\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)y_2 + y_1}{-\frac{z_1}{z_2} + 1} = \frac{y_1 z_2 - y_2 z_1}{z_2 - z_1} $$

Hence, the point of intersection on the $xy$-plane is:

$$ R = \left(\frac{x_1 z_2 - x_2 z_1}{z_2 - z_1}, \frac{y_1 z_2 - y_2 z_1}{z_2 - z_1}, 0\right) $$

(By symmetry, swapping indices and coordinates yields the proofs for $yz$-plane and $zx$-plane).
Q.E.D.

Statement

Problem 4: Advanced Application (Centroid of Derived Midpoint Triangle)

Statement: Let $D, E, F$ be the midpoints of sides $BC, CA, AB$ of $\triangle ABC$ with vertices $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, and $C(x_3, y_3, z_3)$. Prove that the centroid of $\triangle DEF$ coincides with the centroid of $\triangle ABC$.

Complete Step-by-Step Proof:

  • Step 1: Compute the coordinates of midpoints $D, E, F$:

$$ D = \left(\frac{x_2 + x_3}{2}, \frac{y_2 + y_3}{2}, \frac{z_2 + z_3}{2}\right) $$

$$ E = \left(\frac{x_3 + x_1}{2}, \frac{y_3 + y_1}{2}, \frac{z_3 + z_1}{2}\right) $$

$$ F = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}, \frac{z_1 + z_2}{2}\right) $$

Trap Number Frequent Student Error Mathematical Consequence Correct Methodology
Trap 1: The External Sign Error Writing the final answer as "The ratio is $-2 : 3$" or "Divides externally in ratio $-2 : 3$". Redundant negative sign creates a logical contradiction. Examiners deduct $0.5$ to $1$ mark. If $k = -\frac{2}{3}$, state: "The ratio is $2 : 3$ externally" OR "The ratio of division is $-2 : 3$". Never combine "externally" with a negative sign.

Calculates the ratio for the wrong plane ($yz$-plane instead of $xy$-plane), leading to $0$ marks for the calculation. Remember: For the $xy$-plane, the coordinate absent in the name is zero ($z = 0$). For $yz$-plane, $x = 0$. For $zx$-plane, $y = 0$.
Trap 3: Ratio Order Reversal Setting the ratio from $Q$ to $P$ instead of $P$ to $Q$. Results in the inverted ratio (e.g., $3 : 2$ instead of $2 : 3$). Always strictly track the directed segment: In segment $PQ$, $P$ corresponds to $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q$ to $(x_2, y_2, z_2)$. Ratio $k : 1$ attaches $k$ to $Q$'s coordinates.

Loss of $1.5$ to $2$ marks on a $3$ or $4$-mark question. Carefully re-read the prompt: "Also find the coordinates of the point of division". Plug $k$ back into all three coordinate formulas.

5.1 The "Negative Coordinate Ratio" Hack (Instant 5-Second MCQ Solver)

For the line segment joining $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$: - Divided by $yz$-plane ($x = 0$):

$$ \text{Ratio} = -\frac{x_1}{x_2} $$

  • Divided by $zx$-plane ($y = 0$):

$$ \text{Ratio} = -\frac{y_1}{y_2} $$

  • Divided by $xy$-plane ($z = 0$):

$$ \text{Ratio} = -\frac{z_1}{z_2} $$

Instant Inspection Rule:

  • If the two coordinates have opposite signs, the ratio $-\left(\frac{\text{coord}_1}{\text{coord}_2}\right)$ becomes positive $\implies$ Internal division.
  • If the two coordinates have the same sign, the ratio $-\left(\frac{\text{coord}_1}{\text{coord}_2}\right)$ becomes negative $\implies$ External division.

Mental Math Example:

5.2 General Plane Division Hack (Extension to General Linear Planes)

If a line segment joining $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ is divided by any general plane:

$$ \Pi: ax + by + cz + d = 0 $$

The ratio $k : 1$ in which the plane divides $PQ$ is given by:

$$ k = -\frac{ax_1 + by_1 + cz_1 + d}{ax_2 + by_2 + cz_2 + d} = -\frac{\Pi(P)}{\Pi(Q)} $$

(Notice that coordinate planes are simple special cases: for $z = 0$, $a=0, b=0, c=1, d=0 \implies k = -\frac{z_1}{z_2}$).

Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the equation of the locus of a point $P(x, y, z)$ which moves such that the sum of the squares of its distances from the coordinate planes is equal to its squared distance from the point $A(1, 1, 1)$.

Step-by-step Solution: 1. Identify Distances from Coordinate Planes: - Perpendicular distance of $P(x, y, z)$ from $xy$-plane $= |z|$ - Perpendicular distance of $P(x, y, z)$ from $yz$-plane $= |x|$ - Perpendicular distance of $P(x, y, z)$ from $zx$-plane $= |y|$ 2. Sum of Squares of Distances:

$$ S = |z|^2 + |x|^2 + |y|^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 $$

  1. Distance from $A(1, 1, 1)$ Squared:

$$ PA^2 = (x - 1)^2 + (y - 1)^2 + (z - 1)^2 $$

$$ PA^2 = (x^2 - 2x + 1) + (y^2 - 2y + 1) + (z^2 - 2z + 1) $$

$$ PA^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 2x - 2y - 2z + 3 $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 2x - 2y - 2z + 3 $$

  1. Cancel out $x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ from both sides:

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Trap 1: Confusing Distance with the Coordinate Value

  • The Mistake: Stating the perpendicular distance from $P(3, -4, -5)$ to the $xy$-plane is $-5$.
  • The Fact: Distance is a geometric scalar magnitude and is strictly non-negative.
  • Correction: The distance is $|z| = |-5| = 5\text{ units}$.

Trap 2: Incorrect Quadrant-to-Octant Mapping (Sign Errors)

  • The Mistake: Mixing up Octants II, IV, VI, and VIII.
  • Why Students Slip: Forgetting whether Octant IV is $(+, -, +)$ or $(-, +, +)$.
  • Master Rule: Always evaluate the $(x, y)$ pair first using standard 2D quadrants:
  • Quadrant I: $(+, +)$
  • Quadrant II: $(-, +)$
  • Quadrant III: $(-, -)$
  • Quadrant IV: $(+, -)$
  • If $z > 0$, the octant number is identically the quadrant number ($1, 2, 3, 4$).
  • If $z < 0$, add $4$ to the quadrant number ($5, 6, 7, 8$).

Trap 3: Swapping Coordinate Plane Distances

  • The Mistake: Writing distance from $yz$-plane as $\sqrt{y^2 + z^2}$.
  • The Fact:
  • Perpendicular distance to a plane depends on the single missing variable:

$$ \text{Dist to } yz\text{-plane} = |x| $$

  • Perpendicular distance to an axis depends on the sum of squares of the two other variables:

$$ \text{Dist to } x\text{-axis} = \sqrt{y^2 + z^2} $$

Trap 4: Boundary Points Do Not Belong to Any Octant

  • The Question: "In which octant does the point $(0, -3, 5)$ lie?"
  • The Trap: Student marks Octant II or Octant IV.
  • The Fact: If any coordinate is zero, the point does NOT lie in any octant. It lies on a coordinate plane or coordinate axis.
  • For $(0, -3, 5)$, $x = 0 \implies$ it lies strictly on the $yz$-plane.

💡 Example 1: Standard Distance Calculation
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the distance between the points $P(-2, 3, 5)$ and $Q(1, -2, 4)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Let $(x_1, y_1, z_1) = (-2, 3, 5)$ and $(x_2, y_2, z_2) = (1, -2, 4)$.

Apply the distance formula:

$$ PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

Substitute the coordinates:

$$ PQ = \sqrt{(1 - (-2))^2 + (-2 - 3)^2 + (4 - 5)^2} $$

Simplify each difference term:

$$ PQ = \sqrt{(3)^2 + (-5)^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 25 + 1} = \sqrt{35} \text{ units} $$

💡 Example 2: Verifying Collinearity via Metric Method
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Show that the points $A(1, 2, 3)$, $B(-1, -2, -1)$, and $C(2, 4, 5)$ are collinear.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Compute distance $AB$

$$ AB = \sqrt{(-1 - 1)^2 + (-2 - 2)^2 + (-1 - 3)^2} $$

$$ AB = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + (-4)^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 16 + 16} = \sqrt{36} = 6 $$

Step 2: Compute distance $BC$

$$ BC = \sqrt{(2 - (-1))^2 + (4 - (-2))^2 + (5 - (-1))^2} $$

$$ BC = \sqrt{(3)^2 + (6)^2 + (6)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 36 + 36} = \sqrt{81} = 9 $$

Step 3: Compute distance $AC$

$$ AC = \sqrt{(2 - 1)^2 + (4 - 2)^2 + (5 - 3)^2} $$

$$ AC = \sqrt{(1)^2 + (2)^2 + (2)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{9} = 3 $$

Step 4: Analyze segment sum Arrange lengths: $AB = 6$, $AC = 3$, $BC = 9$. Observe:

$$ AB + AC = 6 + 3 = 9 = BC $$

Since the sum of two segments equals the third ($AC + AB = BC$), point $A$ lies on the line segment joining $C$ and $B$. Therefore, points $A, B,$ and $C$ are collinear.

Scoring Breakdown: - Individual distances calculated correctly: [1.5 Marks] - Comparison relation $AC + AB = BC$: [1.0 Mark] - Final deduction: [0.5 Mark]

💡 Example 3: Finding an Unknown Coordinate under Collinearity Condition
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem (NCERT Exemplar): If points $A(3, 2, -4)$, $B(5, 4, -6)$, and $C(9, 8, -10)$ are collinear, find the ratio in which point $B$ divides segment $AC$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Let point $B(5, 4, -6)$ divide the join of $A(3, 2, -4)$ and $C(9, 8, -10)$ in the ratio $k : 1$.

By the section formula, coordinates of $B$ are:

$$ B = \left( \frac{k(9) + 1(3)}{k + 1}, \; \frac{k(8) + 1(2)}{k + 1}, \; \frac{k(-10) + 1(-4)}{k + 1} \right) $$

Equating the $x$-coordinates:

$$ \frac{9k + 3}{k + 1} = 5 \implies 9k + 3 = 5(k + 1) $$

$$ 9k + 3 = 5k + 5 \implies 4k = 2 \implies k = \frac{1}{2} $$

Verify consistency across the remaining coordinates: - $y$-coordinate check:

$$ \frac{8(1/2) + 2}{(1/2) + 1} = \frac{4 + 2}{3/2} = \frac{6 \times 2}{3} = 4 \quad (\text{Matches } y_B) $$

  • $z$-coordinate check:

$$ \frac{-10(1/2) - 4}{(1/2) + 1} = \frac{-5 - 4}{3/2} = \frac{-9 \times 2}{3} = -6 \quad (\text{Matches } z_B) $$

Since $k = \frac{1}{2} > 0$, point $B$ divides segment $AC$ internally in the ratio $1 : 2$.

💡 Example 4: Geometric Shape Verification (Isosceles Right Triangle)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Prove that the points $A(0, 7, 10)$, $B(-1, 6, 6)$, and $C(-4, 9, 6)$ form the vertices of an isosceles right-angled triangle.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Calculate $AB^2$

$$ AB^2 = (-1 - 0)^2 + (6 - 7)^2 + (6 - 10)^2 = (-1)^2 + (-1)^2 + (-4)^2 = 1 + 1 + 16 = 18 $$

$$ AB = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2} $$

Step 2: Calculate $BC^2$

$$ BC^2 = (-4 - (-1))^2 + (9 - 6)^2 + (6 - 6)^2 = (-3)^2 + (3)^2 + (0)^2 = 9 + 9 + 0 = 18 $$

$$ BC = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2} $$

Step 3: Calculate $AC^2$

$$ AC^2 = (-4 - 0)^2 + (9 - 7)^2 + (6 - 10)^2 = (-4)^2 + (2)^2 + (-4)^2 = 16 + 4 + 16 = 36 $$

$$ AC = \sqrt{36} = 6 $$

Step 4: Classification 1. Since $AB^2 = BC^2 = 18 \implies AB = BC$, triangle $\triangle ABC$ is isosceles. 2. Evaluating the converse of Pythagoras' Theorem:

$$ AB^2 + BC^2 = 18 + 18 = 36 = AC^2 $$

Thus, $\angle ABC = 90^\circ$.

Hence, $\triangle ABC$ is an isosceles right-angled triangle.

💡 Example 5: Locus of a Point Moving Equidistant from Fixed Points
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the equation of the set of points $P(x, y, z)$ such that its distances from points $A(3, 4, -5)$ and $B(-2, 1, 4)$ are equal.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Given condition:

$$ PA = PB \iff PA^2 = PB^2 $$

Using the squared distance formula:

$$ (x - 3)^2 + (y - 4)^2 + (z - (-5))^2 = (x - (-2))^2 + (y - 1)^2 + (z - 4)^2 $$

$$ (x - 3)^2 + (y - 4)^2 + (z + 5)^2 = (x + 2)^2 + (y - 1)^2 + (z - 4)^2 $$

Expand both sides completely:

$$ \text{LHS} = (x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 - 8y + 16) + (z^2 + 10z + 25) $$

$$ \text{LHS} = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 6x - 8y + 10z + 50 $$

$$ \text{RHS} = (x^2 + 4x + 4) + (y^2 - 2y + 1) + (z^2 - 8z + 16) $$

$$ \text{RHS} = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + 4x - 2y - 8z + 21 $$

Equate LHS and RHS:

$$ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - 6x - 8y + 10z + 50 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + 4x - 2y - 8z + 21 $$

Cancel second-degree terms ($x^2 + y^2 + z^2$):

$$ -6x - 8y + 10z + 50 = 4x - 2y - 8z + 21 $$

Collect all terms on the right-hand side:

$$ (4x + 6x) + (-2y + 8y) + (-8z - 10z) + (21 - 50) = 0 $$

$$ 10x + 6y - 18z - 29 = 0 $$

Geometric Interpretation: The locus represents a plane perpendicular to segment $AB$ passing through its midpoint—the perpendicular bisector plane of segment $AB$.

PART 6: Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

                          COMMON SIGN ERROR
          WRONG:  (x₂ - x₁)² = (1 - (-2))² = (1 - 2)² = 1
          RIGHT:  (x₂ - x₁)² = (1 - (-2))² = (1 + 2)² = 9

Trap 1: Double-Negative Sign Errors in Subtractions

  • The Slip: When coordinates are negative, students often write $(x_2 - x_1)^2$ as $(x_2 - x_1^2)$ or drop the double minus: $(1 - (-3))^2 \to (1 - 3)^2 = 4$.
  • Correct Practice: Always use explicit grouping parentheses:

$$ (x_2 - x_1)^2 = (1 - (-3))^2 = (1 + 3)^2 = 16 $$

Trap 2: Incorrect Radical Addition

  • The Slip: Writing $\sqrt{a + b + c} = \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} + \sqrt{c}$.
  • Example: $\sqrt{9 + 16 + 25} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2} \ne 3 + 4 + 5 = 12$. Radicals do not distribute over addition.

Trap 3: Premature Radical Decimal Approximation in Collinearity

  • The Slip: Approximating $\sqrt{32} \approx 5.65$ and $\sqrt{8} \approx 2.82$, then adding them as $8.47 \ne \sqrt{72} \approx 8.485$, leading to the false conclusion that points are not collinear.
  • Correct Practice: Always express radicals in simplified surd form:

$$ \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt{2}, \quad \sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2} \implies 4\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{72} $$

Collinearity is then established without decimal error.

Trap 4: Assuming Fixed Order of Collinear Points

  • The Slip: Only checking whether $AB + BC = AC$.
  • Correction: The points can lie in any spatial sequence:
  • If $B$ lies between $A$ and $C$: $AB + BC = AC$
  • If $A$ lies between $B$ and $C$: $BA + AC = BC$
  • If $C$ lies between $A$ and $B$: $AC + CB = AB$
  • Always identify the largest single distance first and check if it equals the sum of the two smaller distances.

Trap 5: Forgetting to Cancel Second-Degree Terms in Locus Problems

  • The Slip: Leaving $(x - a)^2$ unexpanded when asked for the equation of the locus.
  • Correction: Expanding $PA^2 = PB^2$ always cancels $x^2 + y^2 + z^2$, leaving a linear equation of the form $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$.

Sub-question 1: Collinearity of Nodes $A$, $B$, and $C$

Using the 3D Distance Formula:

$$ d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

$$ AB = \sqrt{(1 - 3)^2 + (1 - (-2))^2 + (-2 - 4)^2} = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + (3)^2 + (-6)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{49} = 7\text{ units} $$

  • For segment $BC$:

$$ BC = \sqrt{(-1 - 1)^2 + (4 - 1)^2 + (-8 - (-2))^2} = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + (3)^2 + (-6)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{49} = 7\text{ units} $$

Sub-question 2: Coordinates of Drone Sensor $P$ (External Division $2 : 1$)

$$ z_E = \frac{m \cdot z_D + n \cdot z_A}{m + n} = \frac{2\left(\frac{z_2 + z_3}{2}\right) + 1 \cdot z_1}{2 + 1} = \frac{(z_2 + z_3) + z_1}{3} = \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3} $$

Thus, the coordinates of $E$ are:

$$ E = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3},\, \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3},\, \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3}\right) $$

Marking Scheme: - $\left[1.5\text{ Marks}\right]$: Step-by-step substitution of ratio $2 : 1$ and arriving at the symmetric coordinates for $E$.

Step 3: Prove concurrency of all three medians
By symmetry: - Let $M_B$ be the midpoint of side $AC$. The point dividing the median $B M_B$ internally in the ratio $2 : 1$ has coordinates:

$$ \left(\frac{x_2 + 2\left(\frac{x_1 + x_3}{2}\right)}{2 + 1},\, \frac{y_2 + 2\left(\frac{y_1 + y_3}{2}\right)}{2 + 1},\, \frac{z_2 + 2\left(\frac{z_1 + z_3}{2}\right)}{2 + 1}\right) = \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3},\, \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3},\, \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3}\right) $$

  • Similarly, let $M_C$ be the midpoint of side $AB$. The point dividing the median $C M_C$ internally in the ratio $2 : 1$ has the identical coordinates:

$$ \left(\frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3},\, \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3},\, \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3}\right) $$

Since the point dividing each median in the ratio $2 : 1$ yields the identical unique point $G$, all three medians are concurrent.
The point of concurrency is the Centroid $G$:

$$ G\left(\frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3},\, \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3},\, \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3}\right) $$

Marking Scheme: - $\left[1.5\text{ Marks}\right]$: Demonstrating symmetry for medians from $B$ and $C$ and establishing concurrency at $G$.

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 Octant Determination: The Binary Rule

Rather than visualizing axes or memorizing the 8-octant Roman numeral table linearly, assign signs binary states where $(+) \rightarrow 0$ and $(-) \rightarrow 1$ in the order $(x, y, z)$:

  • Base index formula:

$$ \text{Octant Index} = 1 + \left[\text{SignBit}(x) \times 1 + \text{SignBit}(y) \times 2 + \text{SignBit}(z) \times 4\right] $$

  • The $z$-Coordinate Shortcut:
  • If $z > 0$, the point lies in Octants I to IV. The octant corresponds directly to the standard 2D Cartesian quadrant for $(x, y)$:
    • $(+, +) \rightarrow \text{I}$
    • $(-, +) \rightarrow \text{II}$
    • $(-, -) \rightarrow \text{III}$
    • $(+, -) \rightarrow \text{IV}$
  • If $z < 0$, determine the 2D quadrant of $(x, y)$ and add $4$:
    • $(+, +) \rightarrow 1 + 4 = \text{V}$
    • $(-, +) \rightarrow 2 + 4 = \text{VI}$
    • $(-, -) \rightarrow 3 + 4 = \text{VII}$
    • $(+, -) \rightarrow 4 + 4 = \text{VIII}$
  • Example: For $(-3, -4, -5)$, $(x, y)$ is in Quadrant III $\Rightarrow 3 + 4 = \text{Octant VII}$. Time taken: $< 2 \text{ seconds}$.

1.2 Projections & Perpendicular Distances: The "Missing Coordinate" Law

For any point $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$: * Foot of Perpendicular / Orthogonal Projection: * On coordinate planes: Zero out the missing coordinate variable. * Projection on $xy$-plane: $(x_1, y_1, 0)$ * Projection on $yz$-plane: $(0, y_1, z_1)$ * Projection on $zx$-plane: $(x_1, 0, z_1)$ * On coordinate axes: Retain only that axis's coordinate; zero out the remaining two. * Projection on $x$-axis: $(x_1, 0, 0)$ * Projection on $y$-axis: $(0, y_1, 0)$ * Projection on $z$-axis: $(0, 0, z_1)$ * Perpendicular Distance from an Axis: The distance from an axis equals the square root of the sum of squares of the other two coordinates:

$$ d_{x\text{-axis}} = \sqrt{y_1^2 + z_1^2}, \quad d_{y\text{-axis}} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + z_1^2}, \quad d_{z\text{-axis}} = \sqrt{x_1^2 + y_1^2} $$

  • Perpendicular Distance from a Coordinate Plane: The distance equals the absolute value of the single missing coordinate:

$$ d_{xy\text{-plane}} = |z_1|, \quad d_{yz\text{-plane}} = |x_1|, \quad d_{zx\text{-plane}} = |y_1| $$

1.3 Quick Collinearity Verification (Avoid Distance Formula)

Evaluating three square roots $AB + BC = AC$ is error-prone and consumes 4–5 minutes. * Vector / Direction Ratio Shortcut: For points $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, $C(x_3, y_3, z_3)$, compute:

$$ \Delta x_1 = x_2 - x_1, \quad \Delta y_1 = y_2 - y_1, \quad \Delta z_1 = z_2 - z_1 $$

$$ \Delta x_2 = x_3 - x_2, \quad \Delta y_2 = y_3 - y_2, \quad \Delta z_2 = z_3 - z_2 $$

Points $A, B, C$ are collinear if and only if:

$$ \frac{x_3 - x_2}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{y_3 - y_2}{y_2 - y_1} = \frac{z_3 - z_2}{z_2 - z_1} = \lambda $$

  • Board Writing Tip: While checking via ratios takes 15 seconds mentally, write in the answer script:

$$ \vec{AB} = \lambda \vec{BC} \text{ or } \frac{x_2-x_1}{x_3-x_2} = \frac{y_2-y_1}{y_3-y_2} = \frac{z_2-z_1}{z_3-z_2} = k $$

"Since the direction ratios of $AB$ and $BC$ are proportional and $B$ is a common point, $A, B, C$ are collinear." Full marks are awarded under the CBSE marking scheme without computing square roots.

1.4 Division by Coordinate Planes & Axes ($k:1$ Method)

To find the ratio in which the segment joining $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ is divided: * By the $xy$-plane ($z = 0$):

$$ k = -\frac{z_1}{z_2} \implies \text{Ratio } = -z_1 : z_2 $$

  • By the $yz$-plane ($x = 0$):

$$ k = -\frac{x_1}{x_2} \implies \text{Ratio } = -x_1 : x_2 $$

  • By the $zx$-plane ($y = 0$):

$$ k = -\frac{y_1}{y_2} \implies \text{Ratio } = -y_1 : y_2 $$

  • Interpretation of Sign:
  • If the ratio is positive ($-\frac{z_1}{z_2} > 0$, i.e., $z_1, z_2$ have opposite signs), the division is internal.
  • If the ratio is negative ($-\frac{z_1}{z_2} < 0$, i.e., $z_1, z_2$ have the same sign), the division is external in the ratio $|z_1| : |z_2|$.

1.5 The 4th Vertex of a Parallelogram in 5 Seconds

For a parallelogram $ABCD$ whose vertices taken in order are $A, B, C, D$: * Since the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ bisect each other at the same midpoint:

$$ \frac{x_A + x_C}{2} = \frac{x_B + x_D}{2} \implies D = A + C - B $$

  • Component-wise:

$$ x_D = x_A + x_C - x_B, \quad y_D = y_A + y_C - y_B, \quad z_D = z_A + z_C - z_B $$

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

2.1 The "Minus Sign" Trap in External Division

  • The Error: A student assumes the external section formula as:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 - n x_1}{m - n} $$

and then substitutes a negative value for the ratio $m:n$ (e.g., setting $m/n = -2/3$), resulting in double negation and an incorrect sign. * Examiner Guideline: * If using the ratio $k : 1$ with formula $\left(\frac{k x_2 + x_1}{k + 1}, \dots\right)$, a negative value of $k$ (e.g., $k = -2/3$) directly signifies external division in the ratio $2:3$. * If explicitly invoking the external division formula with indices $m$ and $n$, $m$ and $n$ must both be entered as positive magnitudes:

$$ \left(\frac{m x_2 - n x_1}{m - n}, \frac{m y_2 - n y_1}{m - n}, \frac{m z_2 - n z_1}{m - n}\right), \quad m \neq n $$

  • Mark Deduction: A sign error in the denominator or numerator results in an immediate deduction of 1 mark out of a 2- or 3-mark question.

2.2 Locus Problems: Squaring Without Grouping & Missing Constants

  • Typical Question: "Find the equation of the set of points $P$ such that $PA^2 + PB^2 = 2k^2$."
  • Examiner Pitfalls:
  • Dropping the Squared Bracket Expansion Terms: Students write $(x - x_1)^2 = x^2 - x_1^2$ under exam pressure.
  • Leaving Radicals in Locus Equations: When given $PA + PB = 2a$, students write $\sqrt{\dots} + \sqrt{\dots} = 2a$ and stop there or square both sides incorrectly as $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + B^2$.
  • CBSE Step Penalty: Failing to isolate one radical before squaring:

$$ \sqrt{(x-x_1)^2 + \dots} = 2a - \sqrt{(x-x_2)^2 + \dots} $$

 Squaring directly without showing isolation loses **1 to 1.5 marks** for algebraic structure.

Section Formula for Internal and External Division and Midpoint Formula

Topic Lecture Note: Section Formula (Internal & External Division) and Midpoint Formula

1. PEDAGOGICAL THEORY, INTUITION & CORE DEFINITIONS

Pedagogical Context

In two-dimensional coordinate geometry, a point dividing a directed line segment creates proportional projections onto the $X$ and $Y$ axes. Transitioning to three-dimensional Euclidean space ($\mathbb{R}^3$) preserves collinearity and affine ratios under parallel projection onto three mutually perpendicular reference planes ($XY$, $YZ$, and $ZX$ planes).

The Section Formula establishes the coordinate representation of a point $R$ lying on the straight line passing through two distinct points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ such that the directed segments $PR$ and $RQ$ stand in a given scalar ratio $m : n$.

Definition 1.1: Internal Division ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ be two distinct points in $\mathbb{R}^3$. A point $R(x, y, z)$ is said to divide the directed line segment $PQ$ internally in the ratio $m : n$ (where $m, n > 0$) if: 1. $R$ lies on the line segment joining $P$ and $Q$ between $P$ and $Q$. 2. The ratio of the directed Euclidean distances satisfies:

$$ \frac{PR}{RQ} = \frac{m}{n} $$

The coordinates of $R$ are given by:

$$ R(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{m x_2 + n x_1}{m + n}, \, \frac{m y_2 + n y_1}{m + n}, \, \frac{m z_2 + n z_1}{m + n} \right) $$

Definition 1.2: External Division ⭐⭐⭐⭐

A point $R(x, y, z)$ is said to divide the directed segment $PQ$ externally in the ratio $m : n$ (where $m \neq n$ and $m, n > 0$) if: 1. $R$ lies on the line passing through $P$ and $Q$, but strictly outside the segment $[PQ]$ (either beyond $Q$ if $m > n$, or before $P$ if $m < n$). 2. The ratio of distances satisfies:

$$ \frac{PR}{QR} = \frac{m}{n} $$

The coordinates of $R$ are given by:

$$ R(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{m x_2 - n x_1}{m - n}, \, \frac{m y_2 - n y_1}{m - n}, \, \frac{m z_2 - n z_1}{m - n} \right) $$

Geometric Interpretation of Negative Ratios: External division in ratio $m : n$ is algebraically identical to internal division in ratio $-m : n$ or $m : (-n)$. Setting $k = -m/n$ allows any collinear division to be parameterized uniformly.

Definition 1.3: Midpoint Formula ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The midpoint $M$ of the segment $PQ$ is the unique point dividing $PQ$ internally in the ratio $1 : 1$ ($m = n = 1$). Its coordinates are:

$$ M(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{x_1 + x_2}{2}, \, \frac{y_1 + y_2}{2}, \, \frac{z_1 + z_2}{2} \right) $$

Definition 1.4: Centroid of a Triangle ⭐⭐⭐⭐

The centroid $G$ of $\triangle ABC$ with vertices $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, and $C(x_3, y_3, z_3)$ is the point of concurrency of its medians. It divides each median internally in the ratio $2 : 1$. Its coordinates are:

$$ G(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3}, \, \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3}, \, \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3} \right) $$

Definition 1.5: Centroid of a Tetrahedron ⭐⭐⭐

The centroid $G$ of a tetrahedron with vertices $A_i(x_i, y_i, z_i)$ for $i \in \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ is the point of concurrency of the segments joining each vertex to the centroid of the opposite triangular face. It divides these segments internally in the ratio $3 : 1$:

$$ G(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4}{4}, \, \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3 + y_4}{4}, \, \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3 + z_4}{4} \right) $$

Theorem 2.1: The Internal Section Formula

Statement: Let $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ be two given points. The coordinates of the point $R(x, y, z)$ dividing the segment $PQ$ internally in the ratio $m : n$ are:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 + n x_1}{m + n}, \quad y = \frac{m y_2 + n y_1}{m + n}, \quad z = \frac{m z_2 + n z_1}{m + n} $$

1. Given:

Two points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$, and a point $R(x, y, z)$ lying on the line segment $PQ$ such that:

$$ \frac{PR}{RQ} = \frac{m}{n} \quad (m > 0, \, n > 0) $$

2. To Prove:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 + n x_1}{m + n}, \quad y = \frac{m y_2 + n y_1}{m + n}, \quad z = \frac{m z_2 + n z_1}{m + n} $$

3. Construction:

  • Draw perpendiculars $PL$, $RN$, and $QM$ from the points $P$, $R$, and $Q$ respectively onto the $XY$-plane.
  • The feet of these perpendiculars on the $XY$-plane are:

$$ L(x_1, y_1, 0), \quad N(x, y, 0), \quad M(x_2, y_2, 0) $$

  • Since $PL \perp XY\text{-plane}$, $RN \perp XY\text{-plane}$, and $QM \perp XY\text{-plane}$, the lines $PL$, $RN$, and $QM$ are parallel to the $Z$-axis and hence mutually parallel:

$$ PL \parallel RN \parallel QM $$

  • The points $L, N, M$ lie along a straight line in the $XY$-plane, which is the orthogonal projection of the line segment $PQ$ onto the $XY$-plane.
  • Through the point $R$, draw a line segment $ST$ parallel to the projected line $LM$, meeting the line $PL$ at $S$ and the line $QM$ at $T$.
           Z-axis
             |
             |       P(x1, y1, z1)
             |        \
             |         \ S
             |----------R(x, y, z)------- T
             |           \                |
             |            \               |
             |             Q(x2, y2, z2)  |
             |                            |
             +----------------------------+------ Y-axis
            /   L(x1,y1,0)   N(x,y,0)   M(x2,y2,0)
           /
          X-axis

4. Step-by-Step Algebraic Derivation:

In the plane containing the parallel lines $PL$, $RN$, and $QM$: - The quadrilateral $SLNR$ forms a rectangle, which implies:

$$ SL = RN = z $$

  • The quadrilateral $RNMT$ forms a rectangle, which implies:

$$ TM = RN = z $$

  • Now, consider the lengths along the vertical lines:

$$ PS = PL - SL = z_1 - z $$

$$ QT = QM - TM = z_2 - z $$

(Depending on orientation, the directed vertical segments are $z - z_1$ and $z_2 - z$.)

In $\triangle PSR$ and $\triangle QTR$: - $\angle PSR = \angle QTR = 90^\circ$ (since $ST$ is perpendicular to the vertical lines) - $\angle PRS = \angle QRT$ (vertically opposite angles)

Therefore, by Angle-Angle (AA) criterion:

$$ \triangle PSR \sim \triangle QTR $$

Corresponding sides of similar triangles are proportional:

$$ \frac{PR}{RQ} = \frac{SP}{TQ} $$

Substitute the geometric values:

$$ \frac{m}{n} = \frac{z - z_1}{z_2 - z} $$

Cross-multiplying yields:

$$ m(z_2 - z) = n(z - z_1) $$

$$ m z_2 - m z = n z - n z_1 $$

$$ m z + n z = m z_2 + n z_1 $$

$$ z(m + n) = m z_2 + n z_1 $$

Since $m + n \neq 0$:

$$ z = \frac{m z_2 + n z_1}{m + n} $$

By drawing perpendiculars to the $YZ$-plane (parallel to the $X$-axis) and $ZX$-plane (parallel to the $Y$-axis) respectively, similar triangles yield:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 + n x_1}{m + n} $$

$$ y = \frac{m y_2 + n y_1}{m + n} $$

5. Conclusion:

$$ R(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{m x_2 + n x_1}{m + n}, \, \frac{m y_2 + n y_1}{m + n}, \, \frac{m z_2 + n z_1}{m + n} \right) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.2: The External Section Formula

Statement: The coordinates of the point $R(x, y, z)$ dividing the line segment joining $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ externally in the ratio $m : n$ ($m \neq n$) are:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 - n x_1}{m - n}, \quad y = \frac{m y_2 - n y_1}{m - n}, \quad z = \frac{m z_2 - n z_1}{m - n} $$

1. Given:

$P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ are two points, and $R(x, y, z)$ lies on the line $PQ$ such that $Q$ lies between $P$ and $R$ (assuming $m > n$ without loss of generality):

$$ \frac{PR}{QR} = \frac{m}{n} $$

2. To Prove:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 - n x_1}{m - n}, \quad y = \frac{m y_2 - n y_1}{m - n}, \quad z = \frac{m z_2 - n z_1}{m - n} $$

3. Proof via Internal Division Inversion:

From the order of points on the line: $P - Q - R$, the segment $PR$ is divided internally by $Q$ into segments $PQ$ and $QR$.

Given:

$$ PR = m \cdot \lambda \quad \text{and} \quad QR = n \cdot \lambda \quad (\lambda > 0) $$

The length of segment $PQ$ is:

$$ PQ = PR - QR = m\lambda - n\lambda = (m - n)\lambda $$

Thus, the ratio in which $Q$ divides the segment $PR$ internally is:

$$ \frac{PQ}{QR} = \frac{(m - n)\lambda}{n\lambda} = \frac{m - n}{n} $$

Apply the Internal Section Formula to the point $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ dividing $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $R(x, y, z)$ internally in ratio $(m - n) : n$:

$$ x_2 = \frac{(m - n)x + n x_1}{(m - n) + n} = \frac{(m - n)x + n x_1}{m} $$

Isolating the coordinate $x$:

$$ m x_2 = (m - n)x + n x_1 $$

$$ (m - n)x = m x_2 - n x_1 $$

Since $m \neq n$, dividing both sides by $(m - n)$ gives:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 - n x_1}{m - n} $$

Applying the identical algebraic manipulation to the $y$ and $z$ coordinates:

$$ y_2 = \frac{(m - n)y + n y_1}{m} \implies y = \frac{m y_2 - n y_1}{m - n} $$

$$ z_2 = \frac{(m - n)z + n z_1}{m} \implies z = \frac{m z_2 - n z_1}{m - n} $$

4. Conclusion:

$$ R(x, y, z) = \left( \frac{m x_2 - n x_1}{m - n}, \, \frac{m y_2 - n y_1}{m - n}, \, \frac{m z_2 - n z_1}{m - n} \right) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.3: Coordinates of the Centroid of a Triangle

Statement: The medians of $\triangle ABC$ with vertices $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, and $C(x_3, y_3, z_3)$ concur at the centroid $G$, whose coordinates are:

$$ G = \left( \frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3}, \, \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3}, \, \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3} \right) $$

1. Given:

$\triangle ABC$ with vertices $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, and $C(x_3, y_3, z_3)$. Let $D$ be the midpoint of side $BC$, and $G$ be the point dividing median $AD$ internally in the ratio $2 : 1$.

2. Derivation:

  1. By the Midpoint Formula, the coordinates of $D$ are:

$$ D = \left( \frac{x_2 + x_3}{2}, \, \frac{y_2 + y_3}{2}, \, \frac{z_2 + z_3}{2} \right) $$

  1. $G(x, y, z)$ divides the segment joining $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $D\left(\frac{x_2 + x_3}{2}, \frac{y_2 + y_3}{2}, \frac{z_2 + z_3}{2}\right)$ internally in the ratio $2 : 1$ ($m = 2, n = 1$).

  2. Applying the Internal Section Formula for coordinate $x$:

$$ x = \frac{2 \cdot \left(\frac{x_2 + x_3}{2}\right) + 1 \cdot x_1}{2 + 1} = \frac{(x_2 + x_3) + x_1}{3} = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + x_3}{3} $$

  1. Similarly for $y$ and $z$:

$$ y = \frac{2 \cdot \left(\frac{y_2 + y_3}{2}\right) + 1 \cdot y_1}{2 + 1} = \frac{y_1 + y_2 + y_3}{3} $$

$$ z = \frac{2 \cdot \left(\frac{z_2 + z_3}{2}\right) + 1 \cdot z_1}{2 + 1} = \frac{z_1 + z_2 + z_3}{3} $$

  1. Symmetrical calculations for medians from $B$ to $AC$ and $C$ to $AB$ yield identical coordinates, proving concurrency at $G$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Problem 3.1: Fundamental Application (Internal & External)

Problem: Find the coordinates of the points which divide the line segment joining the points $P(1, -2, 3)$ and $Q(3, 4, -5)$ in the ratio $2 : 3$: 1. Internally 2. Externally

Solution:

Here, $(x_1, y_1, z_1) = (1, -2, 3)$, $(x_2, y_2, z_2) = (3, 4, -5)$, $m = 2$, and $n = 3$.

(i) Internal Division:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 + n x_1}{m + n} = \frac{2(3) + 3(1)}{2 + 3} = \frac{6 + 3}{5} = \frac{9}{5} $$

$$ y = \frac{m y_2 + n y_1}{m + n} = \frac{2(4) + 3(-2)}{2 + 3} = \frac{8 - 6}{5} = \frac{2}{5} $$

$$ z = \frac{m z_2 + n z_1}{m + n} = \frac{2(-5) + 3(3)}{2 + 3} = \frac{-10 + 9}{5} = -\frac{1}{5} $$

$$ \mathbf{R_{\text{int}} = \left( \frac{9}{5}, \, \frac{2}{5}, \, -\frac{1}{5} \right)} $$

(ii) External Division:

$$ x = \frac{m x_2 - n x_1}{m - n} = \frac{2(3) - 3(1)}{2 - 3} = \frac{6 - 3}{-1} = -3 $$

$$ y = \frac{m y_2 - n y_1}{m - n} = \frac{2(4) - 3(-2)}{2 - 3} = \frac{8 + 6}{-1} = -14 $$

$$ z = \frac{m z_2 - n z_1}{m - n} = \frac{2(-5) - 3(3)}{2 - 3} = \frac{-10 - 9}{-1} = 19 $$

$$ \mathbf{R_{\text{ext}} = (-3, \, -14, \, 19)} $$

Problem 3.2: Plane Intersection Ratio (NCERT Exemplar / High Board Frequency) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem: Find the ratio in which the $YZ$-plane divides the line segment formed by joining the points $A(-2, 4, 7)$ and $B(3, -5, 8)$. Also, determine the coordinates of the point of intersection.

Solution:

Step 1: Set up the ratio parameterization.
Let the $YZ$-plane divide the line segment $AB$ at point $R$ in the ratio $k : 1$.
By the section formula, the coordinates of $R$ are:

$$ R = \left( \frac{k(3) + 1(-2)}{k + 1}, \, \frac{k(-5) + 1(4)}{k + 1}, \, \frac{k(8) + 1(7)}{k + 1} \right) $$

$$ R = \left( \frac{3k - 2}{k + 1}, \, \frac{-5k + 4}{k + 1}, \, \frac{8k + 7}{k + 1} \right) $$

Step 2: Apply the geometric constraint.
Every point on the $YZ$-plane has an $x$-coordinate equal to zero ($x = 0$):

$$ \frac{3k - 2}{k + 1} = 0 $$

Assuming $k \neq -1$:

$$ 3k - 2 = 0 \implies 3k = 2 \implies k = \frac{2}{3} $$

Since $k > 0$, the division is internal in the ratio $2 : 3$.

Step 3: Calculate the coordinates of the intersection point.
Substitute $k = \frac{2}{3}$ into $y$ and $z$:

$$ y = \frac{-5\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) + 4}{\frac{2}{3} + 1} = \frac{\frac{-10 + 12}{3}}{\frac{5}{3}} = \frac{2}{5} $$

$$ z = \frac{8\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) + 7}{\frac{2}{3} + 1} = \frac{\frac{16 + 21}{3}}{\frac{5}{3}} = \frac{37}{5} $$

$$ \mathbf{\text{Ratio: } 2 : 3 \text{ internally}; \quad \text{Point of Intersection: } \left( 0, \, \frac{2}{5}, \, \frac{37}{5} \right)} $$

Scoring Breakdown: - Setting up coordinates in terms of $k : 1$: [1 Mark] - Equating $x$-coordinate to $0$ and solving for $k$: [1 Mark] - Stating the ratio nature (internal/external): [0.5 Mark] - Finding $y$ and $z$ coordinates: [1.5 Marks]

Problem 3.3: Collinearity via Section Formula (Board Standard Method) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem: Using the section formula, prove that the points $A(2, -3, 4)$, $B(-1, 2, 1)$, and $C\left(0, \frac{1}{3}, 2\right)$ are collinear.

Solution:

Step 1: Assume $C$ divides segment $AB$ in the ratio $k : 1$.
If points $A, B, C$ are collinear, there must exist a unique, consistent scalar ratio $k$ that satisfies all three coordinates simultaneously.

Coordinates of a point dividing $AB$ in ratio $k : 1$:

$$ x = \frac{-k + 2}{k + 1}, \quad y = \frac{2k - 3}{k + 1}, \quad z = \frac{k + 4}{k + 1} $$

Step 2: Equate the $x$-coordinate to the $x$-coordinate of $C$.

$$ \frac{-k + 2}{k + 1} = 0 \implies -k + 2 = 0 \implies k = 2 $$

Step 3: Check consistency with the $y$ and $z$ coordinates of $C$.
Substitute $k = 2$ into the expressions for $y$ and $z$:

$$ y = \frac{2(2) - 3}{2 + 1} = \frac{4 - 3}{3} = \frac{1}{3} $$

$$ z = \frac{2 + 4}{2 + 1} = \frac{6}{3} = 2 $$

Step 4: Comparison & Conclusion.
The values obtained $(0, 1/3, 2)$ match the coordinates of point $C$ exactly.
Hence, $C$ is a point dividing $AB$ internally in the ratio $2 : 1$.
Therefore, the points $A, B$, and $C$ are collinear.

Scoring Breakdown: - Setting up section formula with ratio $k : 1$: [1 Mark] - Solving $k$ from one coordinate: [1 Mark] - Verifying the remaining two coordinates: [1.5 Marks] - Concluding statement: [0.5 Mark]

Problem 3.4: Points of Trisection ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem: Find the coordinates of the points of trisection of the line segment joining the points $A(2, 1, -3)$ and $B(5, -8, 3)$.

Solution:

Trisection points divide the line segment into three equal parts. There are two such points, say $P$ and $Q$. - $P$ divides $AB$ internally in the ratio $1 : 2$. - $Q$ divides $AB$ internally in the ratio $2 : 1$ (or $Q$ is the midpoint of $PB$).

A(2, 1, -3) ----- P ----- Q ----- B(5, -8, 3)
      1        :     1    :    1

Coordinates of $P$ ($m = 1, n = 2$):

$$ x_P = \frac{1(5) + 2(2)}{1 + 2} = \frac{5 + 4}{3} = 3 $$

$$ y_P = \frac{1(-8) + 2(1)}{1 + 2} = \frac{-8 + 2}{3} = -2 $$

$$ z_P = \frac{1(3) + 2(-3)}{1 + 2} = \frac{3 - 6}{3} = -1 $$

$$ \mathbf{P = (3, -2, -1)} $$

Coordinates of $Q$ ($m = 2, n = 1$):

$$ x_Q = \frac{2(5) + 1(2)}{2 + 1} = \frac{10 + 2}{3} = 4 $$

$$ y_Q = \frac{2(-8) + 1(1)}{2 + 1} = \frac{-16 + 1}{3} = -5 $$

$$ z_Q = \frac{2(3) + 1(-3)}{2 + 1} = \frac{6 - 3}{3} = 1 $$

$$ \mathbf{Q = (4, -5, 1)} $$

(Verification: Midpoint of $PB = \left(\frac{3 + 5}{2}, \frac{-2 + (-8)}{2}, \frac{-1 + 3}{2}\right) = (4, -5, 1) = Q$.)

Problem 3.5: Unknown Vertex of a Parallelogram (NCERT Exemplar) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem: If three consecutive vertices of a parallelogram $ABCD$ are $A(3, -1, 2)$, $B(1, 2, -4)$, and $C(-1, 1, 2)$, find the coordinates of the fourth vertex $D$.

Solution:

Let the coordinates of the fourth vertex be $D(x, y, z)$.

Key Property: The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other. Therefore:

$$ \text{Midpoint of diagonal } AC = \text{Midpoint of diagonal } BD $$

Step 1: Compute the Midpoint of $AC$ ($M_{AC}$):

$$ M_{AC} = \left( \frac{3 + (-1)}{2}, \, \frac{-1 + 1}{2}, \, \frac{2 + 2}{2} \right) = (1, \, 0, \, 2) $$

Step 2: Compute the Midpoint of $BD$ ($M_{BD}$):

$$ M_{BD} = \left( \frac{1 + x}{2}, \, \frac{2 + y}{2}, \, \frac{-4 + z}{2} \right) $$

Step 3: Equate coordinates:

$$ \frac{1 + x}{2} = 1 \implies 1 + x = 2 \implies x = 1 $$

$$ \frac{2 + y}{2} = 0 \implies 2 + y = 0 \implies y = -2 $$

$$ \frac{-4 + z}{2} = 2 \implies -4 + z = 4 \implies z = 8 $$

$$ \mathbf{D = (1, \, -2, \, 8)} $$

1 Cross-Multiplication of Ratios ($m$ and $n$) Multiplying $m$ with $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $n$ with $(x_2, y_2, z_2)$:

$\mathbf{\frac{m x_2 + n x_1}{m + n}}$

3 Interpreting Negative Values of $k$ Stating "Ratio cannot be negative, hence no solution exists." A negative ratio $k = -p/q$ indicates external division in the absolute ratio $p : q$.
4 Plane Equations Confusion Setting $z = 0$ for $YZ$-plane. The missing variable is zero:
• $XY$-plane $\implies z = 0$
• $YZ$-plane $\implies x = 0$
• $ZX$-plane $\implies y = 0$

5. SPEED HACKS, SHORTCUTS & MNEMONICS

1. The "Criss-Cross" Visual Anchor (Zero-Memory Slip)

To avoid mixing up indices, write the ratio directly above or between the coordinate endpoints:

$$ \begin{array}{ccc} m & & n \\ \swarrow & \searrow & \\ P(x_1, y_1, z_1) & & Q(x_2, y_2, z_2) \end{array} $$

Multiply each ratio part by the coordinate on the opposite side:

$$ \text{Coordinate} = \frac{m \cdot (\text{far}) + n \cdot (\text{near})}{m + n} $$

2. The Direct Plane-Division Shortcut (1-Second MCQ Hack)

The ratio $k : 1$ in which reference planes divide the line segment joining $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ is given directly by the negative ratio of the missing coordinates:

  • Divided by $XY$-plane ($z = 0$):

$$ \text{Ratio} = -\frac{z_1}{z_2} $$

  • Divided by $YZ$-plane ($x = 0$):

$$ \text{Ratio} = -\frac{x_1}{x_2} $$

  • Divided by $ZX$-plane ($y = 0$):

$$ \text{Ratio} = -\frac{y_1}{y_2} $$

Sign Interpretation: - If $-\frac{x_1}{x_2} > 0 \implies$ Points have opposite signs $\implies$ Internal division. - If $-\frac{x_1}{x_2} < 0 \implies$ Points have same signs $\implies$ External division.

Verification using Problem 3.2: Points are $A(-2, 4, 7)$ and $B(3, -5, 8)$. Divided by $YZ$-plane ($x = 0$):

$$ \text{Ratio} = -\frac{x_1}{x_2} = -\left(\frac{-2}{3}\right) = \frac{2}{3} \implies 2 : 3 \text{ internally. (Instant answer!)} $$

3. Parallelogram 4th-Vertex Vector Shift Shortcut

For parallelogram $ABCD$, since diagonals bisect each other ($A + C = B + D$):

$$ D = A + C - B $$

In coordinate form:

$$ x_D = x_A + x_C - x_B $$

$$ y_D = y_A + y_C - y_B $$

$$ z_D = z_A + z_C - z_B $$

Verification using Problem 3.5: $A(3, -1, 2), B(1, 2, -4), C(-1, 1, 2)$ - $x_D = 3 + (-1) - 1 = 1$ - $y_D = -1 + 1 - 2 = -2$ - $z_D = 2 + 2 - (-4) = 8$

$$ \implies D = (1, -2, 8) \quad \text{(Solved in under 10 seconds!)} $$

4. General Plane Division Shortcut: $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$

If a line segment joining $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ is divided by any general plane $\Pi: Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$, the ratio $m : n$ is:

$$ \frac{m}{n} = -\frac{Ax_1 + By_1 + Cz_1 + D}{Ax_2 + By_2 + Cz_2 + D} = -\frac{\Pi(P)}{\Pi(Q)} $$
