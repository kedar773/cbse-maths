1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Foundations

1.1 Historical Context & The Epistemological Origin ⭐⭐⭐

Before formalizing the algebraic expansion of a binomial raised to an arbitrary positive integer power, mathematicians sought systematic methods to write down expressions like $(a+b)^2$, $(a+b)^3$, $(a+b)^4$, and higher powers without exhaustive polynomial multiplication.

  • Pingala’s Chhandas Shastra (c. 200 BCE): The earliest recorded systematic arrangement of binomial coefficients appears in Indian mathematics. Acharya Pingala described a rule known as Meru Prastara (the "staircase/pyramid of Mount Meru") to compute the number of poetic meters of given syllable counts (combinations of short Laghu and long Guru syllables).
  • Halayudha (10th Century CE): Wrote the Mritasanjeevani, a comprehensive commentary on Pingala's work, presenting the exact triangular array of binomial numbers.
  • Islamic & Chinese Contributions (11th–13th Century): Al-Karaji (1007 CE) and Omar Khayyam formulated early inductive methods. In China, Jia Xian (c. 1050) and Yang Hui (1261) constructed the triangle ("Yang Hui's Triangle").
  • Blaise Pascal (1654): Formalized these properties in his Traité du triangle arithmétique, explicitly proving the additive recurrence relation between rows and connecting the array directly to the theory of probability.
  • Sir Isaac Newton (1665): Generalized the theorem beyond positive integers to rational and negative real exponents (using infinite series).

1.2 Combinatorial & Geometric Intuition of $(a + b)^n$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Consider the expansion:

$$ (a+b)^n = \underbrace{(a+b)(a+b)(a+b)\cdots(a+b)}_{n \text{ identical binomial factors}} $$

When multiplying out these $n$ factors, every single term in the resulting sum is formed by selecting either $a$ or $b$ from each of the $n$ parentheses: 1. To obtain a term of the form $a^{n-r}b^r$, we must select the symbol $b$ from exactly $r$ factors and the symbol $a$ from the remaining $(n-r)$ factors. 2. The number of distinct ways to choose $r$ factors out of $n$ available factors to contribute a $b$ is given by the combination formula:

$$ \binom{n}{r} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} $$

  1. Because multiplication of real/complex numbers is commutative, all such selected products are identical: $a^{n-r}b^r$.
  2. Summing over all possible choices of $r$ from $r = 0$ (choosing zero $b$'s) to $r = n$ (choosing all $b$'s) produces the complete algebraic expansion.

1.3 Pascal’s Triangle & Its Structural Properties ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Arranging the coefficients of $(a+b)^n$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ produces the classical array:

Row 0:                     1
Row 1:                  1     1
Row 2:               1     2     1
Row 3:            1     3     3     1
Row 4:         1     4     6     4     1
Row 5:      1     5    10    10     5     1

Expressed in standard combinatorial notation:

$$ \begin{array}{ccccccccccc} n=0: & & & & & {}^0\mathrm{C}_0 & & & & & \\ n=1: & & & & {}^1\mathrm{C}_0 & & {}^1\mathrm{C}_1 & & & & \\ n=2: & & & {}^2\mathrm{C}_0 & & {}^2\mathrm{C}_1 & & {}^2\mathrm{C}_2 & & & \\ n=3: & & {}^3\mathrm{C}_0 & & {}^3\mathrm{C}_1 & & {}^3\mathrm{C}_2 & & {}^3\mathrm{C}_3 & & \\ n=4: & {}^4\mathrm{C}_0 & & {}^4\mathrm{C}_1 & & {}^4\mathrm{C}_2 & & {}^4\mathrm{C}_3 & & {}^4\mathrm{C}_4 & \\ n=5: & {}^5\mathrm{C}_0 & {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 & & {}^5\mathrm{C}_2 & & {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 & & {}^5\mathrm{C}_4 & {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 \end{array} $$

Fundamental Properties of the Triangle:

  1. Boundary Values: The first and last entries of row $n$ are always $1$:

$$ {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 = {}^n\mathrm{C}_n = 1 $$

  1. Pascal’s Additive Identity: Any intermediate entry is the sum of the two entries directly above it in the preceding row:

$$ {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = {}^{n-1}\mathrm{C}_{r-1} + {}^{n-1}\mathrm{C}_r \quad (1 \le r \le n-1) $$

  1. Bilateral Symmetry: The coefficients are symmetric about the center of each row:

$$ {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_{n-r} $$

  1. Row Sum Identity: The sum of all numbers in row $n$ is exactly $2^n$:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n = 2^n $$

  1. Alternating Row Sum Identity: For $n \ge 1$, the alternating sum of elements in any row is zero:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^n (-1)^r {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 - \cdots + (-1)^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_n = 0 $$

1.4 Formal Statement of the Binomial Theorem for Positive Integral Indices ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem (Binomial Theorem for $n \in \mathbb{N}$): Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$) and let $n$ be any positive integer ($n \in \mathbb{N}$). Then:

$$ (a + b)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 a^n b^0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1} b^1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 a^{n-2} b^2 + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n a^0 b^n $$

In compact sigma notation:

$$ (a + b)^n = \sum_{r=0}^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r $$

Essential Structural Observations:

  • Total Number of Terms: The expansion contains exactly $(n + 1)$ terms.
  • Degree Invariance: In every term, the sum of the exponents of $a$ and $b$ is constant and equal to $n$:

$$ \text{Power of } a + \text{Power of } b = (n - r) + r = n $$

  • Decreasing and Increasing Powers: The index of $a$ decreases monotonically from $n$ to $0$, while the index of $b$ increases monotonically from $0$ to $n$.
  • General Term ($T_{r+1}$): The $(r+1)$-th term in the expansion is denoted by:

$$ T_{r+1} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r, \quad \text{where } r \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\} $$

  • Symmetry of Coefficients: The binomial coefficients of terms equidistant from the beginning and the end are equal because ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_{n-r}$.

2. Rigorous Proofs & Mathematical Derivations

Proof 1: Pascal’s Identity (The Additive Lemma) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$ Theorem: {}^{n}\mathrm{C}_{r} + {}^{n}\mathrm{C}_{r-1} = {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_{r} \quad (n, r \in \mathbb{N}, \; 1 \le r \le n) $$

  • Given: Integers $n, r$ such that $n \ge 1$ and $1 \le r \le n$.
  • To Prove: ${}^{n}\mathrm{C}_{r} + {}^{n}\mathrm{C}_{r-1} = {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_{r}$

Algebraic Derivation:

Starting from the Left Hand Side ($\text{LHS}$):

$$ \text{LHS} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_r + {}^n\mathrm{C}_{r-1} $$

Using the definition of combinations ${}^n\mathrm{C}_k = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} + \frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-(r-1))!} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} + \frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r+1)!} $$

Expand factorials to reveal common factors ($r! = r \cdot (r-1)!$ and $(n-r+1)! = (n-r+1)(n-r)!$):

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{n!}{r(r-1)!(n-r)!} + \frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r+1)(n-r)!} $$

Factor out the common factor $\frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r)!}$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r)!} \left[ \frac{1}{r} + \frac{1}{n-r+1} \right] $$

Find a common denominator for the bracketed expression:

$$ \frac{1}{r} + \frac{1}{n-r+1} = \frac{(n-r+1) + r}{r(n-r+1)} = \frac{n+1}{r(n-r+1)} $$

Substitute back into the expression:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{n!}{(r-1)!(n-r)!} \cdot \frac{n+1}{r(n-r+1)} $$

Group the terms in numerator and denominator:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{(n+1) \cdot n!}{[r \cdot (r-1)!] \cdot [(n-r+1) \cdot (n-r)!]} $$

Using $(k+1) \cdot k! = (k+1)!$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{(n+1)!}{r!(n+1-r)!} $$

Recognizing this as ${}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_r$:

$$ \text{LHS} = {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_r = \text{RHS} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2: Principle of Mathematical Induction (PMI) on the Binomial Theorem ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Given: Real or complex numbers $a, b$, and $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
  • To Prove:

$$ P(n): (a+b)^n = \sum_{r=0}^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r}b^r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 a^n + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1}b + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n b^n $$

Base Step ($n = 1$):

For $n = 1$:

$$ \text{LHS} = (a+b)^1 = a+b $$

$$ \text{RHS} = \sum_{r=0}^1 {}^1\mathrm{C}_r a^{1-r}b^r = {}^1\mathrm{C}_0 a^1 b^0 + {}^1\mathrm{C}_1 a^0 b^1 = 1 \cdot a \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 \cdot b = a+b $$

Since $\text{LHS} = \text{RHS}$, the base proposition $P(1)$ is True.

Induction Hypothesis:

Assume the proposition $P(k)$ is true for some arbitrary positive integer $k \ge 1$:

$$ (a+b)^k = {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^{k-1}b + {}^k\mathrm{C}_2 a^{k-2}b^2 + \cdots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_r a^{k-r}b^r + \cdots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^k $$

Inductive Step:

We must show that $P(k+1)$ is true, i.e.:

$$ (a+b)^{k+1} = \sum_{r=0}^{k+1} {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r a^{k+1-r}b^r $$

Express $(a+b)^{k+1}$ as $(a+b)(a+b)^k$:

$$ (a+b)^{k+1} = (a+b) \left[ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^{k-1}b + {}^k\mathrm{C}_2 a^{k-2}b^2 + \cdots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^k \right] $$

Distribute $(a + b)$ over the expanded polynomial:

$$ (a+b)^{k+1} = a \left[ \sum_{r=0}^k {}^k\mathrm{C}_r a^{k-r}b^r \right] + b \left[ \sum_{r=0}^k {}^k\mathrm{C}_r a^{k-r}b^r \right] $$

Multiply $a$ and $b$ through each respective sum:

$$ (a+b)^{k+1} = \left[ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^{k+1} + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^k b + {}^k\mathrm{C}_2 a^{k-1}b^2 + \cdots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k a b^k \right] + \left[ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^k b + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^{k-1}b^2 + \cdots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_{k-1} a b^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^{k+1} \right] $$

Collect like terms of the form $a^{k+1-r}b^r$:

$$ (a+b)^{k+1} = {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^{k+1} + \left( {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 \right) a^k b + \left( {}^k\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 \right) a^{k-1} b^2 + \cdots + \left( {}^k\mathrm{C}_r + {}^k\mathrm{C}_{r-1} \right) a^{k+1-r} b^r + \cdots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^{k+1} $$

Apply Pascal's Identity (${}^k\mathrm{C}_r + {}^k\mathrm{C}_{r-1} = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r$) to each grouped coefficient: * ${}^k\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_1$ * ${}^k\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_2$ * In general: ${}^k\mathrm{C}_r + {}^k\mathrm{C}_{r-1} = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r$

For the boundary terms: * ${}^k\mathrm{C}_0 = 1 = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_0$ * ${}^k\mathrm{C}_k = 1 = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_{k+1}$

Substitute these back into the expression:

$$ (a+b)^{k+1} = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_0 a^{k+1} + {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_1 a^k b + {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_2 a^{k-1}b^2 + \cdots + {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r a^{k+1-r}b^r + \cdots + {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_{k+1} b^{k+1} $$

$$ (a+b)^{k+1} = \sum_{r=0}^{k+1} {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r a^{k+1-r}b^r $$

Conclusion:

$P(k+1)$ holds true whenever $P(k)$ is assumed true. Since the base case $P(1)$ is true, by the Principle of Mathematical Induction, $P(n)$ is true for all positive integers $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 3: Fundamental Corollary Deductions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Corollary 1: Expansion of $(a - b)^n$

Replace $b$ with $(-b)$ in the standard expansion:

$$ (a - b)^n = [a + (-b)]^n = \sum_{r=0}^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r}(-b)^r = \sum_{r=0}^n (-1)^r {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r}b^r $$

$$ (a - b)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 a^n - {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1}b + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 a^{n-2}b^2 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 a^{n-3}b^3 + \cdots + (-1)^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_n b^n $$

The signs alternate strictly: positive for even indices $r$, negative for odd indices $r$.

Corollary 2: Expansion of $(1 + x)^n$

Substitute $a = 1$ and $b = x$:

$$ (1 + x)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 x + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n = \sum_{r=0}^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_r x^r $$

Corollary 3: Expansion of $(1 - x)^n$

Substitute $a = 1$ and $b = -x$:

$$ (1 - x)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 x + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \cdots + (-1)^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n = \sum_{r=0}^n (-1)^r {}^n\mathrm{C}_r x^r $$

Corollary 4: Sum and Alternating Sum of Coefficients

  • Set $x = 1$ in $(1+x)^n$:

$$ (1 + 1)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n \implies \sum_{r=0}^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = 2^n $$

  • Set $x = 1$ in $(1-x)^n$:

$$ (1 - 1)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 - \cdots + (-1)^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_n \implies \sum_{r=0}^n (-1)^r {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = 0 $$

3. Solved Examples & Step-by-Step Problem Breakdown

Problem 1: Direct Polynomial Expansion with Fractional & Radicals ⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Expand completely: $\left(2x - \frac{1}{x}\right)^5, \; x \ne 0$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Identification: Here $a = 2x$, $b = -\frac{1}{x}$, and $n = 5$.
  • Number of terms = $n + 1 = 6$.

Write down the general Binomial expansion:

$$ \left(2x - \frac{1}{x}\right)^5 = \sum_{r=0}^5 {}^5\mathrm{C}_r (2x)^{5-r} \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^r $$

Write every term systematically:

$$ \begin{aligned} \left(2x - \frac{1}{x}\right)^5 &= {}^5\mathrm{C}_0 (2x)^5 \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^0 \\ &\quad + {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 (2x)^4 \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^1 \\ &\quad + {}^5\mathrm{C}_2 (2x)^3 \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^2 \\ &\quad + {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 (2x)^2 \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^3 \\ &\quad + {}^5\mathrm{C}_4 (2x)^1 \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^4 \\ &\quad + {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 (2x)^0 \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^5 \end{aligned} $$

Calculate the binomial coefficients for $n = 5$:

$$ {}^5\mathrm{C}_0 = 1, \quad {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 = 5, \quad {}^5\mathrm{C}_2 = 10, \quad {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 = 10, \quad {}^5\mathrm{C}_4 = 5, \quad {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 = 1 $$

Compute each term individually: * Term 1 ($r=0$):

$$ 1 \cdot (32x^5) \cdot 1 = 32x^5 $$

  • Term 2 ($r=1$):

$$ 5 \cdot (16x^4) \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right) = -80x^3 $$

  • Term 3 ($r=2$):

$$ 10 \cdot (8x^3) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{x^2}\right) = +80x $$

  • Term 4 ($r=3$):

$$ 10 \cdot (4x^2) \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{x^3}\right) = -\frac{40}{x} $$

  • Term 5 ($r=4$):

$$ 5 \cdot (2x) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{x^4}\right) = +\frac{10}{x^3} $$

  • Term 6 ($r=5$):

$$ 1 \cdot 1 \cdot \left(-\frac{1}{x^5}\right) = -\frac{1}{x^5} $$

Combine all simplified terms:

$$ \left(2x - \frac{1}{x}\right)^5 = 32x^5 - 80x^3 + 80x - \frac{40}{x} + \frac{10}{x^3} - \frac{1}{x^5} $$

Problem 2: Radical Simplification via Symmetric Sums ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement (NCERT Exemplar): Evaluate $(\sqrt{3} + 1)^5 - (\sqrt{3} - 1)^5$. Hence or otherwise, show that the result is an integer.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Let $a = \sqrt{3}$ and $b = 1$. Consider the general expansions of $(a+b)^n$ and $(a-b)^n$:

$$ (a+b)^5 = {}^5\mathrm{C}_0 a^5 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 a^4 b + {}^5\mathrm{C}_2 a^3 b^2 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 a^2 b^3 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_4 a b^4 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 b^5 $$

$$ (a-b)^5 = {}^5\mathrm{C}_0 a^5 - {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 a^4 b + {}^5\mathrm{C}_2 a^3 b^2 - {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 a^2 b^3 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_4 a b^4 - {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 b^5 $$

Subtract the second equation from the first:

$$ (a+b)^5 - (a-b)^5 = 2 \left[ {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 a^4 b + {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 a^2 b^3 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 b^5 \right] $$

(All even-indexed terms ${}^5\mathrm{C}_0, {}^5\mathrm{C}_2, {}^5\mathrm{C}_4$ cancel out).

Substitute ${}^5\mathrm{C}_1 = 5$, ${}^5\mathrm{C}_3 = 10$, and ${}^5\mathrm{C}_5 = 1$:

$$ (a+b)^5 - (a-b)^5 = 2 \left[ 5 a^4 b + 10 a^2 b^3 + b^5 \right] $$

Substitute $a = \sqrt{3}$ and $b = 1$: * $a^2 = (\sqrt{3})^2 = 3$ * $a^4 = (a^2)^2 = 3^2 = 9$ * $b = 1, \; b^3 = 1, \; b^5 = 1$

Calculate the bracketed term:

$$ 5(9)(1) + 10(3)(1) + 1 = 45 + 30 + 1 = 76 $$

Multiply by 2:

$$ (\sqrt{3}+1)^5 - (\sqrt{3}-1)^5 = 2 \times 76 = 152 $$

Since $152 \in \mathbb{Z}$, the value is an integer.

Problem 3: Large Number Approximation ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Using the Binomial Theorem, evaluate $(0.99)^5$ correct to 4 decimal places.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Express $0.99$ as $(1 - 0.01)$:

$$ (0.99)^5 = (1 - 0.01)^5 $$

Apply the Binomial Theorem with $a = 1$, $x = 0.01 = 10^{-2}$, and $n = 5$:

$$ (1 - x)^5 = {}^5\mathrm{C}_0 - {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 x + {}^5\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 - {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_4 x^4 - {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 x^5 $$

Substitute $x = 0.01$:

$$ (1 - 0.01)^5 = 1 - 5(0.01) + 10(0.01)^2 - 10(0.01)^3 + 5(0.01)^4 - (0.01)^5 $$

Compute each power: * Term 1: $1$ * Term 2: $-5(0.01) = -0.05$ * Term 3: $+10(0.0001) = +0.0010$ * Term 4: $-10(0.000001) = -0.00001$ * Terms with $x^4$ and $x^5$ produce values below $10^{-7}$, which do not affect the 4th decimal place.

Sum the relevant values:

$$ \text{Positive contributions} = 1 + 0.0010 = 1.0010 $$

$$ \text{Negative contributions} = 0.05 + 0.00001 = 0.05001 $$

$$ \text{Value} = 1.0010 - 0.05001 = 0.95099 $$

Rounding to 4 decimal places:

$$ (0.99)^5 \approx 0.9510 $$

Problem 4: Divisibility & Remainder Problems ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement (CBSE Standard Board / NCERT): Prove that $6^n - 5n - 1$ is divisible by $25$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Express the base $6$ as $(1 + 5)$:

$$ 6^n = (1 + 5)^n $$

Expand $(1 + 5)^n$ using the Binomial Theorem:

$$ 6^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 (5) + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 (5)^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (5)^3 + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5)^n $$

Evaluate the first two terms: * ${}^n\mathrm{C}_0 = 1$ * ${}^n\mathrm{C}_1 (5) = n \cdot 5 = 5n$

Substitute these into the equation:

$$ 6^n = 1 + 5n + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 (5^2) + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (5^3) + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5^n) $$

Rearrange terms by moving $1 + 5n$ to the left side:

$$ 6^n - 5n - 1 = {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 (25) + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (125) + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5^n) $$

Factor out $25 = 5^2$ from all terms on the right-hand side:

$$ 6^n - 5n - 1 = 25 \left[ {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (5) + {}^n\mathrm{C}_4 (5^2) + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5^{n-2}) \right] $$

Since $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and each ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r$ is an integer, the expression inside the brackets:

$$ k = {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (5) + \cdots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5^{n-2}) $$

is an integer ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$).

Therefore:

$$ 6^n - 5n - 1 = 25k, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z} $$

Hence, $6^n - 5n - 1$ is divisible by $25$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Trap 1: Failing to Distribute the Exponent to the Constant Coefficient

  • Student Error: Writing $(3x)^3 = 3x^3$.
  • Correct Form: $(3x)^3 = 3^3 \cdot x^3 = 27x^3$.
  • Examiner Insight: In expressions like $\left(2x^2 - \frac{3}{x}\right)^4$, students often evaluate the coefficient as $2 \cdot 3$ instead of raising each term to its respective power:

$$ {}^4\mathrm{C}_1 (2x^2)^3 \left(\frac{3}{x}\right)^1 = 4 \cdot (8x^6) \cdot \left(\frac{3}{x}\right) = 96x^5 \quad (\text{NOT } 24x^5) $$

Trap 2: Sign Confusion in Alternating Series $(a - b)^n$

  • Student Error: Writing $(a - b)^n = a^n - {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1}b - {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 a^{n-2}b^2 - \dots$
  • Correct Rule: The negative sign must be paired with $b$ as $(-b)^r$.

$$ \text{Sign} = (-1)^r $$

Even powers of $r$ ($r=0, 2, 4, \dots$) yield $+$, and odd powers ($r=1, 3, 5, \dots$) yield $-$.

Trap 3: Confusion Between Term Number and Index $r$

  • The Trap: The 4th term ($T_4$) corresponds to $r = 3$, not $r = 4$.
  • Rule: The general term is indexed as $T_{r+1}$:

$$ T_{\text{desired}} = T_{r+1} \implies r = \text{desired} - 1 $$

Trap 4: Truncation Mistakes in Binomial Approximations

  • Student Error: In evaluating $(1.01)^{10}$, keeping only two terms while four decimal places are requested.
  • Guideline:
  • For precision to $k$ decimal places, keep terms until the magnitude of the next term is strictly smaller than $\frac{1}{2} \times 10^{-k}$.
  • For example, $(0.01)^2 = 10^{-4}$, which affects the 4th decimal place. The term ${}^{10}\mathrm{C}_2 (0.01)^2 = 45 \times 10^{-4} = 0.0045$ must be included.

5. Speed Hacks, Mnemonics & Golden Points

Golden Point 1: Symmetry Shortcut for Double Expansions

Save time on combined expansions by cancelling terms before expanding:

$$ \mathbf{(a+b)^n + (a-b)^n = 2 \left[ {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 a^n + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 a^{n-2}b^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_4 a^{n-4}b^4 + \cdots \right]} \quad (\text{Twice the sum of odd-positioned terms}) $$

$$ \mathbf{(a+b)^n - (a-b)^n = 2 \left[ {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1}b + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 a^{n-3}b^3 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_5 a^{n-5}b^5 + \cdots \right]} \quad (\text{Twice the sum of even-positioned terms}) $$

Golden Point 2: Rapid Pascal Coefficient Generation

Rather than recalculating factorials for ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r$, generate row entries iteratively using the ratio property:

$$ {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_{r-1} \times \left(\frac{n - r + 1}{r}\right) $$

Example for $n = 7$:

  1. First entry: ${}^7\mathrm{C}_0 = 1$
  2. Next ($r=1$): $1 \times \frac{7}{1} = 7$
  3. Next ($r=2$): $7 \times \frac{6}{2} = 21$
  4. Next ($r=3$): $21 \times \frac{5}{3} = 35$
  5. By symmetry: ${}^7\mathrm{C}_4 = 35, \; {}^7\mathrm{C}_5 = 21, \; {}^7\mathrm{C}_6 = 7, \; {}^7\mathrm{C}_7 = 1$

Golden Point 3: The "Sum of All Coefficients" Shortcut

To find the sum of all coefficients in any polynomial expansion:

$$ \text{Sum of Coefficients} = \text{Substitute all variable values with } 1 $$

Examples:

  • For $(2x - 3y)^6$:

$$ \text{Sum of coefficients} = (2(1) - 3(1))^6 = (-1)^6 = 1 $$

  • For $(5x^2 - 4x + 1)^{10}$:

$$ \text{Sum of coefficients} = (5(1)^2 - 4(1) + 1)^{10} = (2)^{10} = 1024 $$

Golden Point 4: Remainder & Divisibility Template

For expressions of the form $(1 + a)^n$:

$$ (1 + a)^n = 1 + n a + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} a^2 + \cdots + a^n $$

  • Modulo $a^2$:

$$ (1 + a)^n \equiv 1 + na \pmod{a^2} $$

  • Quick Rule: $(1+a)^n - na - 1$ is always divisible by $a^2$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Historical Context & Combinatorial Motivation (⭐⭐⭐)

Before formalizing the theorem, consider the algebraic product:

$$ (a + b)^n = \underbrace{(a + b)(a + b)(a + b) \cdots (a + b)}_{n \text{ factors}} $$

When expanding this product by distributive law without collecting like terms, each term is formed by choosing either $a$ or $b$ from each of the $n$ distinct parentheses. - A term of the form $a^{n-r} b^r$ corresponds to choosing $b$ from exactly $r$ factors and $a$ from the remaining $(n - r)$ factors. - The total number of ways to choose $r$ factors out of $n$ available factors to contribute $b$ is given by the combination formula:

$$ \binom{n}{r} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = \frac{n!}{r!(n - r)!} $$

Hence, the coefficient of $a^{n-r} b^r$ in the expansion is uniquely determined by ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r$.

1.2 Pascal’s Triangle & Recurrence Relation (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

The coefficients can be arranged in a triangular array discovered by Blaise Pascal (and known earlier to Pingala as Meru Prastara):

$$ \begin{array}{rccccccccc} n = 0: & & & & & 1 & & & & \\ n = 1: & & & & 1 & & 1 & & & \\ n = 2: & & & 1 & & 2 & & 1 & & \\ n = 3: & & 1 & & 3 & & 3 & & 1 & \\ n = 4: & 1 & & 4 & & 6 & & 4 & & 1 \end{array} $$

Fundamental Combinatorial Identity (Pascal's Rule)

For any $n, r \in \mathbb{N}$ with $1 \le r \le n$:

$$ {}^n\mathrm{C}_r + {}^n\mathrm{C}_{r-1} = {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_r $$

Proof:

$$ \begin{aligned} {}^n\mathrm{C}_r + {}^n\mathrm{C}_{r-1} &= \frac{n!}{r!(n - r)!} + \frac{n!}{(r - 1)!(n - r + 1)!} \\ &= \frac{n!}{(r - 1)!(n - r)!} \left[ \frac{1}{r} + \frac{1}{n - r + 1} \right] \\ &= \frac{n!}{(r - 1)!(n - r)!} \left[ \frac{n - r + 1 + r}{r(n - r + 1)} \right] \\ &= \frac{n!(n + 1)}{[r(r - 1)!][(n - r + 1)(n - r)!]} \\ &= \frac{(n + 1)!}{r!(n + 1 - r)!} = {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_r \quad \blacksquare \end{aligned} $$

1.3 Statement of the Binomial Theorem for Positive Integral Index (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$). Then:

$$ (a + b)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 a^n + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1}b + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 a^{n-2}b^2 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r}b^r + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n b^n $$

Using summation notation:

$$ (a + b)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: Principle of Mathematical Induction (PMI) on $(a + b)^n$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Given: $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
To Prove:

$$ P(n): (a + b)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r $$

Step 1: Base Case ($n = 1$)

For $n = 1$:

$$ \text{L.H.S.} = (a + b)^1 = a + b $$

$$ \text{R.H.S.} = \sum_{r=0}^{1} {}^1\mathrm{C}_r a^{1-r} b^r = {}^1\mathrm{C}_0 a^1 b^0 + {}^1\mathrm{C}_1 a^0 b^1 = 1 \cdot a \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 1 \cdot b = a + b $$

Since $\text{L.H.S.} = \text{R.H.S.}$, the statement $P(1)$ is true.

Step 2: Inductive Hypothesis

Assume that $P(k)$ is true for some positive integer $k \ge 1$:

$$ (a + b)^k = {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^{k-1}b + {}^k\mathrm{C}_2 a^{k-2}b^2 + \dots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_r a^{k-r}b^r + \dots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^k $$

Step 3: Inductive Step

We must prove that $P(k + 1)$ is true, i.e.:

$$ (a + b)^{k+1} = \sum_{r=0}^{k+1} {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r a^{k+1-r} b^r $$

Starting from the algebraic definition:

$$ (a + b)^{k+1} = (a + b)(a + b)^k $$

Substitute the induction hypothesis:

$$ (a + b)^{k+1} = (a + b) \left[ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^{k-1}b + {}^k\mathrm{C}_2 a^{k-2}b^2 + \dots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^k \right] $$

Distribute $a$ and $b$ across the summation:

$$ \begin{aligned} (a + b)^{k+1} &= a \left[ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^{k-1}b + \dots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^k \right] + b \left[ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^{k-1}b + \dots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^k \right] \\ &= \left[ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^{k+1} + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^k b + {}^k\mathrm{C}_2 a^{k-1}b^2 + \dots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k a b^k \right] \\ &\quad + \left[ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^k b + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 a^{k-1}b^2 + \dots + {}^k\mathrm{C}_{k-1} a b^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^{k+1} \right] \end{aligned} $$

Group like terms with identical powers of $a$ and $b$:

$$ \begin{aligned} (a + b)^{k+1} = {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 a^{k+1} &+ \left( {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 \right) a^k b \\ &+ \left( {}^k\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^k\mathrm{C}_1 \right) a^{k-1} b^2 \\ &\;\vdots \\ &+ \left( {}^k\mathrm{C}_r + {}^k\mathrm{C}_{r-1} \right) a^{k+1-r} b^r \\ &\;\vdots \\ &+ \left( {}^k\mathrm{C}_k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_{k-1} \right) a b^k + {}^k\mathrm{C}_k b^{k+1} \end{aligned} $$

Using Pascal's Identity: ${}^k\mathrm{C}_r + {}^k\mathrm{C}_{r-1} = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r$, and noting:

$$ {}^k\mathrm{C}_0 = 1 = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_0 \quad \text{and} \quad {}^k\mathrm{C}_k = 1 = {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_{k+1} $$

Substitute these relations:

$$ \begin{aligned} (a + b)^{k+1} &= {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_0 a^{k+1} + {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_1 a^k b + {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_2 a^{k-1} b^2 + \dots + {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r a^{k+1-r} b^r + \dots + {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_{k+1} b^{k+1} \\ &= \sum_{r=0}^{k+1} {}^{k+1}\mathrm{C}_r a^{k+1-r} b^r \end{aligned} $$

Thus, $P(k + 1)$ is true whenever $P(k)$ is true.

Conclusion

By the Principle of Mathematical Induction, $P(n)$ is true for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

$$ \mathbf{Q.E.D.} $$

3. Seven Invariant Observations of the Expansion (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

  1. Number of Terms:
    The expansion contains exactly $(n + 1)$ terms. Since $r$ ranges from $0$ to $n$, the cardinal count of terms is $n - 0 + 1 = n + 1$.
  2. Conservation of Degree:
    In every individual term ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r$, the sum of the exponents of $a$ and $b$ is:

$$ (n - r) + r = n $$

Each term is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $n$ in $a$ and $b$. 3. Monotonic Exponents:
As we progress from left to right: - Exponent of $a$ strictly decreases from $n$ to $0$: $n, n-1, n-2, \dots, 1, 0$. - Exponent of $b$ strictly increases from $0$ to $n$: $0, 1, 2, \dots, n-1, n$. 4. Symmetry of Coefficients:
Because ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_{n-r}$, binomial coefficients equidistant from the beginning and the end are equal:

$$ {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 = {}^n\mathrm{C}_n, \quad {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 = {}^n\mathrm{C}_{n-1}, \quad {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 = {}^n\mathrm{C}_{n-2}, \dots $$

  1. The General Term ($T_{r+1}$):
    The $(r+1)^{\text{th}}$ term, denoted by $T_{r+1}$, is given by:

$$ T_{r+1} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r, \quad \text{where } r \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\} $$

(Note: To compute the $k^{\text{th}}$ term, choose index $r = k - 1$.) 6. Term from the End:
The $m^{\text{th}}$ term from the end in $(a + b)^n$ equals the $(n - m + 2)^{\text{th}}$ term from the beginning.
Alternatively, it is the $m^{\text{th}}$ term from the beginning of $(b + a)^n$:

$$ T_{m \text{ (from end)}} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_{n - m + 1} a^{m - 1} b^{n - m + 1} $$

  1. Sum of All Binomial Coefficients:
    Setting $a = 1$ and $b = 1$ in $(a + b)^n$:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^{n} {}^n\mathrm{C}_r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n = 2^n $$

4. Special Cases & Standard Deductions

4.1 Expansion of $(a - b)^n$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Substitute $b \to -b$ in $(a + b)^n$:

$$ (a - b)^n = [a + (-b)]^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} (-b)^r = \sum_{r=0}^{n} (-1)^r {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r $$

Expanded explicitly:

$$ (a - b)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 a^n - {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1}b + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 a^{n-2}b^2 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 a^{n-3}b^3 + \dots + (-1)^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_n b^n $$

  • The terms are alternating in sign: positive when $r$ is even, negative when $r$ is odd.
  • General Term: $T_{r+1} = (-1)^r {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r$.

4.2 Expansion of $(1 + x)^n$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Substitute $a = 1$ and $b = x$:

$$ (1 + x)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 x + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_r x^r + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n $$

  • General Term: $T_{r+1} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_r x^r$.
  • The coefficient of $x^r$ is directly ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r$.

4.3 Expansion of $(1 - x)^n$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Substitute $a = 1$ and $b = -x$:

$$ (1 - x)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 x + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \dots + (-1)^r {}^n\mathrm{C}_r x^r + \dots + (-1)^n {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n $$

  • General Term: $T_{r+1} = (-1)^r {}^n\mathrm{C}_r x^r$.

4.4 Sum and Difference of Conjugate Expansions (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ \begin{aligned} (a + b)^n + (a - b)^n &= 2 \left[ {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 a^n + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 a^{n-2}b^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_4 a^{n-4}b^4 + \dots \right] \\ (a + b)^n - (a - b)^n &= 2 \left[ {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1}b + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 a^{n-3}b^3 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_5 a^{n-5}b^5 + \dots \right] \end{aligned} $$

Number of Terms in $(a + b)^n \pm (a - b)^n$:

  • If $n$ is even:
  • $(a + b)^n + (a - b)^n$ has $\frac{n}{2} + 1$ terms.
  • $(a + b)^n - (a - b)^n$ has $\frac{n}{2}$ terms.
  • If $n$ is odd:
  • Both $(a + b)^n + (a - b)^n$ and $(a + b)^n - (a - b)^n$ have $\frac{n + 1}{2}$ terms.

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Foundations

1.1 The Binomial Expansion Statement

NCERT Theorem Statement:
For any positive integer $n$ and any real or complex numbers $a$ and $b$:

$$ (a + b)^n = \binom{n}{0} a^n b^0 + \binom{n}{1} a^{n-1} b^1 + \binom{n}{2} a^{n-2} b^2 + \dots + \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r + \dots + \binom{n}{n} a^0 b^n $$

Using compact sigma summation notation:

$$ (a + b)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r $$

where:

$$ \binom{n}{r} = {}^nC_r = \frac{n!}{r!(n - r)!}, \quad 0 \le r \le n, \quad r \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0} $$

1.2 Core Anatomical Properties of $(a + b)^n$

  1. Total Number of Terms:
    The index ranges over $r = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n$. Hence, there are exactly $(n + 1)$ terms in the expansion.

  2. Sum of Exponents (Degree Homogeneity):
    In every individual term $\binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r$, the sum of the exponents of $a$ and $b$ is:

$$ (n - r) + r = n $$

Every term is homogeneous of degree $n$ with respect to the variables $a$ and $b$.

  1. Symmetry of Binomial Coefficients:
    Using the fundamental identity $\binom{n}{r} = \binom{n}{n-r}$, the coefficients of terms equidistant from the beginning and the end are equal:

$$ \binom{n}{0} = \binom{n}{n}, \quad \binom{n}{1} = \binom{n}{n-1}, \quad \binom{n}{2} = \binom{n}{n-2}, \quad \dots $$

2. Rigorous Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: Derivation of the General Term ($T_{r+1}$) Formula

$$ \text{Priority: } \star\star\star\star\star $$

1. Statement

In the binomial expansion of $(a + b)^n$, where $n \in \mathbb{N}$, the $(r+1)^{\text{th}}$ term from the beginning, denoted by $T_{r+1}$, is given by:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r = {}^nC_r a^{n-r} b^r $$

2. Derivation by Term Enumeration and Mathematical Induction

Write out the sequential terms by expanding $(a + b)^n$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{1st term } (r = 0): \quad & T_1 = T_{0+1} = \binom{n}{0} a^{n-0} b^0 = \binom{n}{0} a^n \\ \text{2nd term } (r = 1): \quad & T_2 = T_{1+1} = \binom{n}{1} a^{n-1} b^1 \\ \text{3rd term } (r = 2): \quad & T_3 = T_{2+1} = \binom{n}{2} a^{n-2} b^2 \\ \text{4th term } (r = 3): \quad & T_4 = T_{3+1} = \binom{n}{3} a^{n-3} b^3 \\ & \quad \vdots \\ k^{\text{th}}\text{ term } (r = k - 1): \quad & T_k = T_{(k-1)+1} = \binom{n}{k-1} a^{n-(k-1)} b^{k-1} \end{aligned} $$

Setting $k - 1 = r \implies k = r + 1$:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r $$

3. Formal Verification via the Principle of Mathematical Induction (PMI) on $n$

Base Case ($n = 1$):

$$ (a + b)^1 = \binom{1}{0} a^1 b^0 + \binom{1}{1} a^0 b^1 = T_1 + T_2 $$

  • For $r = 0$: $T_{0+1} = \binom{1}{0} a^{1-0} b^0 = a$, which matches the 1st term.
  • For $r = 1$: $T_{1+1} = \binom{1}{1} a^{1-1} b^1 = b$, which matches the 2nd term.
    The formula holds for $n = 1$.

Inductive Hypothesis:
Assume the theorem holds for a positive integer $m$:

$$ (a + b)^m = \sum_{r=0}^{m} \binom{m}{r} a^{m-r} b^r $$

Inductive Step ($n = m + 1$):

$$ \begin{aligned} (a + b)^{m+1} &= (a + b)(a + b)^m \\ &= (a + b) \sum_{r=0}^{m} \binom{m}{r} a^{m-r} b^r \\ &= a \sum_{r=0}^{m} \binom{m}{r} a^{m-r} b^r + b \sum_{r=0}^{m} \binom{m}{r} a^{m-r} b^r \\ &= \sum_{r=0}^{m} \binom{m}{r} a^{m-r+1} b^r + \sum_{r=0}^{m} \binom{m}{r} a^{m-r} b^{r+1} \end{aligned} $$

Shift index in the second sum by substituting $j = r + 1 \implies r = j - 1$:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^{m} \binom{m}{r} a^{m-r} b^{r+1} = \sum_{j=1}^{m+1} \binom{m}{j-1} a^{m-(j-1)} b^j = \sum_{r=1}^{m+1} \binom{m}{r-1} a^{m-r+1} b^r $$

Separate boundary terms ($r = 0$ from the first sum and $r = m + 1$ from the second sum):

$$ (a + b)^{m+1} = \binom{m}{0} a^{m+1} b^0 + \sum_{r=1}^{m} \left[ \binom{m}{r} + \binom{m}{r-1} \right] a^{m+1-r} b^r + \binom{m}{m} a^0 b^{m+1} $$

Using Pascal's Identity $\binom{m}{r} + \binom{m}{r-1} = \binom{m+1}{r}$, and noting $\binom{m}{0} = \binom{m+1}{0} = 1$ and $\binom{m}{m} = \binom{m+1}{m+1} = 1$:

$$ (a + b)^{m+1} = \binom{m+1}{0} a^{m+1} b^0 + \sum_{r=1}^{m} \binom{m+1}{r} a^{(m+1)-r} b^r + \binom{m+1}{m+1} a^0 b^{m+1} $$

$$ (a + b)^{m+1} = \sum_{r=0}^{m+1} \binom{m+1}{r} a^{(m+1)-r} b^r $$

The general term for power $m+1$ is identically:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{m+1}{r} a^{(m+1)-r} b^r $$

By the Principle of Mathematical Induction, the formula is valid for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Middle Term(s) Characterization

$$ \text{Priority: } \star\star\star\star\star $$

Case 1: When $n$ is EVEN ($n = 2m, \; m \in \mathbb{N}$)

  • Total number of terms: $N = n + 1 = 2m + 1$ (an odd number).
  • An odd collection of numbers $1, 2, 3, \dots, 2m+1$ has exactly one unique central element.
  • Position of the Middle Term:

$$ \text{Position} = \frac{(n + 1) + 1}{2} = \frac{n + 2}{2} = \left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right)^{\text{th}} \text{ term} $$

  • Value of the Middle Term ($T_{\frac{n}{2} + 1}$):
    Substitute $r = \frac{n}{2}$ into the general term formula:

$$ T_{\frac{n}{2} + 1} = \binom{n}{n/2} a^{n - n/2} b^{n/2} = \binom{n}{n/2} a^{n/2} b^{n/2} $$

Case 2: When $n$ is ODD ($n = 2m + 1, \; m \in \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$)

  • Total number of terms: $N = n + 1 = (2m + 1) + 1 = 2m + 2$ (an even number).
  • An even collection of numbers $1, 2, 3, \dots, 2m+2$ has exactly two middle terms.
  • Positions of the Middle Terms:

$$ \text{First Middle Term Position} = \frac{N}{2} = \frac{n + 1}{2} = \left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{ term} $$

$$ \text{Second Middle Term Position} = \frac{N}{2} + 1 = \frac{n + 1}{2} + 1 = \left(\frac{n + 3}{2}\right)^{\text{th}} \text{ term} $$

  • Values of the Middle Terms:
  • For the $\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)^{\text{th}}$ term, set $r + 1 = \frac{n+1}{2} \implies r = \frac{n-1}{2}$:

$$ T_{\frac{n+1}{2}} = \binom{n}{\frac{n-1}{2}} a^{n - \frac{n-1}{2}} b^{\frac{n-1}{2}} = \binom{n}{\frac{n-1}{2}} a^{\frac{n+1}{2}} b^{\frac{n-1}{2}} $$

  • For the $\left(\frac{n+3}{2}\right)^{\text{th}}$ term, set $r + 1 = \frac{n+3}{2} \implies r = \frac{n+1}{2}$:

$$ T_{\frac{n+3}{2}} = \binom{n}{\frac{n+1}{2}} a^{n - \frac{n+1}{2}} b^{\frac{n+1}{2}} = \binom{n}{\frac{n+1}{2}} a^{\frac{n-1}{2}} b^{\frac{n+1}{2}} $$

Notice that the binomial coefficients of these two middle terms are identical because:

$$ \binom{n}{\frac{n-1}{2}} = \binom{n}{n - \frac{n-1}{2}} = \binom{n}{\frac{n+1}{2}} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: Term from the End to Term from the Beginning Relation

$$ \text{Priority: } \star\star\star\star $$

Theorem:

In the expansion of $(a + b)^n$, the $p^{\text{th}}$ term from the end is equal to the $(n - p + 2)^{\text{th}}$ term from the beginning.

Proof:

Total number of terms $= n + 1$.
Let the sequence of terms from the beginning be:

$$ T_1, T_2, T_3, \dots, T_k, \dots, T_{n}, T_{n+1} $$

If a term is at position $p$ counting backwards from $T_{n+1}$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{1st from end} &= T_{n+1} = T_{(n+1) - 1 + 1} \\ \text{2nd from end} &= T_n = T_{(n+1) - 2 + 1} \\ \text{3rd from end} &= T_{n-1} = T_{(n+1) - 3 + 1} \\ & \quad \vdots \\ p^{\text{th}}\text{ from end} &= T_{(n+1) - p + 1} = T_{n - p + 2} \end{aligned} $$

Alternatively: Expanding $(a + b)^n$ from the end is mathematically identical to expanding $(b + a)^n$ from the beginning.
The $p^{\text{th}}$ term of $(b + a)^n$ is:

$$ T_p' = \binom{n}{p-1} b^{n - (p - 1)} a^{p - 1} = \binom{n}{p-1} a^{p-1} b^{n - p + 1} $$

Using $\binom{n}{p-1} = \binom{n}{n - (p - 1)} = \binom{n}{n - p + 1}$:

$$ T_p' = \binom{n}{n - p + 1} a^{(n - (n - p + 1))} b^{n - p + 1} = T_{(n - p + 1) + 1} = T_{n - p + 2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Finding Specific Coefficients and Term Independent of x

MASTER LECTURE NOTES: BINOMIAL THEOREM (CLASS 11)

Topic: Finding Specific Coefficients and Term Independent of $x$

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 The General Term Formulation (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Board Priority)

In the expansion of a binomial expression raised to a non-negative integral power, every term can be generated sequentially by varying an integer parameter $r$.

Statement of the Binomial Theorem for Positive Integral Index

Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ (or $\mathbb{C}$). Then:

$$ (a + b)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r = \binom{n}{0} a^n + \binom{n}{1} a^{n-1}b + \binom{n}{2} a^{n-2}b^2 + \dots + \binom{n}{n} b^n $$

Definition: General Term ($T_{r+1}$)

The term indexed by $(r+1)$ in the expansion of $(a + b)^n$, counting from the left (beginning), is termed the General Term, denoted by $T_{r+1}$:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r $$

  • Index Shift Intuition: The first term corresponds to $r = 0$ ($T_1 = \binom{n}{0}a^n b^0$). The second term corresponds to $r = 1$ ($T_2 = \binom{n}{1}a^{n-1} b^1$). Hence, the $k^{\text{th}}$ term has $r = k - 1$. This $0$-based counting shift is the single most common source of indexing errors.

1.2 Core Analytical Model: Finding Specific Powers of $x$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Board Priority)

Consider the standard rational binomial prototype:

$$ \left( p x^\alpha + \frac{q}{x^\beta} \right)^n, \quad p, q \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}, \; \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{Q}^+, \; n \in \mathbb{N} $$

To isolate the coefficient of a prescribed power $x^m$ ($m \in \mathbb{Z}$): 1. Express $T_{r+1}$ in decomposed form: separate numerical/constant factors from algebraic variable powers. 2. Collect exponents of $x$ using index laws: $x^A / x^B = x^{A-B}$, $(x^A)^B = x^{AB}$. 3. Equate the total net exponent of $x$ to $m$. 4. Solve for $r$. 5. Admissibility Check: The solution for $r$ must satisfy:

$$ r \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \text{and} \quad 0 \le r \le n $$

If $r \notin \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\}$, then the term containing $x^m$ does not exist in the expansion, and its coefficient is strictly $0$.

1.3 The Term Independent of $x$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Board Priority)

Definition

The term independent of $x$ (also known as the constant term or the term free from $x$) is the particular term in the expansion where the net exponent of the variable $x$ evaluates to zero ($x^0$).

Analytical Condition

Setting $m = 0$ in the net exponent equation gives:

$$ \text{Exponent of } x \text{ in } T_{r+1} = 0 $$

1.4 Geometric & Combinatorial Interpretation (⭐⭐⭐⭐ Board Priority)

Combinatorially, the expansion:

$$ (a + b)^n = \underbrace{(a + b)(a + b)\cdots(a + b)}_{n \text{ factors}} $$

is formed by choosing either $a$ or $b$ from each of the $n$ distinct parentheses: * Choosing $b$ exactly $r$ times automatically forces choosing $a$ exactly $(n - r)$ times. * The number of distinct ways to select $r$ factors of $b$ from $n$ distinct linear brackets is given by the combination formula $\binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$. * When $a = p x^\alpha$ and $b = q x^{-\beta}$, choosing $b$ adds a contribution of $-\beta$ to the net degree, while choosing $a$ adds $+\alpha$. Finding the term independent of $x$ is equivalent to finding a combination of selections such that:

$$ (n - r)\alpha + r(-\beta) = 0 \implies \text{Net degree contribution balances to zero.} $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: Explicit Formula for $r$ in $\left(ax^\alpha + \frac{b}{x^\beta}\right)^n$ for $x^m$

Given: A binomial expansion $E = \left(ax^\alpha + \frac{b}{x^\beta}\right)^n$, where $a, b \neq 0$, $\alpha, \beta > 0$, and $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

To Prove: The term containing $x^m$ occurs at the index parameter $r$ given by:

$$ r = \frac{n\alpha - m}{\alpha + \beta} $$

provided $r \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $0 \le r \le n$. In particular, the term independent of $x$ ($m = 0$) occurs at:

$$ r = \frac{n\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} $$

Derivation: 1. Write the general term $T_{r+1}$ using the binomial formula:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} (ax^\alpha)^{n-r} \left(\frac{b}{x^\beta}\right)^r $$

  1. Distribute powers over products and quotients:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} \left(a^{n-r} \cdot (x^\alpha)^{n-r}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{b^r}{(x^\beta)^r}\right) $$

  1. Apply exponent multiplication rules:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r \cdot x^{\alpha(n-r)} \cdot x^{-\beta r} $$

  1. Combine exponents of base $x$:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r \cdot x^{\alpha n - \alpha r - \beta r} = \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r \cdot x^{n\alpha - r(\alpha + \beta)} $$

  1. To obtain the coefficient of $x^m$, equate the exponent of $x$ to $m$:

$$ n\alpha - r(\alpha + \beta) = m $$

  1. Solve for $r$:

$$ n\alpha - m = r(\alpha + \beta) $$

$$ r = \frac{n\alpha - m}{\alpha + \beta} $$

  1. For the term independent of $x$, substitute $m = 0$:

$$ r = \frac{n\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Existence Condition for the Constant Term

Given: The term independent of $x$ in $\left(ax^\alpha + \frac{b}{x^\beta}\right)^n$.

To Prove: A non-zero constant term exists if and only if $(\alpha + \beta)$ divides $n\alpha$ and $\frac{n\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} \le n$.

Derivation: 1. From Proof 2.1, $r = \frac{n\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}$. 2. In the binomial series expansion, $r$ represents the number of selections of the second term from $n$ distinct factors. By definition of $\binom{n}{r}$:

$$ r \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\} $$

  1. Therefore, $r$ must be an integer:

$$ \frac{n\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} \in \mathbb{Z}^+ \cup \{0\} \iff (\alpha + \beta) \mid n\alpha $$

  1. Furthermore:

$$ \alpha > 0, \beta > 0 \implies \frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta} < 1 \implies r = n\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha + \beta}\right) < n $$

Hence, the condition $r \le n$ is unconditionally satisfied whenever $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Thus, the sole necessary and sufficient criterion is the integrality of $r$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: Expansion with Alternating Negative Terms $(a - b)^n$

Given:

$$ (a - b)^n = [a + (-b)]^n $$

To Prove:

$$ T_{r+1} = (-1)^r \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r $$

Derivation: 1. Set $X = a$ and $Y = -b$ in the standard general term:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} X^{n-r} Y^r $$

  1. Substitute $Y = (-1 \cdot b)$:

$$ Y^r = (-1 \cdot b)^r = (-1)^r b^r $$

  1. Substituting back yields:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} (-1)^r b^r = (-1)^r \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Applications of Binomial Theorem in Divisibility Problems and Value Approximations

Advanced Teaching Notes: Applications of the Binomial Theorem

Target Level: CBSE Class 11 Mathematics / JEE Main & Advanced
Subtopic: Divisibility Problems, Remainder Determination & Value Approximations
Prerequisite Knowledge: Binomial Theorem for Positive Integral Indices, Factorial Notation, Combination Notation $\binom{n}{r} = {}^n\mathrm{C}_r$.

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Core Theorem Statement (NCERT Baseline)

For any positive integer $n$ and real numbers $a$ and $b$:

$$ (a + b)^n = \sum_{r=0}^{n} {}^n\mathrm{C}_r a^{n-r} b^r = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 a^n + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 a^{n-1} b + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 a^{n-2} b^2 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n b^n $$

When $a = 1$ and $b = x$:

$$ (1 + x)^n = 1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 x + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n $$

1.2 Topic 1: Divisibility Problems & Remainder Theory

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Board & JEE Main Frequency)

Mathematical Intuition

In standard modular arithmetic, to prove that an expression $E(n)$ is divisible by an integer $m$, we show:

$$ E(n) = m \cdot k \quad \text{where } k \in \mathbb{Z} $$

The Binomial Theorem provides an algebraic framework to isolate multiples of any chosen base. By rewriting a base number $A$ in terms of the divisor $m$ (or a sub-factor of $m$), such that:

$$ A = 1 + m \quad \text{or} \quad A = 1 + k\cdot m \quad \text{or} \quad A = m - 1 $$

expanding $A^n$ allows us to collect every term containing $m^2, m^3, \dots$ into a single composite multiple $m \cdot Q$, isolating the low-order boundary terms.

The Factorization Principle

Consider $(1 + x)^n$:

$$ (1 + x)^n - 1 - n x = {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n = x^2 \left[ {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^{n-2} \right] $$

Since $n \ge 2$ and each combination coefficient ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r$ is an integer, the expression inside the bracket is an integer $k$. Hence:

$$ (1 + x)^n - nx - 1 \text{ is strictly divisible by } x^2 \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{N}, n \ge 2 $$

1.3 Topic 2: Value Approximations

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (NCERT Exercises & Competitive Foundations)

Mathematical Intuition

When $|x| \ll 1$ (i.e., $x$ is significantly smaller than $1$), higher powers $x^2, x^3, x^4, \dots$ rapidly diminish toward zero:

$$ |x| > x^2 > |x^3| > x^4 \dots $$

Therefore: - First-order linear approximation: $(1 + x)^n \approx 1 + nx$ - Second-order quadratic approximation: $(1 + x)^n \approx 1 + nx + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} x^2$

For decimal powers, numbers such as $(1.01)^{10000}$ or $(0.99)^n$ can be decomposed into $(1 \pm 10^{-k})^n$. By evaluating up to a specific power $10^{-m}$, we achieve precision to $m$ decimal places without calculating the full expansion.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs

Proof 2.1: The Fundamental Divisibility Theorem

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem Statement

Prove that for all integers $n \in \mathbb{N}$ ($n \ge 2$) and $x > -1, x \neq 0$:

$$ (1 + x)^n - nx - 1 \text{ is divisible by } x^2 $$

Formal Derivation

  • Given: $n \in \mathbb{N}, n \ge 2$.
  • To Prove: $\frac{(1 + x)^n - nx - 1}{x^2} = k$, where $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ (provided $x \in \mathbb{Z}$).

Step 1: Write the full binomial expansion for $(1 + x)^n$:

$$ (1 + x)^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 x + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n $$

Step 2: Substitute known values ${}^n\mathrm{C}_0 = 1$ and ${}^n\mathrm{C}_1 = n$:

$$ (1 + x)^n = 1 + nx + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n $$

Step 3: Subtract $(1 + nx)$ from both sides:

$$ (1 + x)^n - 1 - nx = {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 x^2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x^3 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^n $$

Step 4: Factor out $x^2$ from the right-hand side:

$$ (1 + x)^n - nx - 1 = x^2 \left[ {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x + {}^n\mathrm{C}_4 x^2 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^{n-2} \right] $$

Step 5: Define the expression within brackets as $k$:

$$ k = {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 x + {}^n\mathrm{C}_4 x^2 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n x^{n-2} $$

Since $n, r \in \mathbb{Z}^+$, each combination ${}^n\mathrm{C}_r = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!} \in \mathbb{Z}$.
If $x \in \mathbb{Z}$, then $k$ is a finite sum of products of integers, which implies $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

$$ (1 + x)^n - nx - 1 = k \cdot x^2 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Classical NCERT Identity: $9^{n+1} - 8n - 9$ is Divisible by $64$

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem Formulation

Prove by Binomial Theorem that $9^{n+1} - 8n - 9$ is divisible by $64$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

Step-by-Step Algebraic Manipulation

  • Given: $E(n) = 9^{n+1} - 8n - 9$, where $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
  • To Prove: $E(n) = 64 \cdot \lambda$, where $\lambda \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Step 1: Express the base $9$ in terms of the target divisor $64 = 8^2$:

$$ 9 = 1 + 8 $$

Thus:

$$ 9^{n+1} = (1 + 8)^{n+1} $$

Step 2: Apply the Binomial Theorem to $(1 + 8)^{n+1}$:

$$ (1 + 8)^{n+1} = {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_1 (8) + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_2 (8^2) + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_3 (8^3) + \dots + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_{n+1} (8^{n+1}) $$

Step 3: Evaluate the first two boundary coefficients: - ${}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_0 = 1$ - ${}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_1 = n + 1$

Substitute these values:

$$ 9^{n+1} = 1 + (n + 1) \cdot 8 + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_2 (64) + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_3 (8^3) + \dots + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_{n+1} (8^{n+1}) $$

Step 4: Simplify the linear terms:

$$ 9^{n+1} = 1 + 8n + 8 + 64 \left[ {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_3 (8) + \dots + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_{n+1} (8^{n-1}) \right] $$

$$ 9^{n+1} = 9 + 8n + 64 \lambda $$

where:

$$ \lambda = {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_3 (8) + \dots + {}^{n+1}\mathrm{C}_{n+1} (8^{n-1}) \in \mathbb{Z} $$

Step 5: Rearrange terms to match $E(n)$:

$$ 9^{n+1} - 8n - 9 = 64 \lambda $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Problem: Expand $\left( 2x - \frac{y}{2} \right)^5$ completely.
(CBSE Class 11 — 3 Marks)

Step-by-Step Solution:
Here $a = 2x$, $b = -\frac{y}{2}$, and $n = 5$.
Using the binomial expansion:

$$ \left( 2x - \frac{y}{2} \right)^5 = \sum_{r=0}^{5} {}^5\mathrm{C}_r (2x)^{5-r} \left( -\frac{y}{2} \right)^r $$

Write all 6 terms ($r = 0$ to $5$):

$$ \begin{aligned} T_1 &= {}^5\mathrm{C}_0 (2x)^5 \left(-\frac{y}{2}\right)^0 = 1 \cdot (32x^5) \cdot 1 = 32x^5 \\ T_2 &= {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 (2x)^4 \left(-\frac{y}{2}\right)^1 = 5 \cdot (16x^4) \left(-\frac{y}{2}\right) = -40x^4 y \\ T_3 &= {}^5\mathrm{C}_2 (2x)^3 \left(-\frac{y}{2}\right)^2 = 10 \cdot (8x^3) \left(\frac{y^2}{4}\right) = 20x^3 y^2 \\ T_4 &= {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 (2x)^2 \left(-\frac{y}{2}\right)^3 = 10 \cdot (4x^2) \left(-\frac{y^3}{8}\right) = -5x^2 y^3 \\ T_5 &= {}^5\mathrm{C}_4 (2x)^1 \left(-\frac{y}{2}\right)^4 = 5 \cdot (2x) \left(\frac{y^4}{16}\right) = \frac{5}{8} x y^4 \\ T_6 &= {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 (2x)^0 \left(-\frac{y}{2}\right)^5 = 1 \cdot 1 \cdot \left(-\frac{y^5}{32}\right) = -\frac{y^5}{32} \end{aligned} $$

Summing all components:

$$ \left( 2x - \frac{y}{2} \right)^5 = 32x^5 - 40x^4 y + 20x^3 y^2 - 5x^2 y^3 + \frac{5}{8} x y^4 - \frac{1}{32} y^5 $$

💡 Example 2: Radical Simplification (NCERT Exemplar)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Simplify $(\sqrt{3} + \sqrt{2})^6 - (\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{2})^6$.
(CBSE / Competition High-Frequency — 4 Marks)

Step-by-Step Solution:
Let $a = \sqrt{3}$ and $b = \sqrt{2}$, with index $n = 6$ (even).
Using the identity:

$$ (a + b)^6 - (a - b)^6 = 2 \left[ {}^6\mathrm{C}_1 a^5 b + {}^6\mathrm{C}_3 a^3 b^3 + {}^6\mathrm{C}_5 a b^5 \right] $$

Calculate binomial coefficients:

$$ {}^6\mathrm{C}_1 = 6, \quad {}^6\mathrm{C}_3 = \frac{6 \times 5 \times 4}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = 20, \quad {}^6\mathrm{C}_5 = 6 $$

Compute the radical factors:

3. Parity of Coefficients (Odd vs. Even Subsets) (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Setting $x = 1$ in $(1 + x)^n$:

$$ {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n = 2^n $$

Setting $x = -1$ in $(1 + x)^n$:

$$ {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 - {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 + \dots = 0 $$

$$ \implies {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_4 + \dots = {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_5 + \dots = \frac{2^n}{2} = 2^{n-1} $$

For small $x \ll 1$:

$$ (1 + x)^n \approx 1 + n x + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2} x^2 $$

Example: $(1.002)^{10} \approx 1 + 10(0.002) + \frac{10(9)}{2}(0.002)^2 = 1 + 0.02 + 45(0.000004) = 1.02018$.

find: 1. The coefficient of $x^8$ 2. The term independent of $x$ (the constant term)

Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Scheme:

Step 1: Write down the General Term $T_{r+1}$ with signs kept intact.
Here, $a = 2x^2$, $b = -\frac{1}{3x}$, and $n = 10$.

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{10}{r} (2x^2)^{10-r} \left(-\frac{1}{3x}\right)^r \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

Step 2: Isolate the purely numerical coefficient from the variable powers of $x$.

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{10}{r} \cdot 2^{10-r} \cdot (x^2)^{10-r} \cdot (-1)^r \cdot 3^{-r} \cdot x^{-r} $$

$$ \text{Coefficient of } x^8 = (-1)^4 \binom{10}{4} \frac{2^{10-4}}{3^4} = (1) \binom{10}{4} \frac{2^6}{3^4} $$

$$ \mathbf{\text{Ans (1): } \frac{4480}{27}} \quad \text{[1.5 Marks]} $$

$$ 20 - 3r = 0 \implies 3r = 20 \implies r = \frac{20}{3} $$

Since $r \notin \mathbb{Z}_{\ge 0}$ (i.e., $r$ is not an integer), there is no such value of $r$ that makes the power of $x$ zero.

Conclusion:

Problem (NCERT Exemplar): Find the middle term(s) in the expansion of:

$$ \left(\frac{p}{x} + \frac{x}{p}\right)^9, \quad p \ne 0, \; x \ne 0 $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Identify $n$ and determine the number of middle terms.
Here, $n = 9$, which is an odd integer.
Total number of terms $= n + 1 = 9 + 1 = 10$.
Since the number of terms is even, there are two middle terms:

$$ \text{Positions} = \frac{9 + 1}{2} = 5^{\text{th}} \text{ term} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{9 + 3}{2} = 6^{\text{th}} \text{ term} \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

$$ \binom{9}{4} = \frac{9 \times 8 \times 7 \times 6}{4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1} = 126 $$

$$ T_5 = 126 \left(\frac{p}{x}\right) \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

$$ T_6 = \binom{9}{5} \left(\frac{x}{p}\right) $$

$$ T_6 = 126 \left(\frac{x}{p}\right) \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

💡 Example 3.3: Proof Problem on Middle Term of $(1 + x)^{2n}$
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem (NCERT Standard 5-Mark Question):
Prove that the middle term in the expansion of $(1 + x)^{2n}$ is:

$$ \frac{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2n - 1)}{n!} 2^n x^n, \quad \text{where } n \in \mathbb{N} $$

Complete Mathematical Proof:

Step 1: Identify the index and middle term position.
The index of the expansion is $2n$, which is an even integer for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
The expansion contains $(2n + 1)$ terms.
Hence, there is only one middle term, located at:

$$ \text{Position} = \frac{2n}{2} + 1 = (n + 1)^{\text{th}} \text{ term} \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

Step 2: Apply the General Term formula.
In $(1 + x)^{2n}$, $a = 1$, $b = x$, and the power is $2n$.

$$ T_{n+1} = \binom{2n}{n} (1)^{2n-n} x^n = \binom{2n}{n} x^n \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

Step 3: Expand the factorial definition of $\binom{2n}{n}$.

$$ \binom{2n}{n} = \frac{(2n)!}{n!(2n - n)!} = \frac{(2n)!}{n! \, n!} $$

Write out $(2n)!$ as the product of integers from $1$ to $2n$:

$$ (2n)! = 1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 \cdots (2n - 1) \cdot (2n) \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

Step 4: Regroup into odd and even factors.

$$ (2n)! = [1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2n - 1)] \times [2 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdots (2n)] $$

Factor out $2$ from each of the $n$ even terms:

$$ [2 \cdot 4 \cdot 6 \cdots (2n)] = (2 \cdot 1)(2 \cdot 2)(2 \cdot 3)\cdots(2 \cdot n) = 2^n [1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots n] = 2^n (n!) \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

Step 5: Substitute back into $\binom{2n}{n}$.

$$ \binom{2n}{n} = \frac{[1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2n - 1)] \cdot 2^n (n!)}{n! \, n!} $$

Cancel $n!$ from numerator and denominator:

$$ \binom{2n}{n} = \frac{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2n - 1)}{n!} 2^n $$

Multiply by $x^n$ to get $T_{n+1}$:

$$ T_{n+1} = \frac{1 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdots (2n - 1)}{n!} 2^n x^n $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{18}{r} \left(x^{1/3}\right)^{18-r} \left(\frac{1}{2} x^{-1/3}\right)^r $$

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{18}{r} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^r x^{\frac{18 - 2r}{3}} $$

Step 3: Impose the Condition for Independence from $x$ Exponent of $x = 0$:

$$ \frac{18 - 2r}{3} = 0 \implies 18 - 2r = 0 \implies r = 9 $$

Step 4: Verification and Computation Since $r = 9 \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 18\}$, the term exists and is $T_{10}$:

$$ T_{10} = \binom{18}{9} \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^9 = \frac{18!}{9! \, 9!} \cdot \frac{1}{512} = \frac{48620}{512} = \frac{24310}{256} = \frac{12155}{128} $$

💡 Example 3: Non-Existence of a Specific Term (Conceptual Rigor)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Show that the expansion of $\left(2x^2 - \frac{1}{x}\right)^{10}$ contains no term involving $x^3$.

Detailed Solution:

Step 1: Write $T_{r+1}$

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{10}{r} (2x^2)^{10-r} \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^r = (-1)^r \binom{10}{r} 2^{10-r} x^{20-2r} x^{-r} = (-1)^r \binom{10}{r} 2^{10-r} x^{20-3r} $$

Step 2: Equate Power of $x$ to $3$

$$ 20 - 3r = 3 \implies 3r = 17 \implies r = \frac{17}{3} $$

Step 3: Analyze Validity of $r$

$$ \frac{17}{3} \notin \mathbb{Z} $$

Because $r$ represents the number of selections, it must be an integer ($r \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, 10\}$). Since no integral value of $r$ satisfies the condition:

$$ \text{The expansion does not contain any term with } x^3. $$

$$ \text{Consequently, the coefficient of } x^3 \text{ is } 0. $$

💡 Example 4: Product of Binomial and Multinomial Factors (Advanced Board / Competitive)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the coefficient of $x^4$ in the expansion of $(1 + 2x + x^2) \left(x - \frac{1}{x}\right)^{10}$.

Detailed Solution:

Step 1: Simplify the Premultiplying Expression Notice that $1 + 2x + x^2 = (1 + x)^2$. However, retaining it in distributed polynomial form is easier:

$$ P(x) = (1 + 2x + x^2) \cdot \sum_{r=0}^{10} \binom{10}{r} x^{10-r} \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)^r $$

Let $E(x) = \left(x - \frac{1}{x}\right)^{10}$. The general term of $E(x)$ is:

$$ t_{r+1} = (-1)^r \binom{10}{r} x^{10-2r} $$

Step 2: Expand the Product

$$ \text{Product} = 1 \cdot E(x) + 2x \cdot E(x) + x^2 \cdot E(x) $$

To get $x^4$ overall: 1. From $1 \cdot E(x)$: Need coefficient of $x^4$ in $E(x)$. 2. From $2x \cdot E(x)$: Need $2 \times (\text{coefficient of } x^3 \text{ in } E(x))$. 3. From $x^2 \cdot E(x)$: Need coefficient of $x^2$ in $E(x)$.

Step 3: Analyze Each Case via $10 - 2r$ * Case I: $10 - 2r = 4 \implies 2r = 6 \implies r = 3$.

$$ \text{Contribution}_1 = 1 \times \left[(-1)^3 \binom{10}{3}\right] = - \frac{10 \times 9 \times 8}{3 \times 2 \times 1} = -120 $$

  • Case II: $10 - 2r = 3 \implies 2r = 7 \implies r = \frac{7}{2} \notin \mathbb{Z}$.

$$ \text{Contribution}_2 = 0 $$

  • Case III: $10 - 2r = 2 \implies 2r = 8 \implies r = 4$.

$$ \text{Contribution}_3 = 1 \times \left[(-1)^4 \binom{10}{4}\right] = + \frac{10 \times 9 \times 8 \times 7}{4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1} = +210 $$

Step 4: Sum Contributions

$$ \text{Total Coefficient of } x^4 = -120 + 0 + 210 = 90 $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Trap 4.1: The Negative Sign Neglect in $(-b)^r$

  • Error: Writing $\left(2x - \frac{3}{x}\right)^n$ as having general term $\binom{n}{r} (2x)^{n-r} \left(\frac{3}{x}\right)^r$, completely dropping the minus sign.
  • Correction: ALWAYS separate the negative sign immediately as $(-1)^r$:

$$ T_{r+1} = (-1)^r \binom{n}{r} (2x)^{n-r} \left(\frac{3}{x}\right)^r $$

An odd value of $r$ will flip the sign of the entire coefficient.

Trap 4.2: Confusing "Coefficient", "Absolute Term", and "Term"

Term Requested Mathematical Meaning Example for $T_5 = -120 x^4$
"The Term" The complete mathematical entity including the variable $-120 x^4$
"Coefficient" The numerical/constant factor multiplying the variable $-120$
"Term Independent of $x$" The entire term evaluated at $x^0$ (just the constant value) A pure number, e.g., $45$

Trap 4.3: Term Number vs. Index $r$

  • Error: Finding $r = 5$ and writing "The fifth term is independent of $x$."
  • Correction: The term is $T_{r+1}$. If $r = 5$, it is the $6^{\text{th}}$ term ($T_6$).

Trap 4.4: Incomplete Exponent Distribution over Multi-Factor Bases

  • Error: Writing $\left(\frac{x}{3}\right)^{n-r}$ as $\frac{x^{n-r}}{3}$.
  • Correction: Power distributes to both numerator and denominator: $\frac{x^{n-r}}{3^{n-r}} = 3^{-(n-r)} x^{n-r}$.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Competitive Exams (JEE / CET)

Golden Shortcut Formula 5.1: The Direct Index Formula

For any expression of the form:

$$ \left(a x^p + \frac{b}{x^q}\right)^n $$

  • Index $r$ for $x^m$:

$$ r = \frac{n p - m}{p + q} $$

  • Index $r$ for Term Independent of $x$ ($m=0$):

$$ r = \frac{n p}{p + q} $$

Flash Application: Find the term independent of $x$ in $\left(x^2 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^9$. Here $n=9, p=2, q=1$.

$$ r = \frac{9(2)}{2 + 1} = \frac{18}{3} = 6 \implies \text{Answer is } T_7 = \binom{9}{6} = 84 $$

Solve time: < 10 seconds.

Golden Shortcut Formula 5.2: Parity Check for Symmetrical Reciprocals

In $\left(x + \frac{1}{x}\right)^n$: * Here $p = 1, q = 1 \implies r = \frac{n}{2}$. * If $n$ is odd, there is NO term independent of $x$ (since $r = \frac{n}{2} \notin \mathbb{Z}$). * If $n$ is even, the term independent of $x$ is always the central/middle term:

$$ T_{\frac{n}{2} + 1} = \binom{n}{n/2} $$

Golden Point 5.3: Ratio of Consecutive Coefficients

To locate terms without expanding fully, use the consecutive term ratio:

$$ \frac{T_{r+1}}{T_r} = \frac{\binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r}{\binom{n}{r-1} a^{n-r+1} b^{r-1}} = \left(\frac{n - r + 1}{r}\right) \cdot \frac{b}{a} $$

This eliminates full factorial expansions and accelerates verification of algebraic reductions.

Quick Self-Diagnostic Checklist for Students

Before submitting any board problem on this topic, ensure you have: 1. Isolated $(-1)^r$ upfront so it is not lost. 2. Verified that the solved $r$ is an integer in the interval $[0, n]$. 3. Explicitly computed $\binom{n}{r}$ and all constant powers $a^{n-r} b^r$. 4. Clarified whether the question asked for the term (includes $x$) or just the coefficient (pure constant).

💡 Example 3.1: Finding the Remainder
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the remainder when $6^n - 5n$ is divided by $25$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Solution:

Write the base $6$ as $(1 + 5)$:

$$ 6^n = (1 + 5)^n $$

Expanding using the Binomial Theorem:

$$ 6^n = {}^n\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_1 (5) + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 (5^2) + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (5^3) + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5^n) $$

Substitute ${}^n\mathrm{C}_0 = 1$ and ${}^n\mathrm{C}_1 = n$:

$$ 6^n = 1 + 5n + {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 (25) + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (125) + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5^n) $$

Subtract $5n$ from both sides:

$$ 6^n - 5n = 1 + 25 \left[ {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (5) + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5^{n-2}) \right] $$

Let $k = {}^n\mathrm{C}_2 + {}^n\mathrm{C}_3 (5) + \dots + {}^n\mathrm{C}_n (5^{n-2})$. Since $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $n \ge 2$, $k$ is an integer.

$$ 6^n - 5n = 25k + 1 $$

By Euclid’s Division Lemma ($A = Bq + R$, with $0 \le R < B$): - Quotient $= k$ - Remainder $= 1$

(Note: If $n = 1$, $6^1 - 5(1) = 1 = 25(0) + 1$, which holds universally).

💡 Example 3.2: High-Exponent Remainder with a Negative Offset
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the remainder when $2^{2024}$ is divided by $17$.
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Solution:

We express powers of $2$ in terms of a base close to a multiple of $17$:

$$ 2^4 = 16 = 17 - 1 $$

Decompose the exponent $2024$:

$$ 2024 = 4 \times 506 $$

Thus:

$$ 2^{2024} = (2^4)^{506} = 16^{506} = (17 - 1)^{506} $$

Expand using the Binomial Theorem:

$$ (17 - 1)^{506} = {}^{506}\mathrm{C}_0 (17)^{506} - {}^{506}\mathrm{C}_1 (17)^{505} + \dots - {}^{506}\mathrm{C}_{505} (17)(1)^{505} + {}^{506}\mathrm{C}_{506} (-1)^{506} $$

Factor out $17$ from all terms except the last:

$$ 2^{2024} = 17 \left[ {}^{506}\mathrm{C}_0 (17)^{505} - {}^{506}\mathrm{C}_1 (17)^{504} + \dots - {}^{506}\mathrm{C}_{505} \right] + (-1)^{506} $$

Since $(-1)^{506} = +1$:

$$ 2^{2024} = 17 \cdot M + 1 \quad (M \in \mathbb{Z}) $$

Since $0 \le 1 < 17$, the Remainder $= 1$.

💡 Example 3.3: Fractional Adjustments & Negative Remainders
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the remainder when $3^{105}$ is divided by $10$.
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Solution:

Express $3$ in terms of powers close to a multiple of $10$:

$$ 3^2 = 9 = 10 - 1 $$

Decompose the odd power $105$:

$$ 3^{105} = 3 \cdot 3^{104} = 3 \cdot (3^2)^{52} = 3 \cdot (9)^{52} = 3 \cdot (10 - 1)^{52} $$

Expand $(10 - 1)^{52}$:

$$ (10 - 1)^{52} = {}^{52}\mathrm{C}_0 (10)^{52} - {}^{52}\mathrm{C}_1 (10)^{51} + \dots - {}^{52}\mathrm{C}_{51}(10) + {}^{52}\mathrm{C}_{52}(-1)^{52} = 10k + 1 $$

Multiply by $3$:

$$ 3^{105} = 3(10k + 1) = 30k + 3 = 10(3k) + 3 $$

Thus, the Remainder $= 3$.

💡 Example 3.4: Value Approximation
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Using the Binomial Theorem, evaluate $(1.02)^5$ correct to $4$ decimal places.
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐

Solution:

Split the base into $1 + x$:

$$ 1.02 = 1 + 0.02 = 1 + \frac{2}{100} $$

Apply the expansion:

$$ (1 + 0.02)^5 = {}^5\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_1 (0.02) + {}^5\mathrm{C}_2 (0.02)^2 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_3 (0.02)^3 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_4 (0.02)^4 + {}^5\mathrm{C}_5 (0.02)^5 $$

Compute each term individually: 1. ${}^5\mathrm{C}_0 = 1$ 2. ${}^5\mathrm{C}_1 (0.02) = 5 \times 0.02 = 0.10$ 3. ${}^5\mathrm{C}_2 (0.02)^2 = 10 \times 0.0004 = 0.0040$ 4. ${}^5\mathrm{C}_3 (0.02)^3 = 10 \times 0.000008 = 0.00008$ 5. ${}^5\mathrm{C}_4 (0.02)^4 = 5 \times 0.00000016 = 0.0000008$ (negligible past 4 places)

Sum the significant terms:

$$ (1.02)^5 = 1 + 0.10 + 0.0040 + 0.00008 + \dots = 1.10408 \dots $$

Rounding to $4$ decimal places gives:

$$ (1.02)^5 \approx 1.1041 $$

💡 Example 3.5: Inequality Comparison
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Which is larger: $(1.01)^{1000000}$ or $10,000$?
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (NCERT Exemplar)

Solution:

Express $(1.01)^{1000000}$ as $(1 + 0.01)^{1000000}$.
Expand using the first two terms of the Binomial Theorem:

$$ (1 + 0.01)^{1000000} = {}^{1000000}\mathrm{C}_0 + {}^{1000000}\mathrm{C}_1 (0.01) + \text{positive remaining terms} $$

Evaluate the first two terms:

$$ {}^{1000000}\mathrm{C}_0 = 1 $$

$$ {}^{1000000}\mathrm{C}_1 (0.01) = 1000000 \times \frac{1}{100} = 10000 $$

Since all subsequent terms ${}^{1000000}\mathrm{C}_r (0.01)^r > 0$ for $r \ge 2$:

$$ (1.01)^{1000000} = 1 + 10000 + \sum_{r=2}^{1000000} {}^{1000000}\mathrm{C}_r (0.01)^r > 10001 > 10000 $$

Hence:

$$ (1.01)^{1000000} > 10000 $$

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Rules

Rule 1: Fast Remainder Rule for Base Offsets

For finding remainders with powers of form $(a \cdot m \pm 1)^n \div m$:

$$ (a \cdot m + 1)^n \equiv 1 \pmod m $$

$$ (a \cdot m - 1)^n \equiv (-1)^n \pmod m = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if } n \text{ is even} \\ m - 1, & \text{if } n \text{ is odd} \end{cases} $$

Rule 2: Exponent Extraction Trick

When asked for the remainder of $A^n \div D$, find an integer power $k$ such that:

$$ A^k = m \cdot D \pm 1 \quad \text{or} \quad A^k = m \cdot D \pm r \quad (\text{where } r \text{ is minimal}) $$

Write $n = k \cdot q + s$. Then:

$$ A^n = (A^k)^q \cdot A^s \equiv (\pm 1)^q \cdot A^s \pmod D $$

Example: To find $7^{103} \div 25$: 1. $7^2 = 49 = 50 - 1 = 2(25) - 1$ 2. $7^{103} = 7 \cdot (7^2)^{51} = 7 \cdot (50 - 1)^{51}$ 3. $(50 - 1)^{51} = 25K + (-1)^{51} = 25K - 1$ 4. $7(25K - 1) = 175K - 7 = 25(7K) - 7 = 25(7K - 1) + (25 - 7) = 25M + 18$ 5. Remainder is directly $18$.

Rule 3: Binomial Truncation Criterion

For estimating $(1 + x)^n$ up to $k$ decimal places: - Express $x$ as $10^{-p}$. - The $r$-th term involves $x^r = 10^{-pr}$. - Compute terms up to the index $r$ where:

$$ pr \ge k + 1 $$

Any term where $pr \ge k + 1$ contributes zero to the first $k$ decimal places (accounting for potential carries from intermediate terms).

The total transmission profile across all configurations of bit errors is governed by the binomial probability identity:

$$ r = \frac{n}{2} = \frac{8}{2} = 4 $$

$$ {^8C_4} = \frac{8 \times 7 \times 6 \times 5}{4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1} = 70 $$

$$ T_2 = 8 \times (0.9)^7 \times (0.1) $$

Question A3.1: Three Consecutive Coefficients in Arithmetic Progression

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency HOTS, 5 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: If the coefficients of three consecutive terms in the expansion of $(1 + x)^n$ are in Arithmetic Progression (A.P.), prove that:

$$ n^2 - n(4r + 1) + 4r^2 - 2 = 0 $$

Furthermore, determine the smallest positive integer $n$ for which the expansion of $(1 + x)^n$ possesses three consecutive coefficients forming an A.P., and find these three coefficients explicitly.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

  • Step 1: Setting up the terms and A.P. condition Let the three consecutive terms in $(1 + x)^n$ be the $(r-1)^{\text{th}}$, $r^{\text{th}}$, and $(r+1)^{\text{th}}$ terms, where their binomial coefficients are:

$$ a_1 = {^nC_{r-1}}, \quad a_2 = {^nC_r}, \quad a_3 = {^nC_{r+1}} $$

Since these three coefficients are in Arithmetic Progression:

$$ 2 a_2 = a_1 + a_3 \implies 2 \cdot {^nC_r} = {^nC_{r-1}} + {^nC_{r+1}} $$

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Formulation of consecutive coefficients: [½ Mark] - Applying $2b = a + c$: [½ Mark]

  • Step 2: Dividing by ${^nC_r}$ to utilize consecutive ratio identities Divide both sides by ${^nC_r}$:

$$ 2 = \frac{{^nC_{r-1}}}{{^nC_r}} + \frac{{^nC_{r+1}}}{{^nC_r}} $$

We know the standard identities:

$$ \frac{{^nC_r}}{{^nC_{r-1}}} = \frac{n - r + 1}{r} \implies \frac{{^nC_{r-1}}}{{^nC_r}} = \frac{r}{n - r + 1} $$

$$ \frac{{^nC_{r+1}}}{{^nC_r}} = \frac{n - (r+1) + 1}{r+1} = \frac{n - r}{r+1} $$

Substituting these ratios into the equation:

$$ 2 = \frac{r}{n - r + 1} + \frac{n - r}{r + 1} $$

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Division and simplification using ratio formulas: [1 Mark]

  • Step 3: Algebraic simplification into quadratic in $n$

$$ 2 = \frac{r(r + 1) + (n - r)(n - r + 1)}{(n - r + 1)(r + 1)} $$

Cross-multiplying:

$$ 2(n - r + 1)(r + 1) = r^2 + r + \left[(n - r)^2 + (n - r)\right] $$

Expand Left-Hand Side:

$$ \text{LHS} = 2 [nr + n - r^2 - r + r + 1] = 2 [nr + n - r^2 + 1] = 2nr + 2n - 2r^2 + 2 $$

Expand Right-Hand Side:

$$ \text{RHS} = r^2 + r + n^2 - 2nr + r^2 + n - r = n^2 - 2nr + 2r^2 + n $$

Equating LHS and RHS:

$$ n^2 - 2nr + 2r^2 + n = 2nr + 2n - 2r^2 + 2 $$

Bring all terms to one side:

$$ n^2 - 2nr - 2nr + n - 2n + 2r^2 + 2r^2 - 2 = 0 $$

$$ n^2 - (4r + 1)n + 4r^2 - 2 = 0 $$

(Hence Proved)

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Complete expansion and regrouping of terms: [1 Mark] - Arrival at the target equation $n^2 - n(4r+1) + 4r^2 - 2 = 0$: [½ Mark]

  • Step 4: Determination of smallest positive integer $n$ Treat the equation as a quadratic in $n$:

$$ n = \frac{(4r + 1) \pm \sqrt{(4r + 1)^2 - 4(1)(4r^2 - 2)}}{2} $$

Calculate discriminant $\Delta$:

$$ \Delta = (16r^2 + 8r + 1) - (16r^2 - 8) = 8r + 9 $$

For $n$ to be an integer, $\Delta = 8r + 9$ must be an odd perfect square:

$$ 8r + 9 = k^2 \implies 8r = k^2 - 9 = (k - 3)(k + 3) $$

For consecutive terms $^{n}C_{r-1}, ^{n}C_r, ^{n}C_{r+1}$ to exist, we must have $r - 1 \ge 0 \implies r \ge 1$. - Test $r = 1$: $\Delta = 8(1) + 9 = 17$ (Not a perfect square). - Test $r = 2$: $\Delta = 8(2) + 9 = 25 = 5^2$ (A perfect square!). Substitute $r = 2$ and $\sqrt{\Delta} = 5$:

$$ n = \frac{4(2) + 1 \pm 5}{2} = \frac{9 \pm 5}{2} $$

  • $n = \frac{9 + 5}{2} = 7$
  • $n = \frac{9 - 5}{2} = 2$ If $n = 2$, then $r = 2$ would require $r+1 = 3$, giving $^{2}C_3$, which is undefined. Therefore, the smallest viable positive integer index is:

$$ n = 7 $$

The consecutive terms correspond to $r = 2$, giving indices $r-1=1, r=2, r+1=3$:

$$ {^7C_1} = 7 $$

$$ {^7C_2} = 21 $$

$$ {^7C_3} = 35 $$

Notice that:

$$ 21 - 7 = 14 \quad \text{and} \quad 35 - 21 = 14 $$

They form an exact Arithmetic Progression with common difference $d = 14$.

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Quadratic discriminant setup $\Delta = 8r + 9$: [½ Mark] - Solving for integer constraints on $r$: [½ Mark] - Rejecting extraneous roots and determining $n = 7$: [½ Mark]

$$ (1 + 8)^n = {^nC_0} + {^nC_1}(8) + {^nC_2}(8^2) + {^nC_3}(8^3) + \dots + {^nC_n}(8^n) $$

Substitute ${^nC_0} = 1$ and ${^nC_1} = n$:

$$ (1 + 8)^n = 1 + 8n + 64 \left[{^nC_2} + {^nC_3}(8) + \dots + {^nC_n}(8^{n-2})\right] $$

Let $M = {^nC_2} + {^nC_3}(8) + \dots + {^nC_n}(8^{n-2})$. Since $n \ge 1$ and combination coefficients are integers, $M$ is an integer.

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Expanding $(1+8)^n$ and isolating first two terms: [1.5 Marks]

  • Step 3: Substitute back into original expression

$$ 3^{2n+2} = 9 [1 + 8n + 64M] = 9 + 72n + 576M $$

Now evaluate the full target expression:

$$ 3^{2n+2} - 8n - 9 = (9 + 72n + 576M) - 8n - 9 $$

$$ = (9 - 9) + (72n - 8n) + 576M $$

$$ = 64n + 576M $$

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Substitution and elimination of constants: [1.5 Marks]

  • Step 4: Factor out 64

$$ 3^{2n+2} - 8n - 9 = 64(n + 9M) $$

Since $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $M \in \mathbb{Z}$, the term $(n + 9M)$ is an integer. Therefore:

$$ 3^{2n+2} - 8n - 9 = 64k, \quad \text{where } k \in \mathbb{Z} $$

Thus, $3^{2n+2} - 8n - 9$ is divisible by $64$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$. (Hence Proved)

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Factoring $64$ and stating integer closure: [1 Mark]

Question C2.1: Double Binomial Summation via Integral Evaluation

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar Analytical Problem, 5 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: Prove that:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^{n} {^nC_r} \cdot \frac{1}{r + 1} = \frac{2^{n+1} - 1}{n + 1} $$

and hence find the value of:

$$ {^{10}C_0} + \frac{{^{10}C_1}}{2} + \frac{{^{10}C_2}}{3} + \dots + \frac{{^{10}C_{10}}}{11} $$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

  • Step 1: Algebraic transformation of the general term Consider the general term of the summation:

$$ t_r = \frac{{^nC_r}}{r + 1} $$

Using the factorial definition:

$$ \frac{{^nC_r}}{r + 1} = \frac{n!}{(r+1) \cdot r!(n - r)!} = \frac{n!}{(r+1)!(n - r)!} $$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $(n + 1)$:

$$ \frac{{^nC_r}}{r + 1} = \frac{1}{n + 1} \cdot \frac{(n + 1)!}{(r + 1)!((n + 1) - (r + 1))!} = \frac{1}{n + 1} \cdot {^{n+1}C_{r+1}} $$

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Rigorous factorial manipulation showing $\frac{{^nC_r}}{r+1} = \frac{1}{n+1} {^{n+1}C_{r+1}}$: [2 Marks]

  • Step 2: Execute the summation Summing both sides from $r = 0$ to $n$:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^{n} \frac{{^nC_r}}{r + 1} = \frac{1}{n + 1} \sum_{r=0}^{n} {^{n+1}C_{r+1}} $$

Expanding the sum on the right-hand side:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^{n} {^{n+1}C_{r+1}} = {^{n+1}C_1} + {^{n+1}C_2} + {^{n+1}C_3} + \dots + {^{n+1}C_{n+1}} $$

By the standard binomial coefficient sum property:

$$ \sum_{k=0}^{n+1} {^{n+1}C_k} = {^{n+1}C_0} + \sum_{r=0}^{n} {^{n+1}C_{r+1}} = 2^{n+1} $$

Since $^{n+1}C_0 = 1$:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^{n} {^{n+1}C_{r+1}} = 2^{n+1} - 1 $$

Substituting this back:

$$ \sum_{r=0}^{n} \frac{{^nC_r}}{r + 1} = \frac{2^{n+1} - 1}{n + 1} $$

(Hence Proved)

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Applying the complete sum identity $\sum {^{n+1}C_k} = 2^{n+1}$: [1 Mark] - Subtracting $^{n+1}C_0 = 1$ and arriving at formula: [1 Mark]

  • Step 3: Numerical computation for $n = 10$ The given expression is precisely our proven sum with $n = 10$:

$$ S = \sum_{r=0}^{10} \frac{{^{10}C_r}}{r+1} = \frac{2^{10+1} - 1}{10 + 1} = \frac{2^{11} - 1}{11} $$

Evaluating powers of 2:

$$ 2^{11} = 2048 $$

$$ S = \frac{2048 - 1}{11} = \frac{2047}{11} $$

Dividing 2047 by 11:

$$ 2047 = 11 \times 186 + 1 \implies 2047 / 11 = 186.0909... $$

Check exact division:

$$ 2047 = 11 \times 186 + 1 \implies 11 \times 186 = 2046 $$

$$ 2047 = 11 \times 186.09... \text{ (Keep as exact irreducible fraction):} $$

$$ S = \frac{2047}{11} $$

(Note: $2047 = 23 \times 89$, which is not divisible by 11. The exact fractional answer is $\frac{2047}{11}$.)

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Substitution of $n=10$: [½ Mark] - Final evaluation: [½ Mark]

Question C2.2: Finding Integral and Fractional Parts via Conjugates

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar HOTS, 5 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: Show that the integer just greater than $(\sqrt{3} + 1)^{2n}$ is divisible by $2^{n+1}$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Rubric:

  • Step 1: Simplify the base using exponent properties

$$ (\sqrt{3} + 1)^{2n} = \left[(\sqrt{3} + 1)^2\right]^n = [3 + 2\sqrt{3} + 1]^n = (4 + 2\sqrt{3})^n = 2^n (2 + \sqrt{3})^n $$

Let:

$$ X = (2 + \sqrt{3})^n $$

Then our target quantity is $2^n X$.

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Factoring out $2^n$: [1 Mark]

  • Step 2: Define the conjugate and set up bounds Let $Y = (2 - \sqrt{3})^n$. Since $1.732 < \sqrt{3} < 1.733$:

$$ 0 < 2 - \sqrt{3} < 0.268 < 1 $$

Since $0 < 2 - \sqrt{3} < 1$, for any positive integer $n$:

$$ 0 < Y < 1 \implies 0 < (2 - \sqrt{3})^n < 1 $$

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Introducing conjugate $Y = (2-\sqrt{3})^n$ and proving $0 < Y < 1$: [1 Mark]

  • Step 3: Expand $X + Y$ using the Binomial Theorem

$$ X = (2 + \sqrt{3})^n = {^nC_0} 2^n + {^nC_1} 2^{n-1} \sqrt{3} + {^nC_2} 2^{n-2} (\sqrt{3})^2 + {^nC_3} 2^{n-3} (\sqrt{3})^3 + \dots $$

$$ Y = (2 - \sqrt{3})^n = {^nC_0} 2^n - {^nC_1} 2^{n-1} \sqrt{3} + {^nC_2} 2^{n-2} (\sqrt{3})^2 - {^nC_3} 2^{n-3} (\sqrt{3})^3 + \dots $$

Adding the two expansions:

$$ X + Y = 2 \left[{^nC_0} 2^n + {^nC_2} 2^{n-2} (3) + {^nC_4} 2^{n-4} (3^2) + \dots\right] $$

Notice that inside the bracket, every single term is an integer because powers of $\sqrt{3}$ are even. Let:

$$ k = {^nC_0} 2^n + 3 \cdot {^nC_2} 2^{n-2} + 9 \cdot {^nC_4} 2^{n-4} + \dots \in \mathbb{Z}^+ $$

Then:

$$ X + Y = 2k \quad (\text{an even integer}) $$

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Expanding both series and canceling odd radical powers: [1 Mark] - Proving sum is an even integer $2k$: [½ Mark]

  • Step 4: Establish the integer just greater than the original number Multiply the relation by $2^n$:

$$ 2^n X + 2^n Y = 2^n (2k) = 2^{n+1} k $$

Recall that:

$$ (\sqrt{3} + 1)^{2n} = 2^n X $$

Therefore:

$$ (\sqrt{3} + 1)^{2n} + 2^n Y = 2^{n+1} k $$

Since $0 < Y < 1$, we have:

$$ 0 < 2^n Y < 2^n $$

Let $I$ be the integer part of $(\sqrt{3}+1)^{2n}$ and $f$ be its fractional part, $0 < f < 1$. From $2^n X = 2^{n+1} k - 2^n Y$: The integer just greater than $2^n X = (\sqrt{3} + 1)^{2n}$ is precisely:

$$ \lceil 2^n X \rceil = 2^{n+1} k $$

Since $k$ is an integer, $2^{n+1} k$ is directly divisible by $2^{n+1}$. (Hence Proved)

CBSE Marking Rubric: - Multiplying through by $2^n$: [½ Mark] - Proving ceiling/just greater integer equals $2^{n+1}k$: [½ Mark] - Divisibility conclusion: [½ Mark]

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 The "Index Balance" Shortcut for $x^k$ in $\left(a x^p + \frac{b}{x^q}\right)^n$

Instead of writing out full equations for $T_{r+1}$, use the direct closed-form formula for the index $r$:

$$ r = \frac{n \cdot p - k}{p + q} $$

  • Term independent of $x$ ($k=0$):

$$ r = \frac{n \cdot p}{p + q} $$

  • Mental Verification Rule: If $r \notin \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\}$ or $r$ is fractional/negative, conclude immediately: "No such term exists; coefficient is $0$." Do not waste time simplifying the binomial coefficients.

1.2 The "Ratio of Consecutive Terms" Shortcut $\left(\frac{T_{r+1}}{T_r}\right)$

To find the numerically greatest term (NGT) or solve ratio problems:

$$ \frac{T_{r+1}}{T_r} = \frac{\binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} x^r}{\binom{n}{r-1} a^{n-r+1} x^{r-1}} = \left(\frac{n - r + 1}{r}\right) \cdot \frac{x}{a} $$

  • Numerically Greatest Term (NGT) in $(1 + x)^n$: Set $\left|\frac{T_{r+1}}{T_r}\right| \ge 1 \implies r \le \frac{(n + 1)|x|}{|x| + 1}$.
  • If $\frac{(n + 1)|x|}{|x| + 1} = m$ (an integer), then $T_m$ and $T_{m+1}$ are equal and both are the greatest terms.
  • If non-integer, take $r = \left\lfloor \frac{(n + 1)|x|}{|x| + 1} \right\rfloor$, and $T_{r+1}$ is the unique greatest term.

1.3 Rapid Divisibility / Remainder Hack: Base Splitting

To find the remainder when $A^n$ is divided by $B$: 1. Express $A$ as $(mB + 1)^n$ or $(mB - 1)^n$ or $(mB + k)^n$. 2. Mentally discard all terms with powers of $mB \ge 2$ when dividing by $B^2$. 3. Negative Remainder Correction Rule: If $(B - 1)^n = \dots - 1$, the remainder is $-1$. Board Penalty Alert: In CBSE, a remainder must satisfy $0 \le R < \text{Divisor}$. Immediately convert $-1 \pmod B$ to $B - 1$.

$$ \text{Remainder} = B + (\text{negative residual}) $$

1.4 Calculus & Combinatorial Evaluation Shortcuts (Competitive / Checking Tricks)

For quick validation of series involving binomial coefficients: * Derivative Shortcut: If the index $r$ is multiplied with $\binom{n}{r}$ ($\sum r \binom{n}{r}$), differentiate $(1 + x)^n = \sum \binom{n}{r} x^r$ with respect to $x$ and substitute $x = 1$:

$$ n(1 + x)^{n-1} = \sum_{r=1}^n r \binom{n}{r} x^{r-1} \xrightarrow{x=1} \sum_{r=1}^n r \binom{n}{r} = n \cdot 2^{n-1} $$

  • Integral Shortcut: If $r+1$ appears in the denominator ($\sum \frac{\binom{n}{r}}{r+1}$), integrate from $0$ to $1$:

$$ \int_0^1 (1 + x)^n \, dx = \left[ \frac{(1+x)^{n+1}}{n+1} \right]_0^1 = \frac{2^{n+1}-1}{n+1} $$

1.5 Middle Term Quick Map

In $(a + b)^n$: * $n$ is Even: Exactly $1$ middle term:

$$ T_{\frac{n}{2} + 1} $$

  • $n$ is Odd: Exactly $2$ middle terms:

$$ T_{\frac{n+1}{2}} \quad \text{and} \quad T_{\frac{n+3}{2}} \quad \left(\text{i.e., } r = \frac{n-1}{2}, \; r = \frac{n+1}{2}\right) $$

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

Trap 2.1: The General Term Index Confusion ($r$ vs. $r+1$)

  • The Slip: When asked for the "$5^{\text{th}}$ term", writing $r = 5$ instead of $r = 4$.
  • The Board Rubric:
  • Formula line: $T_{r+1} = \binom{n}{r} a^{n-r} b^r$ ($\frac{1}{2}$ mark).
  • Substitution line: If the student writes $T_5 = \binom{n}{5}\dots$, a full $1$ mark deduction occurs instantly, propagating arithmetic errors to the final answer.

Trap 2.2: The Negative Sign Absorber in Alternate Series

  • Problem Type: Find the coefficient in $\left(2x - \frac{1}{3x^2}\right)^7$.
  • The Trap: Writing $b = \frac{1}{3x^2}$ and forgetting $(-1)^r$.
  • Examiner Grading Rule:

$$ T_{r+1} = \binom{7}{r} (2x)^{7-r} \left(-\frac{1}{3x^2}\right)^r = (-1)^r \binom{7}{r} 2^{7-r} 3^{-r} x^{7-3r} $$

Leaving out $(-1)^r$ reduces an otherwise complete 3-mark question to 1 mark (zero awarded for calculation steps due to sign error).

Trap 2.3: "Coefficient" vs. "Term" Misinterpretation

  • The Trap: When the question asks: "Find the coefficient of $x^3$," students frequently write:

$$ \text{Answer: } 560 x^3 \quad (INCORRECT) $$

  • Penalty: $-\frac{1}{2}$ mark. The variable component must NOT be included. The correct answer is strictly the scalar/constant multiplier:

$$ \text{Answer: } 560 \quad (CORRECT) $$

Trap 2.4: Finding Terms "From the End"

  • The Pitfall: In the expansion of $(a + b)^n$, the $k^{\text{th}}$ term from the end is often miscalculated as $n - k + 1$.
  • Standard NCERT Identity:

$$ \text{Total number of terms} = n + 1 $$

$$ \text{Term from the end } (k^{\text{th}}) = (n + 1 - k + 1)^{\text{th}} \text{ term from the beginning} = (n - k + 2)^{\text{th}} \text{ term} $$

  • Examiner Approved Inversion Trick: Rather than recalculating indices, rewrite $(a + b)^n$ as $(b + a)^n$ and compute the $k^{\text{th}}$ term from the beginning directly:

$$ T_k \text{ in } (b + a)^n $$

Always state: "Reversing terms: $(a + b)^n = (b + a)^n$."

Trap 2.5: Non-Integral / Out-of-Bounds $r$ Values

  • When searching for a term independent of $x$, if algebraic equating yields $r = \frac{11}{3}$ or $r < 0$ or $r > n$:
  • Mistake: Forcing rounding ($r \approx 4$) or assuming an algebraic manipulation error.
  • CBSE Prescription: You must explicitly state:

$$ \because r \notin \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\}, \; r \notin \mathbb{W} $$

$$ \therefore \text{There is no term independent of } x \text{ in the given expansion. Coefficient} = 0. $$

  • Failing to explicitly mention that $r$ must be a non-negative integer $\le n$ costs $\frac{1}{2}$ mark.

Trap 2.6: Truncated Remainder Solutions in Binomial Divisibility Proofs

  • Example: Prove that $9^{n+1} - 8n - 9$ is divisible by $64$, for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
  • Required Step Sequence for Full Marks:
  • Express $9^{n+1}$ as $(1 + 8)^{n+1}$.
  • Write explicit expansion:

$$ (1 + 8)^{n+1} = \binom{n+1}{0} + \binom{n+1}{1}\cdot 8 + \binom{n+1}{2}\cdot 8^2 + \binom{n+1}{3}\cdot 8^3 + \dots + \binom{n+1}{n+1}\cdot 8^{n+1} $$

  1. Simplify the first two terms:

$$ (1 + 8)^{n+1} = 1 + (n + 1)8 + 64 \left[ \binom{n+1}{2} + \binom{n+1}{3}\cdot 8 + \dots + 8^{n-1} \right] $$

$$ 9^{n+1} = 1 + 8n + 8 + 64k \implies 9^{n+1} - 8n - 9 = 64k $$

  1. The Trap: Failing to declare: "where $k = \binom{n+1}{2} + \dots + 8^{n-1}$ is an integer since $n \in \mathbb{N}$."
  2. Omitting the statement that $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ costs $\frac{1}{2}$ mark under board evaluation scheme marking keys.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

  1. Domain of the Exponent ($n$): In Class 11 CBSE NCERT, the Binomial Theorem is strictly studied for positive integral indices ($n \in \mathbb{N}$). If $n$ is not a natural number, the series is infinite and requires $|x| < 1$, which is outside the Class 11 examination syllabus.

  2. Total Number of Terms: The expansion of $(a + b)^n$ contains exactly $n + 1$ terms.

  3. Expansion of $(a + b + c)^n$: Total terms $= \frac{(n + 1)(n + 2)}{2}$.

  4. Expansion of $(x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_k)^n$: Total terms $= \binom{n + k - 1}{k - 1}$.

  5. Parity Addition & Subtraction Forms:

  6. $(x + a)^n + (x - a)^n = 2 \left[ T_1 + T_3 + T_5 + \dots \right] = 2 \left[ \binom{n}{0}x^n + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}a^2 + \dots \right]$
    • Number of terms when $n$ is even: $\frac{n}{2} + 1$
    • Number of terms when $n$ is odd: $\frac{n + 1}{2}$

  7. $(x + a)^n - (x - a)^n = 2 \left[ T_2 + T_4 + T_6 + \dots \right] = 2 \left[ \binom{n}{1}x^{n-1}a + \binom{n}{3}x^{n-3}a^3 + \dots \right]$

    • Number of terms when $n$ is even: $\frac{n}{2}$
    • Number of terms when $n$ is odd: $\frac{n + 1}{2}$

  8. Symmetry of Coefficients:

$$ \binom{n}{r} = \binom{n}{n-r} $$

Coefficients equidistant from the beginning and the end are strictly equal.

  1. Sum of Coefficients Property: To find the sum of all coefficients in any polynomial expansion $P(x, y) = (a x + b y)^n$:

$$ \text{Sum of coefficients} = P(1, 1) = (a + b)^n $$

  • For $(1 - 3x + x^2)^{111}$, the sum of coefficients is $(1 - 3(1) + 1^2)^{111} = (-1)^{111} = -1$.

  • Equidistant Term Ratio Identity: In $(1 + x)^n$, if coefficients of three consecutive terms are in A.P., i.e., $\binom{n}{r-1}, \binom{n}{r}, \binom{n}{r+1}$ are in A.P., then:

$$ n^2 - n(4r + 1) + 4r^2 - 2 = 0 $$

Vandermonde's Identity $\sum_{k=0}^r \binom{m}{k}\binom{n}{r-k} = \binom{m+n}{r}$ Product of $(1+x)^m(1+x)^n = (1+x)^{m+n}$
Sum of Squares $\sum_{r=0}^n \binom{n}{r}^2 = \binom{2n}{n} = \frac{(2n)!}{(n!)^2}$ Equating coefficient of $x^n$ in $(1+x)^{2n}$
Consecutive Term Ratio $\frac{\binom{n}{r}}{\binom{n}{r-1}} = \frac{n - r + 1}{r}$ Crucial for NGT (Numerically Greatest Term)
