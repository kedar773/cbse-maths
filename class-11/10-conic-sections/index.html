MODULE 1: THE CIRCLE (Definition, Standard & General Equations, Center, and Radius)

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Conic Section Context (Geometric Locus Perspective)

A conic section is the curve obtained by taking the intersection of a plane with a double-napped right circular cone.

Let the angle between the vertical central axis of the cone and any generator line be $\alpha$, and let the angle between the cutting plane and the axis of the cone be $\beta$. - When the cutting plane is perpendicular to the axis of the cone ($\beta = 90^\circ$), the section formed is a Circle. - In the Cartesian coordinate plane, this spatial slice translates into a fundamental planar locus problem.

1.2 Formal Definition of a Circle ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definition (NCERT):
A circle is the locus of all points in a plane that are at a constant distance from a fixed point in that plane. - The fixed point is called the center of the circle. - The constant distance from the center to any point on the curve is called the radius of the circle.

                  P(x, y) [Arbitrary point on locus]
                 /
                /  r (Constant Radius)
               /
              C(h, k) [Fixed Center]

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 2.1: Derivation of the Standard Equation of a Circle ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
The equation of a circle having center at $C(h, k)$ and radius equal to $r$ ($r > 0$) is given by:

$$ (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 $$

Proof:

  1. Given:
    Fixed center $C(h, k)$ and a constant radius $r \in \mathbb{R}^+$.
  2. To Prove:
    The locus of any point $P(x, y)$ equidistant from $C$ by distance $r$ satisfies:

$$ (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 $$

  1. Geometric Condition:
    Let $P(x, y)$ be any arbitrary point lying on the circumference of the circle.
    By definition of a circle:

$$ |CP| = r $$

  1. Distance Formula Application:
    Applying the Euclidean distance formula between coordinates $C(h, k)$ and $P(x, y)$:

$$ \sqrt{(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2} = r $$

  1. Squaring Both Sides:
    Since both sides are non-negative ($r > 0$ and Euclidean distance $\ge 0$), squaring preserves equivalence:

$$ \left(\sqrt{(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2}\right)^2 = r^2 $$

$$ (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 $$

  1. Converse Verification:
    Let $(x_0, y_0)$ be any coordinate pair satisfying $(x_0 - h)^2 + (y_0 - k)^2 = r^2$. Taking the positive square root gives $\sqrt{(x_0 - h)^2 + (y_0 - k)^2} = r$, establishing that the distance of $(x_0, y_0)$ from $C(h, k)$ is identically $r$.

$$ \text{Hence, the locus represents the circle. } \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 2.2: Reduction of General Second-Degree Equation to Center-Radius Form ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
The general equation of the second degree in two variables:

$$ ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 $$

represents a circle if and only if: 1. Coefficient of $x^2$ = Coefficient of $y^2 \neq 0$ (i.e., $a = b \neq 0$) 2. Coefficient of $xy$ is zero (i.e., $h = 0$)

When normalized such that $a = b = 1$, the equation becomes:

$$ x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 $$

representing a circle with:

$$ \text{Center} = (-g, -f), \qquad \text{Radius} = \sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c} $$

Proof:

  1. Given:
    The quadratic equation:

$$ x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 $$

  1. To Prove:
    It can be expressed in the form $(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = R^2$, identifying center $(h, k)$ and radius $R$.
  2. Regrouping Terms:
    Group the terms containing $x$ and $y$ separately, and isolate the constant $c$:

$$ (x^2 + 2gx) + (y^2 + 2fy) = -c $$

  1. Completing the Square:
  2. For the $x$-terms: Add $\left(\frac{2g}{2}\right)^2 = g^2$ to both sides.
  3. For the $y$-terms: Add $\left(\frac{2f}{2}\right)^2 = f^2$ to both sides.

$$ (x^2 + 2gx + g^2) + (y^2 + 2fy + f^2) = g^2 + f^2 - c $$

  1. Factoring into Perfect Squares:

$$ (x + g)^2 + (y + f)^2 = g^2 + f^2 - c $$

  1. Mapping to Standard Form:
    Rewrite in canonical central form:

$$ \Big(x - (-g)\Big)^2 + \Big(y - (-f)\Big)^2 = \left(\sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c}\right)^2 $$

Comparing with $(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = R^2$:

$$ h = -g, \quad k = -f \implies \text{Center } C = (-g, -f) $$

$$ R = \sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} \quad \blacksquare $$

2.3 Character of a Circle Based on the Discriminant $\Delta_c = g^2 + f^2 - c$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐

The expression under the radical determines the nature of the circle:

  1. Real Circle:

$$ g^2 + f^2 - c > 0 $$

The radius is a positive real number; the locus is a circle in the real Euclidean plane with non-zero area $\pi R^2$.

  1. Point Circle (Degenerate Circle):

$$ g^2 + f^2 - c = 0 $$

The radius $R = 0$. The equation reduces to $(x + g)^2 + (y + f)^2 = 0$. In the real plane $\mathbb{R}^2$, the sum of two non-negative terms is zero if and only if each term is zero:

$$ x + g = 0 \implies x = -g \quad \text{and} \quad y + f = 0 \implies y = -f $$

The locus collapses to the single isolated point $(-g, -f)$.

  1. Imaginary / Virtual Circle (No Real Locus):

$$ g^2 + f^2 - c < 0 $$

The radius is purely imaginary ($R \in \mathbb{C} \setminus \mathbb{R}$). No real coordinates $(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ satisfy the equation. The solution set in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the empty set $\emptyset$.

Theorem 2.3: Diametric Form of the Equation of a Circle ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
The equation of a circle having the line segment joining $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$ as diameter is:

$$ (x - x_1)(x - x_2) + (y - y_1)(y - y_2) = 0 $$

Proof:

  1. Given:
    End-points of a diameter $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$.
  2. Construction / Geometry:
    Let $P(x, y)$ be any point on the circle distinct from $A$ and $B$.
    By the Angle in a Semicircle Theorem, the angle subtended by a diameter at any point on the circumference is a right angle:

$$ \angle APB = 90^\circ \implies AP \perp BP $$

  1. Slope Product Condition:
    The slope of line segment $AP$ ($m_1$) and $BP$ ($m_2$) are:

$$ m_1 = \frac{y - y_1}{x - x_1}, \qquad m_2 = \frac{y - y_2}{x - x_2} $$

Since $AP \perp BP$:

$$ m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1 $$

$$ \left(\frac{y - y_1}{x - x_1}\right)\left(\frac{y - y_2}{x - x_2}\right) = -1 $$

  1. Cross-Multiplication & Algebraic Arrangement:

$$ (y - y_1)(y - y_2) = -(x - x_1)(x - x_2) $$

$$ (x - x_1)(x - x_2) + (y - y_1)(y - y_2) = 0 $$

Note: The coordinates of endpoints $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$ trivially satisfy this equation, so it holds for all points on the circle.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} \quad \blacksquare $$

Parabola: Definition, Standard Forms, Focus, Directrix, Axis & Latus Rectum

Class 11 Mathematics — Conic Sections

Comprehensive Lecture Notes: Parabola (Definition, Standard Forms, Focus, Directrix, Axis & Latus Rectum)

1. Pedagogical Theory & Geometric Intuition

1.1 Definition as a Section of a Cone

A conic section is the curve obtained by the intersection of a double right circular cone with a plane. Let: - $\alpha$ be the semi-vertical angle of the double cone. - $\beta$ be the angle that the intersecting plane makes with the vertical axis of the cone.

When the cutting plane is tilted such that it is parallel to a generator (slant edge) of the cone, the cutting angle equals the semi-vertical angle:

$$ \beta = \alpha $$

The resulting open, unbounded plane curve is defined geometrically as a Parabola.

1.2 Locus Definition in a Plane (NCERT Standard Definition)

Definition (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐):
A parabola is the set of all points in a plane that are equidistant from a fixed line (the directrix) and a fixed point (the focus) not on the line.

Let: - $S$ be the fixed point (Focus). - $L = 0$ be the fixed straight line (Directrix). - $P(x, y)$ be any arbitrary point on the parabola. - $M$ be the foot of the perpendicular drawn from $P$ to the directrix $L$.

By definition:

$$ SP = PM \quad \implies \quad \frac{SP}{PM} = 1 $$

Here, the ratio $e = \frac{SP}{PM}$ is termed the eccentricity of the conic. For a parabola:

$$ e = 1 $$

1.3 Core Nomenclature & Structural Anatomy (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

  1. Focus ($S$): The fixed point defining the locus. For the standard parabola $y^2 = 4ax$, $S = (a, 0)$.
  2. Directrix ($L$): The fixed straight line from which distances are measured. For $y^2 = 4ax$, equation of directrix is $x + a = 0$.
  3. Axis of the Parabola: The straight line passing through the focus and perpendicular to the directrix. It acts as an axis of bilateral symmetry for the curve.
  4. Vertex ($V$ or $O$): The point of intersection of the parabola with its axis. The vertex bisects the perpendicular segment joining the focus to the directrix.
  5. Focal Chord: Any chord of the parabola passing through the focus $S$.
  6. Double Ordinate: A chord perpendicular to the axis of the parabola.
  7. Latus Rectum ($LL'$): A special double ordinate passing directly through the focus. It is perpendicular to the axis and terminated at both ends by the parabola.
  8. Focal Distance of a Point: The Euclidean distance from any point $P(x_1, y_1)$ on the parabola to the focus $S$.

2. Rigorous Derivations and Mathematical Proofs

2.1 Derivation of the Standard Equation: $y^2 = 4ax$ ($a > 0$) (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Given:
- Focus $S = (a, 0)$ where $a > 0$.
- Directrix $d$: Straight line perpendicular to the $x$-axis at distance $a$ to the left of the origin, given by $x = -a \iff x + a = 0$.

To Prove:
The locus of $P(x, y)$ satisfying $SP = PM$ is:

$$ y^2 = 4ax $$

Construction:
Drop a perpendicular $PM$ from an arbitrary point $P(x, y)$ onto the directrix line $x + a = 0$. The coordinates of $M$ are $(-a, y)$.

Step-by-Step Algebraic Derivation:

  1. Distance $SP$ (using Euclidean distance formula):

$$ SP = \sqrt{(x - a)^2 + (y - 0)^2} = \sqrt{(x - a)^2 + y^2} $$

  1. Distance $PM$ (perpendicular distance to $x + a = 0$):

$$ PM = \frac{|x + a|}{\sqrt{1^2 + 0^2}} = |x + a| $$

  1. Equating distances using the definition of a parabola ($SP = PM$):

$$ \sqrt{(x - a)^2 + y^2} = |x + a| $$

  1. Squaring both sides (valid since both quantities are non-negative distances):

$$ (x - a)^2 + y^2 = (x + a)^2 $$

  1. Expanding both binomial squares:

$$ x^2 - 2ax + a^2 + y^2 = x^2 + 2ax + a^2 $$

  1. Cancelling identical terms ($x^2$ and $a^2$) on both sides:

$$ -2ax + y^2 = 2ax $$

  1. Transposing $-2ax$ to the right-hand side:

$$ y^2 = 2ax + 2ax $$

$$ y^2 = 4ax $$

Q.E.D.

2.2 Proof: The Vertex Bisects the Distance from Focus to Directrix (⭐⭐⭐)

Let the directrix intersect the axis at point $Z(-a, 0)$. The focus is $S(a, 0)$. The origin $O(0, 0)$ lies on the curve because:

$$ 0^2 = 4a(0) \implies 0 = 0 $$

Distance of $O$ from focus $S$:

$$ OS = |a - 0| = a $$

Distance of $O$ from directrix $x = -a$:

$$ OZ = |0 - (-a)| = a $$

Since $OS = OZ = a$ and $O, S, Z$ are collinear on the $x$-axis, $O(0, 0)$ is the exact midpoint of segment $ZS$.
Q.E.D.

2.3 Derivation of Length of Latus Rectum and Endpoints (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Given:
Standard equation $y^2 = 4ax$.
Latus rectum $LL'$ is a chord passing through focus $S(a, 0)$ perpendicular to the axis of symmetry ($x$-axis).

To Prove:
1. Coordinates of endpoints are $L(a, 2a)$ and $L'(a, -2a)$. 2. Total length of Latus Rectum = $4a$. 3. Semi-Latus Rectum = $2a$.

Proof: 1. Since the line $LL'$ passes through $S(a, 0)$ and is parallel to the $y$-axis, its equation is:

$$ x = a $$

  1. Substitute $x = a$ into the parabola equation $y^2 = 4ax$:

$$ y^2 = 4a(a) = 4a^2 $$

  1. Taking square roots on both sides:

$$ y = \pm 2a $$

  1. Hence, the coordinates of the endpoints of the latus rectum are:

$$ L = (a, 2a) \quad \text{and} \quad L' = (a, -2a) $$

  1. The length of the latus rectum is the Euclidean distance $LL'$:

$$ \text{Length}(LL') = \sqrt{(a - a)^2 + (2a - (-2a))^2} = \sqrt{0 + (4a)^2} = 4a $$

$$ \text{Semi-Latus Rectum} = \frac{4a}{2} = 2a $$

Q.E.D.

2.4 Derivation of the Focal Distance Formula (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem:
For any point $P(x_1, y_1)$ lying on the parabola $y^2 = 4ax$, the focal distance $SP$ is given by:

$$ SP = x_1 + a $$

Proof:
From the fundamental definition:

$$ SP = PM $$

where $M$ is the foot of perpendicular from $P(x_1, y_1)$ to the directrix $x + a = 0$. The perpendicular distance is:

$$ PM = |x_1 + a| $$

Since $y_1^2 = 4ax_1$ and $a > 0$, we have $x_1 = \frac{y_1^2}{4a} \ge 0$.
Because $x_1 \ge 0$ and $a > 0$, the sum $(x_1 + a) > 0$. Therefore, the absolute value can be removed directly:

$$ SP = x_1 + a $$

Q.E.D.

Comprehensive Teaching Notes: The Ellipse

Section 1: In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Conic Section Context & Classical Definition

An ellipse is a conic section obtained when a right circular cone is intersected by a plane that cuts completely across one nappe of the cone at an angle $\beta$ greater than the semi-vertical angle $\alpha$ of the cone ($\alpha < \beta < 90^\circ$).

In plane coordinate geometry, an ellipse is formally defined in two complementary ways:

Definition 1: Sum of Focal Distances Property (Focal-Locus Definition — Primary NCERT Approach) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definition: An ellipse is the set (or locus) of all points $P$ in a plane such that the sum of their distances from two fixed points $F_1$ and $F_2$ in the plane is a constant, and this constant is strictly greater than the distance between the two fixed points.

Mathematically, if $F_1$ and $F_2$ are the two foci, then for any point $P(x, y)$ on the ellipse:

$$ PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a \quad \text{where } 2a > F_1F_2 $$

Geometric Intuition: - If $2a > F_1F_2$, the locus forms a closed smooth oval curve (an ellipse). - If $2a = F_1F_2$, the locus collapses into the straight line segment joining $F_1$ and $F_2$. - If $2a < F_1F_2$, by the triangle inequality ($PF_1 + PF_2 \ge F_1F_2$), no such real point $P$ can exist (empty set). - If the two foci coincide ($F_1 = F_2$), the distance $F_1F_2 = 0$, and the condition becomes $2PF_1 = 2a \implies PF_1 = a$, which gives a circle of radius $a$. Thus, a circle is a limiting case of an ellipse with eccentricity zero.

Definition 2: Focus-Directrix Property (Eccentricity Definition) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definition: An ellipse is the locus of a point $P$ which moves in a plane such that the ratio of its distance from a fixed point $S$ (focus) to its perpendicular distance from a fixed straight line $D$ (directrix) is a constant $e$ (eccentricity), where:

$$ 0 < e < 1 $$

$$ \frac{SP}{PM} = e \implies SP = e \cdot PM $$

Section 2: Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: Fundamental Relation Between $a, b,$ and $c$ ($c^2 = a^2 - b^2$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Let an ellipse have semi-major axis $a$, semi-minor axis $b$, and focal distance from center $c$. Then $b^2 = a^2 - c^2$, or equivalently, $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$.

  • Given:
  • An ellipse with foci $F_1(-c, 0)$ and $F_2(c, 0)$.
  • The sum of distances from any point on the ellipse to the foci is $2a$.
  • The vertex $V_1(a, 0)$ lies on the ellipse.

  • The co-vertex $B(0, b)$ lies on the ellipse.

  • To Prove:

$$ c^2 = a^2 - b^2 \iff a^2 = b^2 + c^2 $$

  • Proof: Consider the co-vertex $B(0, b)$ located on the minor axis.

By the definition of the ellipse:

$$ BF_1 + BF_2 = 2a $$

Using the Euclidean distance formula between $B(0, b)$ and $F_1(-c, 0)$:

$$ BF_1 = \sqrt{(0 - (-c))^2 + (b - 0)^2} = \sqrt{c^2 + b^2} $$

Similarly, between $B(0, b)$ and $F_2(c, 0)$:

$$ BF_2 = \sqrt{(0 - c)^2 + (b - 0)^2} = \sqrt{(-c)^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{c^2 + b^2} $$

Substituting these into the focal sum equation:

$$ \sqrt{c^2 + b^2} + \sqrt{c^2 + b^2} = 2a $$

$$ 2\sqrt{b^2 + c^2} = 2a $$

$$ \sqrt{b^2 + c^2} = a $$

Squaring both sides gives:

$$ b^2 + c^2 = a^2 \implies c^2 = a^2 - b^2 $$

Since eccentricity is defined as $e = \frac{c}{a}$, we have:

$$ e^2 = \frac{c^2}{a^2} = \frac{a^2 - b^2}{a^2} = 1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2} \implies e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Derivation of the Standard Equation of a Horizontal Ellipse ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Derive the Cartesian equation of an ellipse centered at the origin with major axis along the $x$-axis:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad (a > b > 0) $$

  • Given:
  • Foci located at $F_1(-c, 0)$ and $F_2(c, 0)$.
  • Constant focal distance sum: $PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a$ ($a > c > 0$).

  • A general point $P(x, y)$ lying on the locus.

  • To Prove:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2 - c^2} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

  • Algebraic Derivation:

  • Express focal distances via the distance formula:

$$ PF_1 = \sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} $$

$$ PF_2 = \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

  1. Set up the defining condition:

$$ \sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} + \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} = 2a $$

  1. Isolate one radical term to avoid unwieldy cross-products:

$$ \sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} = 2a - \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

  1. Square both sides:

$$ (x + c)^2 + y^2 = 4a^2 - 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} + \left[(x - c)^2 + y^2\right] $$

  1. Expand the binomial squares:

$$ x^2 + 2cx + c^2 + y^2 = 4a^2 - 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} + x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2 $$

  1. Cancel identical terms ($x^2, c^2, y^2$) from both sides:

$$ 2cx = 4a^2 - 2cx - 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

$$ 4cx - 4a^2 = -4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

  1. Divide the entire equation by $-4$:

$$ a^2 - cx = a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

  1. Square both sides again to eliminate the remaining radical:

$$ (a^2 - cx)^2 = a^2 \left[(x - c)^2 + y^2\right] $$

$$ a^4 - 2a^2cx + c^2x^2 = a^2 \left[x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2\right] $$

$$ a^4 - 2a^2cx + c^2x^2 = a^2x^2 - 2a^2cx + a^2c^2 + a^2y^2 $$

  1. Cancel the cross-term $-2a^2cx$ on both sides:

$$ a^4 + c^2x^2 = a^2x^2 + a^2c^2 + a^2y^2 $$

  1. Group terms involving $x^2$ and $y^2$ on one side and constants on the other:

$$ a^4 - a^2c^2 = a^2x^2 - c^2x^2 + a^2y^2 $$

$$ a^2(a^2 - c^2) = (a^2 - c^2)x^2 + a^2y^2 $$

  1. Substitute $b^2 = a^2 - c^2$ (from Proof 2.1):

$$ a^2b^2 = b^2x^2 + a^2y^2 $$

  1. Divide both sides by $a^2b^2$:

$$ \frac{b^2x^2}{a^2b^2} + \frac{a^2y^2}{a^2b^2} = \frac{a^2b^2}{a^2b^2} $$

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: Derivation of the Length of the Latus Rectum ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: The length of the latus rectum of the standard ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ is $\frac{2b^2}{a}$.

  • Given:
  • Standard horizontal ellipse: $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$.
  • Focus $F_2 = (c, 0) = (ae, 0)$.

  • A chord passing through $F_2$ perpendicular to the major axis ($x$-axis) intersects the curve at $L(c, y_1)$ and $L'(c, -y_1)$.

  • To Prove:

$$ \text{Length}(LL') = 2y_1 = \frac{2b^2}{a} $$

  • Proof: Since point $L(c, y_1)$ lies on the ellipse, its coordinates satisfy the equation:

$$ \frac{c^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

Substitute $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$:

$$ \frac{a^2 - b^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

$$ 1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

Cancel $1$ from both sides:

$$ \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = \frac{b^2}{a^2} $$

$$ y_1^2 = \frac{b^4}{a^2} $$

$$ y_1 = \pm \frac{b^2}{a} $$

The semi-latus rectum is $y_1 = \frac{b^2}{a}$. By vertical symmetry across the $x$-axis, the full length of the latus rectum $LL'$ is:

$$ \text{Length}(LL') = 2 \cdot y_1 = 2\left(\frac{b^2}{a}\right) = \frac{2b^2}{a} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.4: Focal Distance of Any Point on the Ellipse ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: For any point $P(x_0, y_0)$ on the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$:

$$ PF_1 = a + ex_0 \quad \text{and} \quad PF_2 = a - ex_0 $$

Consequently, $PF_1 + PF_2 = 2a$ identically.

  • Proof: Let $F_1 = (-ae, 0)$ and $F_2 = (ae, 0)$.

$$ PF_2^2 = (x_0 - ae)^2 + y_0^2 = x_0^2 - 2aex_0 + a^2e^2 + y_0^2 $$

Since $P(x_0, y_0)$ lies on the ellipse, $y_0^2 = b^2\left(1 - \frac{x_0^2}{a^2}\right) = (a^2 - a^2e^2)\left(1 - \frac{x_0^2}{a^2}\right)$:

$$ y_0^2 = a^2 - a^2e^2 - x_0^2 + e^2x_0^2 $$

Substitute $y_0^2$ back into $PF_2^2$:

$$ PF_2^2 = x_0^2 - 2aex_0 + a^2e^2 + \left(a^2 - a^2e^2 - x_0^2 + e^2x_0^2\right) $$

$$ PF_2^2 = a^2 - 2aex_0 + e^2x_0^2 = (a - ex_0)^2 $$

Since $x_0 \in [-a, a]$ and $0 < e < 1$, we have $ex_0 < a$, meaning $a - ex_0 > 0$. Taking the positive square root:

$$ PF_2 = a - ex_0 $$

By exact parity of reasoning for $F_1(-ae, 0)$:

$$ PF_1 = a + ex_0 $$

Summing both focal distances:

$$ PF_1 + PF_2 = (a + ex_0) + (a - ex_0) = 2a $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Section 4: High-Yield Solved Problems & Board Marking Schemes

Problem 1: Direct Feature Extraction (NCERT Class 11 Core)

Find the coordinates of the foci, the vertices, the lengths of major and minor axes, the eccentricity, and the length of the latus rectum for the ellipse:

$$ 25x^2 + 9y^2 = 225 $$

Solution:

Step 1: Reduce to Standard Form Divide the given equation by the constant term on the RHS ($225$):

$$ \frac{25x^2}{225} + \frac{9y^2}{225} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 1 $$

(Marking scheme: 1 Mark)

Step 2: Identify Orientation and Parameters Comparing with $\frac{x^2}{b^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2} = 1$: Since the denominator under $y^2$ ($25$) is greater than the denominator under $x^2$ ($9$), the major axis lies along the $y$-axis.

$$ a^2 = 25 \implies a = 5 $$

$$ b^2 = 9 \implies b = 3 $$

(Marking scheme: 1 Mark)

Step 3: Calculate $c$ and Eccentricity $e$

$$ c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = \sqrt{25 - 9} = \sqrt{16} = 4 $$

$$ e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8 $$

(Marking scheme: 1 Mark)

Step 4: Compute All Required Elements - Vertices: $(0, \pm a) = (0, \pm 5)$ - Foci: $(0, \pm c) = (0, \pm 4)$ - Length of Major Axis: $2a = 2(5) = 10$ - Length of Minor Axis: $2b = 2(3) = 6$ - Length of Latus Rectum:

$$ \text{L.R.} = \frac{2b^2}{a} = \frac{2(9)}{5} = \frac{18}{5} = 3.6 $$

(Marking scheme: 1 Mark)

Problem 2: Equation Construction from Geometric Constraints (NCERT Exemplar)

Find the standard equation of an ellipse whose center is at $(0, 0)$, major axis is on the $x$-axis, passes through the point $(4, 3)$, and has focal length equal to $4\sqrt{5}$.

Solution:

Step 1: Formulate the Model Since the major axis lies along the $x$-axis and center is at $(0, 0)$, the standard equation is:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad (a > b) \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)} $$

(Marking scheme: 0.5 Mark)

Step 2: Utilize Given Focal Length Distance between foci is $2c = 4\sqrt{5} \implies c = 2\sqrt{5}$. Using the fundamental identity $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$:

$$ (2\sqrt{5})^2 = a^2 - b^2 \implies 20 = a^2 - b^2 \implies a^2 = b^2 + 20 \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)} $$

(Marking scheme: 1 Mark)

Step 3: Substitute the Point $(4, 3)$ Since $(4, 3)$ lies on the curve:

$$ \frac{4^2}{a^2} + \frac{3^2}{b^2} = 1 \implies \frac{16}{a^2} + \frac{9}{b^2} = 1 \quad \text{--- (Equation 3)} $$

(Marking scheme: 0.5 Mark)

Step 4: Solve the System for $a^2$ and $b^2$ Substitute Equation (2) into Equation (3):

$$ \frac{16}{b^2 + 20} + \frac{9}{b^2} = 1 $$

$$ 16b^2 + 9(b^2 + 20) = b^2(b^2 + 20) $$

$$ 16b^2 + 9b^2 + 180 = (b^2)^2 + 20b^2 $$

$$ (b^2)^2 - 5b^2 - 180 = 0 $$

Factorize the quadratic in terms of $b^2$:

$$ (b^2 - 15)(b^2 + 12) = 0 $$

Since $b^2 > 0$ for any real ellipse, $b^2 \ne -12$. Therefore:

$$ b^2 = 15 $$

From Equation (2):

$$ a^2 = b^2 + 20 = 15 + 20 = 35 $$

(Marking scheme: 1.5 Marks)

Step 5: Final Equation

$$ \frac{x^2}{35} + \frac{y^2}{15} = 1 $$

(Marking scheme: 0.5 Mark)

Problem 3: Advanced Locus & Focal Distance Application (NCERT Exemplar / Competitive Foundation)

An equilateral triangle is inscribed in the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ such that one vertex coincides with one end of the major axis $(a, 0)$. Find the length of each side of this triangle.

Solution:

Step 1: Establish Symmetry Coordinates Let the ellipse be $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$. Let the vertex on the major axis be $A(a, 0)$. By the bilateral symmetry of the ellipse about the $x$-axis, the other two vertices $P$ and $Q$ of the inscribed equilateral triangle must be mirror images across the $x$-axis. Let:

$$ P = (x_1, y_1) \quad \text{and} \quad Q = (x_1, -y_1) \quad \text{with } y_1 > 0 $$

(Marking scheme: 1 Mark)

Step 2: Apply Equilateral Geometry The segment $PQ$ is perpendicular to the $x$-axis, and its length is $2y_1$. The line segment joining $A(a, 0)$ to the midpoint of $PQ$, $(x_1, 0)$, is of length $a - x_1$. In equilateral triangle $\triangle APQ$, the angle made by $AP$ with the $x$-axis is $30^\circ$.

$$ \tan 30^\circ = \frac{y_1}{a - x_1} \implies \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{y_1}{a - x_1} \implies a - x_1 = \sqrt{3}y_1 \implies x_1 = a - \sqrt{3}y_1 $$

(Marking scheme: 1.5 Marks)

Step 3: Impose Ellipse Equation Constraint Since $P(x_1, y_1)$ lies on $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$:

$$ \frac{(a - \sqrt{3}y_1)^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

$$ \frac{a^2 - 2\sqrt{3}ay_1 + 3y_1^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

$$ 1 - \frac{2\sqrt{3}y_1}{a} + \frac{3y_1^2}{a^2} + \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

$$ \left(\frac{3}{a^2} + \frac{1}{b^2}\right)y_1^2 = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{a}y_1 $$

Since $y_1 > 0$, divide both sides by $y_1$:

$$ \left(\frac{3b^2 + a^2}{a^2b^2}\right)y_1 = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{a} $$

$$ y_1 = \frac{2\sqrt{3}ab^2}{a^2 + 3b^2} $$

(Marking scheme: 1.5 Marks)

Step 4: Compute the Side Length of the Triangle The length of a side of the equilateral triangle is the length of segment $PQ = 2y_1$:

$$ \text{Side Length} = 2y_1 = \frac{4\sqrt{3}ab^2}{a^2 + 3b^2} $$

(Marking scheme: 1 Mark)

Section 5: Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Trap 1: Assuming $a$ is Always Paired with $x$

  • The Error: Students blindly write $a^2$ as the denominator under $x^2$. When given $\frac{x^2}{16} + \frac{y^2}{49} = 1$, they incorrectly declare $a = 4, b = 7$.
  • The Correction Rule: In an ellipse, $a$ is strictly defined as the semi-MAJOR axis, hence:

$$ a > b \quad \text{always} $$

The larger denominator is always $a^2$. If the larger denominator is under $y^2$, the ellipse is vertical.

Trap 2: Direct Latus Rectum Swap for Vertical Ellipses

  • The Error: Writing $\text{L.R.} = \frac{2a^2}{b}$ when the ellipse is vertical.
  • The Correction Rule: The length of the latus rectum is always $\frac{2(\text{semi-minor})^2}{\text{semi-major}} = \frac{2b^2}{a}$. It does not flip to $\frac{2a^2}{b}$ because $b$ remains defined as the semi-minor axis.

Trap 3: Sign Confusion with the Hyperbola Identity

  • The Error: Mixing up $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$ (Ellipse) with $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$ (Hyperbola).
  • Mnemonic:
  • Ellipse has a $+$ in its standard equation $\left(\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\right)$ and a $-$ in its focus relationship ($c^2 = a^2 - b^2$).
  • Hyperbola has a $-$ in its standard equation $\left(\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1\right)$ and a $+$ in its focus relationship ($c^2 = a^2 + b^2$).

Trap 4: Forgetting to Normalize the RHS to 1

  • The Error: Given $4x^2 + 9y^2 = 1$, students take $a^2 = 4, b^2 = 9$.
  • The Correction Rule: The coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ in the numerator must be unity ($1$). Rewrite as:

$$ \frac{x^2}{1/4} + \frac{y^2}{1/9} = 1 \implies a^2 = \frac{1}{4}, \quad b^2 = \frac{1}{9} $$

Section 6: Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Competitive Exams

  1. The Area Shortcut:

$$ \text{Area of Ellipse } \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \text{ is } \pi a b $$

(Compare with a circle of radius $r$: $\pi \cdot r \cdot r = \pi r^2$)

  1. Parametric Form Speed Coordinate: Any point on the standard horizontal ellipse can be written with a single parameter $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$:

$$ (x, y) = (a\cos\theta, \; b\sin\theta) $$

Notice: $\frac{(a\cos\theta)^2}{a^2} + \frac{(b\sin\theta)^2}{b^2} = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$.

  1. Perimeter Approximation (Ramanujan's Formula): Unlike a circle, the perimeter of an ellipse cannot be integrated into elementary algebraic functions. A rapid competitive approximation is:

$$ P \approx \pi \left[ 3(a + b) - \sqrt{(3a + b)(a + 3b)} \right] $$

  1. Sum of Focal Distances Constant: If a question states: "A point moves such that the sum of its distances from $(3, 0)$ and $(-3, 0)$ is $10$," identify immediately:

$$ 2c = 6 \implies c = 3 $$

$$ 2a = 10 \implies a = 5 $$

$$ b^2 = a^2 - c^2 = 25 - 9 = 16 \implies \frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{16} = 1 $$

Time taken: under 15 seconds.

  1. Eccentricity Mental Math Trick:

$$ e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{\text{smaller denominator}}{\text{larger denominator}}} $$

Works universally for both horizontal and vertical ellipses without needing to rename variables.

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Geometric Foundations

1.1 Core Geometric Definition (Focal Property) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

A hyperbola is the locus of a point in a plane which moves such that the difference of its distances from two fixed points in the plane is a constant, and this constant is strictly less than the distance between the two fixed points.

Let: * The two fixed points be $F_1$ and $F_2$, termed the foci of the hyperbola. * The distance between the foci be $2c$, so $F_1F_2 = 2c > 0$. * A variable point on the curve be $P(x, y)$.

By definition:

$$ |PF_1 - PF_2| = 2a $$

where $2a$ is a fixed positive constant, and $0 < 2a < 2c \implies a < c$.

Pedagogical Note on the Absolute Value:
The absolute value $|PF_1 - PF_2|$ is mandatory. If $PF_1 - PF_2 = 2a > 0$, point $P$ traces the right branch (points closer to $F_2$). If $PF_1 - PF_2 = -2a \iff PF_2 - PF_1 = 2a$, point $P$ traces the left branch (points closer to $F_1$). Dropping the absolute value gives only a single branch, not the complete hyperbola.

1.2 Focus-Directrix-Eccentricity Definition (Conic Definition) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

A hyperbola is the locus of a point $P$ which moves such that the ratio of its distance from a fixed point (focus $S$) to its perpendicular distance from a fixed straight line (directrix $M$) is a constant $e$, known as the eccentricity, where:

$$ e > 1 $$

$$ \frac{SP}{PM} = e \implies SP = e \cdot PM \quad (e > 1) $$

  • If $e = 0$: Circle
  • If $0 < e < 1$: Ellipse
  • If $e = 1$: Parabola
  • If $e > 1$: Hyperbola
  • If $e = \sqrt{2}$: Rectangular (Equilateral) Hyperbola

2. Rigorous Derivations & Proofs

Proof 2.1: Derivation of the Standard Equation of a Hyperbola ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

Given:

  • Two foci located at $F_1(-c, 0)$ and $F_2(c, 0)$ on the $x$-axis.
  • Constant focal distance difference: $|PF_1 - PF_2| = 2a$, where $0 < a < c$.
  • Variable point $P(x, y)$ lying on the locus.

To Prove:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad \text{where } b^2 = c^2 - a^2 $$

Proof:

Step 1: Express distances using the Cartesian distance formula

$$ PF_1 = \sqrt{(x - (-c))^2 + (y - 0)^2} = \sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} $$

$$ PF_2 = \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + (y - 0)^2} = \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

By the definition of the hyperbola:

$$ PF_1 - PF_2 = \pm 2a $$

$$ \sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} - \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} = \pm 2a $$

Step 2: Isolate one radical and square both sides

$$ \sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2} = \pm 2a + \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

Squaring both sides:

$$ \left(\sqrt{(x + c)^2 + y^2}\right)^2 = \left(\pm 2a + \sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2}\right)^2 $$

$$ (x + c)^2 + y^2 = 4a^2 \pm 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} + (x - c)^2 + y^2 $$

Step 3: Expand the algebraic binomials

$$ x^2 + 2cx + c^2 + y^2 = 4a^2 \pm 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} + x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2 $$

Cancelling identical terms ($x^2, c^2, y^2$) from both sides:

$$ 2cx = 4a^2 \pm 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} - 2cx $$

$$ 4cx - 4a^2 = \pm 4a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

Divide throughout by $4$:

$$ cx - a^2 = \pm a\sqrt{(x - c)^2 + y^2} $$

Step 4: Square both sides a second time to eliminate the remaining radical

$$ (cx - a^2)^2 = a^2 \left((x - c)^2 + y^2\right) $$

$$ c^2x^2 - 2a^2cx + a^4 = a^2 \left(x^2 - 2cx + c^2 + y^2\right) $$

$$ c^2x^2 - 2a^2cx + a^4 = a^2x^2 - 2a^2cx + a^2c^2 + a^2y^2 $$

Cancelling $-2a^2cx$ on both sides:

$$ c^2x^2 + a^4 = a^2x^2 + a^2c^2 + a^2y^2 $$

Step 5: Group coefficients of $x^2$, $y^2$, and constant terms

$$ c^2x^2 - a^2x^2 - a^2y^2 = a^2c^2 - a^4 $$

$$ (c^2 - a^2)x^2 - a^2y^2 = a^2(c^2 - a^2) $$

Step 6: Introduce the parameter $b$
Since $c > a > 0$, we have $c^2 > a^2 \implies c^2 - a^2 > 0$.
Let $b^2 = c^2 - a^2$. Substituting $b^2$:

$$ b^2x^2 - a^2y^2 = a^2b^2 $$

Step 7: Normalise by dividing throughout by $a^2b^2$

$$ \frac{b^2x^2}{a^2b^2} - \frac{a^2y^2}{a^2b^2} = \frac{a^2b^2}{a^2b^2} $$

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Length of the Latus Rectum of a Hyperbola ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given:

  • Standard hyperbola: $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$
  • Right-hand focus at $F_2(c, 0) = (ae, 0)$.
  • Chord $L L'$ passing through $F_2(c, 0)$ and perpendicular to the transverse axis ($x$-axis).

To Prove:

$$ \text{Length}(LL') = \frac{2b^2}{a} $$

Proof:

Step 1: Set up coordinates of the endpoints
Since $LL'$ is perpendicular to the $x$-axis and passes through $(c, 0)$, every point on this line has $x = c = ae$.
Let the coordinates of the upper endpoint be $L(c, y_1)$ where $y_1 > 0$. By symmetry about the $x$-axis, $L'$ is $(c, -y_1)$.
The length of the chord is:

$$ \text{Length}(LL') = y_1 - (-y_1) = 2y_1 $$

Step 2: Substitute $L(c, y_1)$ into the hyperbola equation

$$ \frac{c^2}{a^2} - \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

Step 3: Solve for $y_1$ using the relation $c^2 - a^2 = b^2$

$$ \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = \frac{c^2}{a^2} - 1 = \frac{c^2 - a^2}{a^2} $$

Since $c^2 - a^2 = b^2$:

$$ \frac{y_1^2}{b^2} = \frac{b^2}{a^2} \implies y_1^2 = \frac{b^4}{a^2} $$

Taking the principal positive square root:

$$ y_1 = \frac{b^2}{a} $$

Step 4: Compute full length

$$ \text{Length}(LL') = 2y_1 = \frac{2b^2}{a} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

💡 Example 3.3: Center Lying on a Given Line (NCERT Exemplar Variant) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the equation of the circle passing through the points $(2, 3)$ and $(-1, 1)$, and whose center lies on the straight line $x - 3y - 11 = 0$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Define parameters using the Standard Equation.
    Let the center of the circle be $C(h, k)$ and its radius be $r$.
    The equation is:

$$ (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 $$

  • Step 2: Formulate distance equality.
    Since $P(2, 3)$ and $Q(-1, 1)$ lie on the circle, $CP^2 = CQ^2 = r^2$:

$$ (2 - h)^2 + (3 - k)^2 = (-1 - h)^2 + (1 - k)^2 $$

Expanding both sides:

$$ (4 - 4h + h^2) + (9 - 6k + k^2) = (1 + 2h + h^2) + (1 - 2k + k^2) $$

Cancel $h^2 + k^2$:

$$ 13 - 4h - 6k = 2 + 2h - 2k $$

$$ -6h - 4k + 11 = 0 \implies 6h + 4k = 11 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

[Mark: 2]

  • Step 3: Use the line constraint.
    The center $(h, k)$ lies on $x - 3y - 11 = 0$:

$$ h - 3k = 11 \implies h = 3k + 11 \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

[Mark: 1]

  • Step 4: Solve the linear system for $(h, k)$.
    Substitute (2) into (1):

$$ 6(3k + 11) + 4k = 11 $$

$$ 18k + 66 + 4k = 11 $$

$$ 22k = 11 - 66 = -55 $$

$$ k = -\frac{55}{22} = -\frac{5}{2} $$

Find $h$:

$$ h = 3\left(-\frac{5}{2}\right) + 11 = -\frac{15}{2} + \frac{22}{2} = \frac{7}{2} $$

Thus, Center $C = \left(\frac{7}{2}, -\frac{5}{2}\right)$. [Mark: 1]

  • Step 5: Calculate $r^2$ and state the equation.

$$ r^2 = (2 - h)^2 + (3 - k)^2 = \left(2 - \frac{7}{2}\right)^2 + \left(3 - \left(-\frac{5}{2}\right)\right)^2 $$

$$ r^2 = \left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{11}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{9}{4} + \frac{121}{4} = \frac{130}{4} = \frac{65}{2} $$

The equation of the circle is:

$$ \left(x - \frac{7}{2}\right)^2 + \left(y + \frac{5}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{130}{4} $$

Expanding:

$$ x^2 - 7x + \frac{49}{4} + y^2 + 5y + \frac{25}{4} = \frac{130}{4} $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 - 7x + 5y + \frac{74 - 130}{4} = 0 $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 - 7x + 5y - \frac{56}{4} = 0 \implies x^2 + y^2 - 7x + 5y - 14 = 0 $$

[Mark: 1]

Final Answer:

$$ x^2 + y^2 - 7x + 5y - 14 = 0 $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

⚠️ Trap 1: The Leading Coefficient Blunder

  • Error: When given $2x^2 + 2y^2 + 8x - 10y - 1 = 0$, writing $g = 4$, $f = -5$ and stating Center $= (-4, 5)$.
  • Correction: The formulas Center $= (-g, -f)$ and Radius $= \sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c}$ apply only when the coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ are $+1$. Divide every term by the leading coefficient first:

$$ x^2 + y^2 + 4x - 5y - \frac{1}{2} = 0 \implies g = 2, \; f = -\frac{5}{2} \implies \text{Center} = \left(-2, \frac{5}{2}\right) $$

⚠️ Trap 2: The Sign Inversion Confusion

  • Error: For $(x + 4)^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 25$, reading the center as $(4, -3)$.
  • Correction: The standard form is $(x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2$.
    Rewrite with explicit minus signs: $(x - (-4))^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 5^2 \implies (h, k) = (-4, 3)$.

⚠️ Trap 3: The Constant Term Sign in Radius

  • Error: In $r = \sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c}$, incorrectly writing $+ c$ inside the radical.
  • Correction: If the equation has a negative constant term, such as $-15$, then:

$$ -c = -(-15) = +15 $$

Remember that moving a positive constant $c$ to the right side gives $-c$, so it enters the radical as $-c$.

⚠️ Trap 4: Forgetting to Take the Square Root for Radius

  • Error: Stating the radius of $(x - 1)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 16$ as $r = 16$.
  • Correction: The right-hand side is $r^2$. Therefore, $r = \sqrt{16} = 4$.

⚠️ Trap 5: Overlooking Geometric Degeneracy Conditions

  • Error: Blindly assuming any second-degree equation with $a = b$ and $h = 0$ is a circle.
  • Correction: Always evaluate the discriminant $\Delta_c = g^2 + f^2 - c$:
  • If $\Delta_c < 0$, no real circle exists (the locus has zero real points).
  • If $\Delta_c = 0$, it is a single point, not a curve with an interior area.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Shortcuts (Competitive & Board Verification)

⚡ Shortcut 1: The Direct General-to-Center Shortcut

Given $ax^2 + ay^2 + Bx + Cy + D = 0$:

$$ \text{Center} = \left(-\frac{B}{2a}, -\frac{C}{2a}\right) $$

$$ \text{Radius} = \frac{\sqrt{B^2 + C^2 - 4aD}}{2|a|} $$

Mental Math Example: For $2x^2 + 2y^2 - 8x + 12y - 10 = 0$:
Center $= \left(-\frac{-8}{4}, -\frac{12}{4}\right) = (2, -3)$
Radius $= \frac{\sqrt{64 + 144 - 4(2)(-10)}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{208 + 80}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{288}}{4} = \frac{12\sqrt{2}}{4} = 3\sqrt{2}$.

⚡ Shortcut 2: Circles Tangent to the Coordinate Axes

   y-axis                             y-axis
     |                                  |
     |   C(r, r)                        |   C(h, r)
     |  *---                            |  *
     |  |   |                           |  |
     |--+---+---- x-axis                +--+------- x-axis
     Touching Both Axes                    Touching x-axis
       |h| = |k| = r                         r = |k|
  1. Circle touching the $x$-axis: The radius equals the absolute value of the $y$-coordinate of the center:

$$ r = |k| \implies (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = k^2 \iff x^2 + y^2 - 2hx - 2ky + h^2 = 0 $$

  1. Circle touching the $y$-axis: The radius equals the absolute value of the $x$-coordinate of the center:

$$ r = |h| \implies (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = h^2 \iff x^2 + y^2 - 2hx - 2ky + k^2 = 0 $$

  1. Circle touching both coordinate axes:

$$ |h| = |k| = r $$

Equation in the first quadrant:

$$ (x - r)^2 + (y - r)^2 = r^2 \iff x^2 + y^2 - 2rx - 2ry + r^2 = 0 $$

⚡ Shortcut 3: Intercepts Made by a Circle on the Axes

For the general circle $x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0$: - Length of Intercept on $x$-axis: Set $y = 0 \implies x^2 + 2gx + c = 0$. The roots $x_1, x_2$ are the points of intersection.

$$ \text{Length } = |x_1 - x_2| = \sqrt{(x_1 + x_2)^2 - 4x_1 x_2} = \sqrt{(-2g)^2 - 4c} = 2\sqrt{g^2 - c} $$

  • If $g^2 > c$: Circle intersects the $x$-axis at two distinct real points.
  • If $g^2 = c$: Circle touches the $x$-axis ($x$-intercept $= 0$).

  • If $g^2 < c$: Circle does not meet the $x$-axis.

  • Length of Intercept on $y$-axis: Set $x = 0 \implies y^2 + 2fy + c = 0$.

$$ \text{Length } = |y_1 - y_2| = 2\sqrt{f^2 - c} $$

  • If $f^2 > c$: Circle intersects the $y$-axis at two distinct real points.
  • If $f^2 = c$: Circle touches the $y$-axis.
  • If $f^2 < c$: Circle does not meet the $y$-axis.

⚡ Shortcut 4: Circumcircle of a Right-Angled Triangle

If a triangle is right-angled at vertex $C$: - By Thales' Theorem, the hypotenuse $AB$ must be a diameter of the circumcircle. - Center: Midpoint of hypotenuse $AB = \left(\frac{x_A + x_B}{2}, \frac{y_A + y_B}{2}\right)$. - Radius: Half the length of hypotenuse $= \frac{1}{2}|AB|$. - Skip solving the 3-point simultaneous system; use the Diametric Form directly on $A$ and $B$:

$$ (x - x_A)(x - x_B) + (y - y_A)(y - y_B) = 0 $$

$$ SP = PM $$

$$ SP^2 = (x - 1)^2 + (y - (-1))^2 = (x - 1)^2 + (y + 1)^2 $$

(Step mark: 1.5 Marks)

$$ 2(x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 2y + 2) = [ (x - y) + 2 ]^2 $$

$$ 2x^2 + 2y^2 - 4x + 4y + 4 = (x - y)^2 + 4(x - y) + 4 $$

$$ 2x^2 + 2y^2 - 4x + 4y + 4 = x^2 - 2xy + y^2 + 4x - 4y + 4 $$

(Step mark: 1.5 Marks)

$$ (2x^2 - x^2) + 2xy + (2y^2 - y^2) + (-4x - 4x) + (4y - (-4y)) + (4 - 4) = 0 $$

$$ (x + y)^2 - 8(x - y) = 0 $$

  • Vertex: Set $X = 0, Y = 0$:

$$ x + 1 = 0 \implies x = -1 $$

$$ y - 2 = 0 \implies y = 2 $$

$$ \text{Vertex } V = (-1, 2) $$

  1. Focus: Set $X = a = 2, Y = 0$:

$$ x + 1 = 2 \implies x = 1 $$

$$ y - 2 = 0 \implies y = 2 $$

$$ \text{Focus } S = (1, 2) $$

  1. Directrix: Equation is $X = -a \implies X = -2$:

$$ x + 1 = -2 \implies x = -3 \iff x + 3 = 0 $$

  1. Axis of Symmetry: Equation is $Y = 0$:

$$ y - 2 = 0 \implies y = 2 $$

(Step mark: 2 Marks)

6. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Trap 1: Confusing $a$ with $4a$

  • Error: When given $y^2 = 8x$, students often write $a = 8$ and conclude focus is $(8, 0)$.
  • Correction: The standard equation is $y^2 = 4ax$. Here $4a = 8 \implies a = 2$. The focus is $(2, 0)$, and the latus rectum length is $4a = 8$.

Trap 2: Directrix Sign and Linear Term Direction

  • Error: For $y^2 = -16x$, setting the directrix as $x = -4$.
  • Correction: The curve opens leftward ($x \le 0$). The directrix must lie on the positive side: $x = +a \implies x = 4 \iff x - 4 = 0$. The directrix never intersects the opening mouth of the parabola.

Trap 3: Swapping Coordinate Axes for Vertical Parabolas

  • Error: For $x^2 = 12y$, writing the focus as $(3, 0)$ and directrix as $x = -3$.
  • Correction: The squared term is $x$, meaning symmetry is along the $y$-axis. The focus must be on the $y$-axis: $(0, a) = (0, 3)$. The directrix must be a horizontal line: $y = -3$.

Trap 4: Treating the Parameter $a$ as Negative

  • Error: When solving $y^2 = -20x$, writing $-4a = -20 \implies a = -5$, and calculating the length of latus rectum as $4a = -20$.
  • Correction: $a$ is strictly a geometric distance:

$$ a > 0 $$

For $y^2 = -4ax$, $4a = 20 \implies a = 5$. Length of Latus Rectum is an absolute length: $|4a| = 20$ units.

Trap 5: Forgetting Square Roots Produce Pairs of Points

  • Error: When finding the extremities of the latus rectum for $y^2 = 4ax$, writing only $L(a, 2a)$ and omitting $L'(a, -2a)$.
  • Correction: Solving $y^2 = 4a^2$ yields $y = \pm 2a$. A latus rectum has two distinct endpoints.

7. Speed Hacks, Mnemonics & Golden Rules

The "First Degree Variable" Master Rule

Look at the variable with degree 1 (the non-squared variable): 1. It dictates the axis of symmetry. 2. Its sign dictates the direction in which the parabola opens.

  • In $y^2 = -8x$: Degree 1 variable is $x$ with negative sign $\implies$ Axis is $x$-axis, opens toward the negative $x$-direction (left).
  • In $x^2 = 12y$: Degree 1 variable is $y$ with positive sign $\implies$ Axis is $y$-axis, opens toward the positive $y$-direction (upward).

The Midpoint Property of the Vertex

The vertex $V$ is ALWAYS the exact midpoint of the line segment joining the focus $S$ and the foot of the directrix $Z$ on the axis:

$$ V = \frac{S + Z}{2} $$

Quick Check Hack:
If an exam question gives Focus $S(4, 0)$ and Directrix $x = -4$ (which meets axis at $Z(-4, 0)$):

$$ \text{Midpoint} = \left(\frac{4 + (-4)}{2}, \frac{0 + 0}{2}\right) = (0, 0) = \text{Vertex} $$

This verifies the parabola is in standard position immediately without plotting.

Focal Distance Mental Math Shortcut

For any point on: - $y^2 = 4ax \implies \text{Focal Distance} = x\text{-coordinate} + a$ - $x^2 = 4ay \implies \text{Focal Distance} = y\text{-coordinate} + a$

No distance formula square-roots needed.

💡 Example 4.1: Canonical Analysis from General Form
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the coordinates of the foci and vertices, the eccentricity, and the length of the latus rectum for the hyperbola:

$$ 9x^2 - 16y^2 = 144 $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Reduce to standard form
    Divide both sides by the constant term $144$:

$$ \frac{9x^2}{144} - \frac{16y^2}{144} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{16} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1 $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 2: Identify parameters and orientation
    The positive term is on $x^2$, so the transverse axis lies along the $x$-axis.
    Comparing with $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$:

$$ a^2 = 16 \implies a = 4 $$

$$ b^2 = 9 \implies b = 3 $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 3: Calculate $c$ and eccentricity $e$

$$ c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 5 $$

$$ e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{5}{4} = 1.25 $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 4: Coordinates of vertices and foci

$$ \text{Vertices} = (\pm a, 0) = (\pm 4, 0) $$

$$ \text{Foci} = (\pm c, 0) = (\pm 5, 0) $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 5: Length of latus rectum

$$ \text{Length} = \frac{2b^2}{a} = \frac{2(9)}{4} = \frac{18}{4} = \frac{9}{2} $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

💡 Example 4.2: Vertical Transverse Axis Case
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the standard equation of the hyperbola having vertices at $(0, \pm 5)$ and foci at $(0, \pm 8)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Determine orientation
    The vertices and foci both lie on the $y$-axis (since the $x$-coordinates are identically zero).
    Therefore, the transverse axis is along the $y$-axis, and the equation is of the form:

$$ \frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 2: Determine $a$ and $c$
    Vertices are $(0, \pm a) = (0, \pm 5) \implies a = 5 \implies a^2 = 25$.
    Foci are $(0, \pm c) = (0, \pm 8) \implies c = 8 \implies c^2 = 64$.
    (Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 3: Compute $b^2$
    Using the relation $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$:

$$ b^2 = c^2 - a^2 = 64 - 25 = 39 $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 4: Formulate equation
    Substituting $a^2 = 25$ and $b^2 = 39$:

$$ \frac{y^2}{25} - \frac{x^2}{39} = 1 $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

💡 Example 4.3: NCERT Exemplar — Determining Equation from Latus Rectum & Eccentricity
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the equation of the hyperbola with foci on the $x$-axis, centred at $(0, 0)$, passing through the point $(4, 3)$, and having length of latus rectum equal to $12$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Formulate the model equation
    Since the foci lie on the $x$-axis:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 2: Use the Latus Rectum condition

$$ \frac{2b^2}{a} = 12 \implies b^2 = 6a $$

(Step Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Step 3: Substitute $(4, 3)$ and $b^2 = 6a$ into the hyperbola equation

$$ \frac{4^2}{a^2} - \frac{3^2}{6a} = 1 \implies \frac{16}{a^2} - \frac{9}{6a} = 1 \implies \frac{16}{a^2} - \frac{3}{2a} = 1 $$

Clear denominators by multiplying the entire equation by $2a^2$ ($a \neq 0$):

$$ 32 - 3a = 2a^2 \implies 2a^2 + 3a - 32 = 0 $$

(Step Marking: 1.5 Marks)

  • Step 4: Solve the quadratic equation for $a$
    Using the quadratic formula:

$$ a = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{3^2 - 4(2)(-32)}}{2(2)} = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 256}}{4} = \frac{-3 \pm \sqrt{265}}{4} $$

Since $a$ must be positive:

$$ a = \frac{\sqrt{265} - 3}{4} $$

Then:

$$ b^2 = 6a = \frac{6(\sqrt{265} - 3)}{4} = \frac{3(\sqrt{265} - 3)}{2} $$

$$ a^2 = \frac{265 - 6\sqrt{265} + 9}{16} = \frac{274 - 6\sqrt{265}}{16} = \frac{137 - 3\sqrt{265}}{8} $$

Substitute $a^2$ and $b^2$ back into $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ to complete the solution.
(Step Marking: 1.5 Marks)

5. Examiner Traps, Misconceptions & Diagnostic Alerts

Trap 1: Confusing "Which Axis is Which" via Magnitude of Denominators

  • The Error: In an ellipse, the larger denominator is always $a^2$ ($a > b$). Students carry this habit into hyperbolas and assume $a^2 > b^2$ must hold.
  • The Reality: For a hyperbola, $a$ can be greater than, equal to, or strictly less than $b$:
  • $a > b$: e.g., $\frac{x^2}{16} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$
  • $a < b$: e.g., $\frac{x^2}{9} - \frac{y^2}{25} = 1$ (Here $a=3, b=5$; transverse axis is still the $x$-axis!)
  • $a = b$: $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{a^2} = 1$ (Rectangular Hyperbola, $e = \sqrt{2}$)
  • The Rule: The transverse axis is dictated solely by the positive term, never by which denominator is larger.

Trap 2: The Sign in the Fundamental Relation

$$ \text{Ellipse: } c^2 = a^2 - b^2 \quad \text{vs.} \quad \text{Hyperbola: } c^2 = a^2 + b^2 $$

  • The Error: Swapping the minus and plus signs when calculating $c$ or $e$.
  • Mnemonic: In the hyperbola equation there is a minus ($\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$), so in the auxiliary relation there is a plus ($c^2 = a^2 + b^2$). They are sign opposites!

Trap 3: Writing the Vertical Hyperbola in Incorrect Order

  • Writing $\frac{x^2}{b^2} - \frac{y^2}{a^2} = 1$ and calling it a vertical hyperbola.
  • The Reality: If $x^2$ has the positive sign, its vertices are on the $x$-axis, so it is horizontal. A vertical hyperbola must have $y^2$ positive:

$$ \frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

Trap 4: Forgetting the Boundary Condition on $e$

  • For any hyperbola, $e = \frac{c}{a} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2}} > 1$.
  • If an algebraic calculation yields $e \le 1$, check for swapped axes or dropped negative signs immediately.

6. Speed Hacks, Shortcuts & Golden Rules

1. Conjugate Hyperbola Eccentricity Relation

If $e_1$ is the eccentricity of a standard hyperbola $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ and $e_2$ is the eccentricity of its conjugate hyperbola $\frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{x^2}{a^2} = 1$, then:

$$ \frac{1}{e_1^2} + \frac{1}{e_2^2} = 1 $$

Proof in 2 steps:

$$ e_1^2 = 1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2} = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{a^2} \implies \frac{1}{e_1^2} = \frac{a^2}{a^2 + b^2} $$

$$ e_2^2 = 1 + \frac{a^2}{b^2} = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{b^2} \implies \frac{1}{e_2^2} = \frac{b^2}{a^2 + b^2} $$

$$ \frac{1}{e_1^2} + \frac{1}{e_2^2} = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{a^2 + b^2} = 1 $$

2. The Rectangular Hyperbola Shortcut

When $a = b$: * Equation: $x^2 - y^2 = a^2$ * Eccentricity is always constant:

$$ e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{a^2}{a^2}} = \sqrt{2} $$

  • Length of latus rectum equals the length of transverse axis:

$$ \text{L.R.} = \frac{2b^2}{a} = \frac{2a^2}{a} = 2a $$

3. Quick Focal Distance Formula

For any point $P(x_1, y_1)$ on $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$: * Distance to right-hand focus $F_2(ae, 0)$: $PF_2 = |ex_1 - a|$ * Distance to left-hand focus $F_1(-ae, 0)$: $PF_1 = |ex_1 + a|$ * Difference: $|PF_1 - PF_2| = |(ex_1 + a) - (ex_1 - a)| = 2a$ (verifying the focal property instantly).

Context & Scenario:

An alternative energy laboratory is engineering a parabolic reflector trough to focus incoming parallel solar radiation onto a high-efficiency fluid conduit pipe. The cross-section of the reflector forms a parabola symmetric about the vertical $y$-axis with its vertex positioned at the origin $(0, 0)$.

The aperture (top opening) across the rim has a total width of $12\text{ m}$, and the depth of the reflector at its center is $3\text{ m}$. Engineers need to accurately position the absorber pipe at the focal line to ensure maximum thermal absorption without optical dispersion losses.

                  y
                  |         (6, 3)
      (-6, 3) *---|---* 
           \      |      /
             \    | F   /
              \   | .  /
               \  |   /
                 \_|_/
                  O------> x  (Vertex at origin)

Sub-Questions:

  1. Find the standard equation of the parabolic profile representing the cross-section of the reflector. (1 Mark)
  2. Calculate the exact coordinates of the focus where the solar fluid conduit must be installed. (1 Mark)
  3. Determine the focal distance and the width of the parabolic reflector at the level of the focus (length of latus rectum). (1 Mark)
  4. If the absorber pipe requires maintenance clearance, find the vertical depth of the reflector at a point situated horizontally $4\text{ m}$ away from the central axis of symmetry. (1 Mark)
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Part (1): Standard Equation of the Parabola
  • The parabola has its vertex at $(0, 0)$, opens upwards along the positive $y$-axis, and is symmetric about the $y$-axis.
  • Standard form:

$$ x^2 = 4ay $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for identifying standard upward parabola equation)*
  • The top aperture width is $12\text{ m}$, so horizontal span extends from $x = -6$ to $x = 6$. The depth is $3\text{ m}$, meaning the rim points are $(\pm 6, 3)$.
  • Substituting $(6, 3)$ into the standard equation:

$$ (6)^2 = 4a(3) \implies 36 = 12a \implies a = 3\text{ m} $$

  • Therefore, the equation of the parabolic profile is:

$$ x^2 = 4(3)y \implies x^2 = 12y $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for determining $a$ and writing $x^2 = 12y$)*
  • Part (2): Coordinates of the Focus
  • For an upward parabola $x^2 = 4ay$, the focus is located at $F(0, a)$.
  • Since $a = 3$, the focus coordinates are:

$$ \text{Focus } F = (0, 3) $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for stating $F(0, a)$ and concluding $(0, 3)$)*
  • Part (3): Focal Distance & Length of Latus Rectum
  • Focal distance from the vertex:

$$ \text{Focal Distance } = a = 3\text{ m} $$

  • The width of the reflector at the focal level is given by the length of the latus rectum:

$$ \text{Length of Latus Rectum } (LLR) = 4a = 4(3) = 12\text{ m} $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for focal distance, $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for $LLR = 12\text{ m}$)*
  • Part (4): Depth at Horizontal Distance of $4\text{ m}$
  • Let the point on the reflector at horizontal distance $x = 4\text{ m}$ be $(4, y_1)$.
  • Substitute $x = 4$ into $x^2 = 12y$:

$$ (4)^2 = 12y_1 \implies 16 = 12y_1 \implies y_1 = \frac{16}{12} = \frac{4}{3}\text{ m} \approx 1.33\text{ m} $$

  • Thus, the vertical depth from the base at $4\text{ m}$ horizontally from the center is $\frac{4}{3}\text{ m}$. (CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for substitution and algebraic evaluation $\frac{4}{3}\text{ m}$)

Context & Scenario:

An architectural design firm is constructing an acoustical museum hall known as a "Whisper Gallery". The ceiling cross-section is shaped as a semi-ellipse. An elliptical whisper gallery has the physical property that sound emitted from one focus reflects off the curved ceiling directly into the other focus.

The hall has a total floor span (major axis) of $30\text{ m}$ and a maximum ceiling height at the center of $9\text{ m}$. The floor corresponds to the major axis, with the center of the ellipse located at the floor midpoint $(0, 0)$.

                       y (Ceiling)

              *        |        *
          *            |            *
      *     F1         |         F2    *
     ---|---|----------+----------|---|---> x (Floor)

  2. Find the eccentricity ($e$) of the elliptical archway. (1 Mark)
  3. Determine the exact distance between the two listening stations located at the foci $F_1$ and $F_2$. (1 Mark)

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Part (1): Equation of the Ellipse
  • Center is at $(0, 0)$, major axis along $x$-axis, minor axis along $y$-axis:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad (y \ge 0) $$

  • Total floor span: $2a = 30 \implies a = 15\text{ m}$.
  • Maximum central height: $b = 9\text{ m}$.
  • Equation:

$$ \frac{x^2}{15^2} + \frac{y^2}{9^2} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{225} + \frac{y^2}{81} = 1 $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for identifying parameters $a=15, b=9$; $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for final equation)*
  • Part (2): Eccentricity of the Ellipse
  • Eccentricity relation:

$$ c^2 = a^2 - b^2 \implies c = \sqrt{15^2 - 9^2} = \sqrt{225 - 81} = \sqrt{144} = 12\text{ m} $$

  • Eccentricity $e$:

$$ e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{12}{15} = \frac{4}{5} = 0.8 $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$; $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for $e = \frac{4}{5}$)*
  • Part (3): Distance Between the Listening Stations
  • The listening stations are at the two foci $F_1(-c, 0)$ and $F_2(c, 0)$.
  • Distance between foci:

$$ 2c = 2ae = 2(12) = 24\text{ m} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for formula $2c = 2ae$ and final answer $24\text{ m}$)*
  • Part (4): Ceiling Height at $x = 5\text{ m}$
  • Substitute $x = 5$ into the ellipse equation:

$$ \frac{5^2}{225} + \frac{y^2}{81} = 1 \implies \frac{25}{225} + \frac{y^2}{81} = 1 \implies \frac{1}{9} + \frac{y^2}{81} = 1 $$

  • Rearranging:

$$ \frac{y^2}{81} = 1 - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{8}{9} $$

$$ y^2 = 81 \times \frac{8}{9} = 72 \implies y = \sqrt{72} = 6\sqrt{2}\text{ m} $$

  • Numerically:

$$ y \approx 6 \times 1.414 = 8.484\text{ m} $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for substitution; $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for exact simplified radical form $6\sqrt{2}\text{ m}$)*

Question A.3: Case Study — LORAN Long-Range Hyperbolic Navigation System

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, Case Study, 4 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [High-Yield]

Context & Scenario:

In maritime radio navigation, two transmitting stations $T_1$ and $T_2$ located on a coastline simultaneously broadcast synchronized radio signals at speed $c = 300\text{ m/\mu s}$. A container vessel out at sea determines that the radio signal from $T_1$ arrives $40\text{ }\mu\text{s}$ earlier than the signal from $T_2$.

Because the difference in arrival times corresponds to a constant difference in distances from the two fixed stations (foci), the vessel's path is constrained to a branch of a hyperbola.

The stations are positioned along the $x$-axis at $(-10000\text{ m}, 0)$ and $(10000\text{ m}, 0)$ respectively.

Sub-Questions:

  1. Find the constant path difference $2a$ in meters between the distances of the ship from stations $T_1$ and $T_2$. (1 Mark)
  2. Formulate the standard Cartesian equation of the hyperbola representing the ship's line of position. (1 Mark)
  3. Calculate the eccentricity of this hyperbolic trajectory. (1 Mark)
  4. If the vessel continues along this hyperbola until its $y$-coordinate is $8000\text{ m}$, calculate its corresponding $x$-coordinate. (1 Mark)
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Part (1): Constant Path Difference
  • Difference in travel time $\Delta t = 40\text{ }\mu\text{s} = 40 \times 10^{-6}\text{ s}$.
  • Distance difference:

$$ 2a = c \cdot \Delta t = (300\text{ m/\mu s}) \times (40\text{ }\mu\text{s}) = 12000\text{ m} $$

  • Therefore:

$$ a = 6000\text{ m} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for correctly applying $2a = \Delta d = 12000\text{ m}$ and finding $a = 6000\text{ m}$)*
  • Part (2): Equation of the Hyperbola
  • The foci are $T_1(-c, 0)$ and $T_2(c, 0)$ where $c = 10000\text{ m}$.
  • Hyperbolic focal relation:

$$ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \implies b^2 = c^2 - a^2 $$

$$ b^2 = (10000)^2 - (6000)^2 = 100000000 - 36000000 = 64000000 = (8000)^2 $$

$$ b = 8000\text{ m} $$

  • Standard equation of hyperbola:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{(6000)^2} - \frac{y^2}{(8000)^2} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{36 \times 10^6} - \frac{y^2}{64 \times 10^6} = 1 $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for computing $b^2 = 64 \times 10^6$; $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for writing standard form)*
  • Part (3): Eccentricity
  • Eccentricity $e$:

$$ e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{10000}{6000} = \frac{5}{3} > 1 $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for $e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{5}{3}$)*
  • Part (4): Determining Position at $y = 8000\text{ m}$
  • Substitute $y = 8000$ into the hyperbola equation:

$$ \frac{x^2}{(6000)^2} - \frac{(8000)^2}{(8000)^2} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{(6000)^2} - 1 = 1 $$

$$ \frac{x^2}{(6000)^2} = 2 \implies x^2 = 2 \times (6000)^2 $$

$$ x = \pm 6000\sqrt{2}\text{ m} $$

  • Since signal from $T_1(-10000, 0)$ arrives earlier, ship is closer to $T_1$, so $x = -6000\sqrt{2}\text{ m}$. (CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for $x^2 = 7.2 \times 10^7$; $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for selecting appropriate branch $x = -6000\sqrt{2}\text{ m}$)

Question A.4: Assertion-Reason — Eccentricity & Conic Degeneration

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

  • Assertion (A): The eccentricity of a circle is $0$, and as the eccentricity of an ellipse increases towards $1$, the ellipse becomes increasingly elongated and flattened.

  • Reason (R): For any central conic section, eccentricity is given by $e = \frac{c}{a}$, where $c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$. When $b \to a$, $c \to 0 \implies e \to 0$, producing a circle.

  • Answer: (a) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

  • Explanation:
  • (A) is true: When $e = 0$, $c = 0 \implies b = a$, which yields a circle $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$. As $e \to 1^-$, $\frac{b^2}{a^2} = 1 - e^2 \to 0$, so $b \ll a$, making the ellipse extremely flat/elongated.
  • (R) is true: The mathematical ratio $e = \frac{c}{a} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}}$ directly provides the mechanical justification for the geometric transformation stated in (A).

Question A.5: Assertion-Reason — Focal Distance Property of Ellipse

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

  • Assertion (A): For any point $P(x_1, y_1)$ lying on the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ ($a > b$), the sum of its focal distances $SP + S'P$ is always a constant equal to $2a$.

  • Reason (R): The distance of any point on the ellipse from the focus is directly proportional to its perpendicular distance from the corresponding directrix with proportionality constant $e < 1$.

  • Answer: (a) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A).

  • Explanation:
  • By the focus-directrix definition of an ellipse: $SP = e \cdot PM_1$ and $S'P = e \cdot PM_2$, where $PM_1$ and $PM_2$ are perpendicular distances to directrices $x = \frac{a}{e}$ and $x = -\frac{a}{e}$.
  • $PM_1 = \frac{a}{e} - x_1$ and $PM_2 = x_1 - \left(-\frac{a}{e}\right) = \frac{a}{e} + x_1$.
  • Thus:

$$ SP + S'P = e\left(\frac{a}{e} - x_1\right) + e\left(\frac{a}{e} + x_1\right) = (a - ex_1) + (a + ex_1) = 2a $$

  • Hence, (R) directly derives and explains (A).

Question A.6: Assertion-Reason — Transverse Axis Identification

  • Assertion (A): In the hyperbola $\frac{y^2}{16} - \frac{x^2}{9} = 1$, the length of the transverse axis is $6$.

  • Reason (R): For any hyperbola $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ or $\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1$, the transverse axis is always associated with the positive squared term.

  • Answer: (d) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

  • Explanation:
  • In the hyperbola $\frac{y^2}{16} - \frac{x^2}{9} = 1$, the positive term is $\frac{y^2}{16}$, which means the transverse axis lies along the $y$-axis.
  • Here, $a^2 = 16 \implies a = 4$.
  • The length of the transverse axis is $2a = 2(4) = 8$ (not $6$). Hence, Assertion (A) is false.
  • Reason (R) states the standard mathematical definition correctly: the transverse axis is determined by the variable possessing the positive coefficient in standard form.

Question A.7: Equilateral Triangle Inscribed in a Parabola

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, HOTS, 4 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: An equilateral triangle is inscribed inside the parabola $y^2 = 4ax$ such that one of its vertices coincides with the vertex of the parabola. Find the side length of this equilateral triangle and determine the coordinates of its other two vertices.

                   y
                   |      A (x1, y1)
                   |     *
                   |    /|
                   |   / |
                   |  /  |
        -----------O-----+---------> x
                   |  \  |
                   |   \ |
                   |    \|
                   |     *
                   |      B (x1, -y1)
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Set up symmetry and coordinates
  • Let the parabola be $y^2 = 4ax$. Its vertex is at $O(0, 0)$.
  • Let $\triangle OAB$ be the inscribed equilateral triangle with vertex $O(0, 0)$.
  • Due to the symmetry of the parabola $y^2 = 4ax$ about the $x$-axis, the line segment $AB$ must be perpendicular to the $x$-axis.
  • Thus, the $x$-coordinates of $A$ and $B$ are identical: let $x_1 > 0$.
  • The coordinates of vertices are:

$$ A(x_1, y_1) \quad \text{and} \quad B(x_1, -y_1) \quad (y_1 > 0) $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for utilizing parabola symmetry to assign coordinates $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_1, -y_1)$)*
  • Step 2: Apply geometric properties of the equilateral triangle
  • In $\triangle OAB$, the angle $\angle AOB = 60^\circ$.
  • Since the $x$-axis bisects $\angle AOB$, the angle that the line segment $OA$ makes with the positive $x$-axis is:

$$ \theta = \frac{60^\circ}{2} = 30^\circ $$

  • The slope of the line $OA$ is:

$$ \tan 30^\circ = \frac{y_1 - 0}{x_1 - 0} \implies \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{y_1}{x_1} \implies x_1 = \sqrt{3}y_1 $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for finding relation $x_1 = \sqrt{3}y_1$ using trigonometry)*
  • Step 3: Solve simultaneously with the parabola equation
  • Since point $A(x_1, y_1)$ lies on $y^2 = 4ax$:

$$ y_1^2 = 4a x_1 $$

  • Substitute $x_1 = \sqrt{3}y_1$:

$$ y_1^2 = 4a(\sqrt{3}y_1) $$

  • Since $y_1 \neq 0$ (as $A$ is distinct from the origin):

$$ y_1 = 4\sqrt{3}a $$

  • Then calculate $x_1$:

$$ x_1 = \sqrt{3}y_1 = \sqrt{3}(4\sqrt{3}a) = 12a $$

  • Coordinates of the vertices:

$$ A = (12a, 4\sqrt{3}a), \quad B = (12a, -4\sqrt{3}a) $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for computing $x_1 = 12a$ and $y_1 = 4\sqrt{3}a$)*
  • Step 4: Compute side length of the equilateral triangle
  • The side length $L$ equals the length of $AB$:

$$ L = AB = y_1 - (-y_1) = 2y_1 = 2(4\sqrt{3}a) = 8\sqrt{3}a $$

  • Alternatively, check length $OA$:

$$ OA = \sqrt{x_1^2 + y_1^2} = \sqrt{(12a)^2 + (4\sqrt{3}a)^2} = \sqrt{144a^2 + 48a^2} = \sqrt{192a^2} = 8\sqrt{3}a $$

  • Final Answer: Side length of the equilateral triangle is $8\sqrt{3}a\text{ units}$. (CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for determining length $8\sqrt{3}a$ with verification)

Question A.8: Common Chord & Intersection Analysis (Circle & Parabola)

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, HOTS, 5 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: A circle has its center at the focus of the parabola $y^2 = 8x$ and is tangent to the directrix of the parabola. 1. Determine the equation of the circle. (2 Marks) 2. Find the coordinates of the points of intersection of this circle and the parabola. (2 Marks) 3. Calculate the length of the common chord connecting these points of intersection. (1 Mark)

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Determine the equation of the circle
  • Parabola equation: $y^2 = 8x \implies 4a = 8 \implies a = 2$.
  • Focus of the parabola $S = (a, 0) = (2, 0)$.
  • Equation of the directrix: $x = -a \implies x = -2 \implies x + 2 = 0$.
  • The center of the circle is at the focus $C(2, 0)$.
  • Since the circle is tangent to the directrix, the radius $R$ is the perpendicular distance from $(2, 0)$ to the line $x = -2$:

$$ R = |2 - (-2)| = 4\text{ units} $$

  • Standard equation of the circle with center $(2, 0)$ and radius $R = 4$:

$$ (x - 2)^2 + (y - 0)^2 = 4^2 \implies (x - 2)^2 + y^2 = 16 $$

$$ x^2 - 4x + 4 + y^2 = 16 \implies x^2 - 4x + y^2 - 12 = 0 $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for center and radius determination; $1\text{ Mark}$ for expanding standard circle equation)*
  • Step 2: Find points of intersection
  • Substitute $y^2 = 8x$ from the parabola into the circle equation:

$$ (x - 2)^2 + 8x = 16 $$

$$ x^2 - 4x + 4 + 8x = 16 $$

$$ x^2 + 4x + 4 = 16 \implies (x + 2)^2 = 16 $$

  • Taking square roots:

$$ x + 2 = \pm 4 \implies x = 4 - 2 = 2 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -4 - 2 = -6 $$

  • Since the parabola is $y^2 = 8x$, we must have $x \ge 0$.
  • Therefore, $x = -6$ is inadmissible (extraneous root).
  • Accepting $x = 2$:

$$ y^2 = 8(2) = 16 \implies y = \pm 4 $$

  • Points of intersection:

$$ P(2, 4) \quad \text{and} \quad Q(2, -4) $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for quadratic formation and rejecting extraneous root; $1\text{ Mark}$ for evaluating intersection coordinates)*
  • Step 3: Length of common chord
  • The common chord connects $P(2, 4)$ and $Q(2, -4)$.
  • Since both points share $x = 2$, the chord is a vertical line segment:

$$ \text{Length of Common Chord } PQ = |4 - (-4)| = 8\text{ units} $$

  • (Notice: The common chord is the latus rectum of the parabola $y^2 = 8x$). (CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for final chord length computation)

Question B.1: Circle Passing Through Three Non-Collinear Points

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exercise Standard, 5 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: Find the equation of the circle passing through the three points $(2, 3)$, $(-1, 1)$, and $(4, 1)$. Also, identify its center and radius.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: General Equation Representation
  • The general equation of a circle is:

$$ x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for writing the general equation)*
  • Step 2: Setting up three simultaneous equations
  • Point $(2, 3)$ lies on the circle:

$$ (2)^2 + (3)^2 + 2g(2) + 2f(3) + c = 0 \implies 4 + 9 + 4g + 6f + c = 0 $$

$$ 4g + 6f + c = -13 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

  • Point $(-1, 1)$ lies on the circle:

$$ (-1)^2 + (1)^2 + 2g(-1) + 2f(1) + c = 0 \implies 1 + 1 - 2g + 2f + c = 0 $$

$$ -2g + 2f + c = -2 \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

  • Point $(4, 1)$ lies on the circle:

$$ (4)^2 + (1)^2 + 2g(4) + 2f(1) + c = 0 \implies 16 + 1 + 8g + 2f + c = 0 $$

$$ 8g + 2f + c = -17 \quad \text{--- (3)} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\frac{1}{2}\text{ Marks}$ ($\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for each properly simplified linear equation))*
  • Step 3: Eliminate constant $c$ to solve for $g$ and $f$
  • Subtract Eq (2) from Eq (3):

$$ (8g + 2f + c) - (-2g + 2f + c) = -17 - (-2) $$

$$ 10g = -15 \implies g = -\frac{15}{10} = -\frac{3}{2} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for finding $g = -\frac{3}{2}$)*
  • Subtract Eq (2) from Eq (1):

$$ (4g + 6f + c) - (-2g + 2f + c) = -13 - (-2) $$

$$ 6g + 4f = -11 $$

  • Substitute $g = -\frac{3}{2}$:

$$ 6\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) + 4f = -11 \implies -9 + 4f = -11 \implies 4f = -2 \implies f = -\frac{1}{2} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for finding $f = -\frac{1}{2}$)*
  • Step 4: Determine $c$, Center, and Radius
  • Substitute $g$ and $f$ into Eq (2):

$$ -2\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) + 2\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) + c = -2 $$

$$ 3 - 1 + c = -2 \implies 2 + c = -2 \implies c = -4 $$

  • Center:

$$ C(-g, -f) = \left(\frac{3}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\right) $$

  • Radius:

$$ r = \sqrt{g^2 + f^2 - c} = \sqrt{\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right)^2 + \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 - (-4)} = \sqrt{\frac{9}{4} + \frac{1}{4} + 4} = \sqrt{\frac{10}{4} + 4} = \sqrt{\frac{26}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{26}}{2} $$

  • Circle equation:

$$ x^2 + y^2 - 3x - y - 4 = 0 $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for determining $c = -4$, center, radius, and final equation)*

Question B.2: Parabola with Given Focus and Directrix (General Slanted Directrix)

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Miscellaneous/Critical, 4 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: Find the equation of the parabola whose focus is at $S(1, -1)$ and whose directrix is the line $x - y + 3 = 0$. Also find the length of its latus rectum.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Apply Focus-Directrix Definition of Parabola
  • By definition, any point $P(x, y)$ on the parabola satisfies:

$$ SP = PM $$

where $SP$ is the distance from focus $S(1, -1)$ and $PM$ is the perpendicular distance from $P(x, y)$ to the directrix $x - y + 3 = 0$.
*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for stating the fundamental focus-directrix definition $SP^2 = PM^2$)*
  • Step 2: Formulate the distance expressions
  • Distance to focus:

$$ SP = \sqrt{(x - 1)^2 + (y - (-1))^2} = \sqrt{(x - 1)^2 + (y + 1)^2} $$

  • Perpendicular distance to line $Ax + By + C = 0$:

$$ PM = \frac{|x - y + 3|}{\sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2}} = \frac{|x - y + 3|}{\sqrt{2}} $$

  • Equating $SP^2 = PM^2$:

$$ (x - 1)^2 + (y + 1)^2 = \frac{(x - y + 3)^2}{2} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for setting up algebraic distance expressions)*
  • Step 3: Algebraic Expansion and Simplification
  • Expand Left Hand Side:

$$ (x^2 - 2x + 1) + (y^2 + 2y + 1) = x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 2y + 2 $$

  • Expand Right Hand Side using $(a + b + c)^2 = a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + 2ab + 2bc + 2ca$:

$$ \frac{x^2 + (-y)^2 + 3^2 + 2(x)(-y) + 2(-y)(3) + 2(x)(3)}{2} = \frac{x^2 + y^2 + 9 - 2xy - 6y + 6x}{2} $$

  • Cross-multiply by $2$:

$$ 2(x^2 + y^2 - 2x + 2y + 2) = x^2 + y^2 - 2xy + 6x - 6y + 9 $$

$$ 2x^2 + 2y^2 - 4x + 4y + 4 = x^2 + y^2 - 2xy + 6x - 6y + 9 $$

  • Group all terms on the left:

$$ x^2 + 2xy + y^2 - 10x + 10y - 5 = 0 $$

$$ (x + y)^2 - 10x + 10y - 5 = 0 $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for correct algebraic expansion and reduction to standard general quadratic form)*
  • Step 4: Compute the Length of Latus Rectum
  • The perpendicular distance from focus $S(1, -1)$ to the directrix $x - y + 3 = 0$ is $2a$:

$$ 2a = \frac{|1 - (-1) + 3|}{\sqrt{1^2 + (-1)^2}} = \frac{|1 + 1 + 3|}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{2}} $$

  • Length of Latus Rectum $= 4a = 2 \times (2a)$:

$$ \text{LLR} = 2 \times \frac{5}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{2}} = 5\sqrt{2}\text{ units} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for finding perpendicular distance $2a$ and calculating $\text{LLR} = 5\sqrt{2}$)*

Question B.3: Equation of Ellipse from Geometric Boundary Conditions

Problem Statement: Find the equation of the ellipse whose center is at $(0, 0)$, major axis is along the $x$-axis, and which passes through the points $(4, 3)$ and $(6, 2)$.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Write the standard equation
  • Since the major axis is along the $x$-axis and center is at $(0, 0)$:

$$ \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \quad (a > b) $$

  • Let $u = \frac{1}{a^2}$ and $v = \frac{1}{b^2}$. The equation becomes:

$$ x^2 u + y^2 v = 1 $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for standard form)*
  • Step 2: Substitute given points
  • Point $(4, 3)$ lies on the ellipse:

$$ 4^2 u + 3^2 v = 1 \implies 16u + 9v = 1 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

  • Point $(6, 2)$ lies on the ellipse:

$$ 6^2 u + 2^2 v = 1 \implies 36u + 4v = 1 \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for formulating linear system in $u$ and $v$)*
  • Step 3: Solve the linear system
  • Multiply Eq (1) by $4$ and Eq (2) by $9$:

$$ 64u + 36v = 4 \quad \text{--- (3)} $$

$$ 324u + 36v = 9 \quad \text{--- (4)} $$

  • Subtract Eq (3) from Eq (4):

$$ 260u = 5 \implies u = \frac{5}{260} = \frac{1}{52} $$

  • Since $u = \frac{1}{a^2}$, we have $a^2 = 52$.
  • Substitute $u = \frac{1}{52}$ into Eq (1):

$$ 16\left(\frac{1}{52}\right) + 9v = 1 \implies \frac{4}{13} + 9v = 1 $$

$$ 9v = 1 - \frac{4}{13} = \frac{9}{13} \implies v = \frac{1}{13} $$

  • Since $v = \frac{1}{b^2}$, we have $b^2 = 13$. (CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for finding $a^2 = 52$ and $b^2 = 13$)

  • Step 4: Final Equation

  • Substituting $a^2$ and $b^2$:

$$ \frac{x^2}{52} + \frac{y^2}{13} = 1 \quad \text{or} \quad x^2 + 4y^2 = 52 $$

*(CBSE Step: $\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}$ for final equation)*

Question B.4: Rod of Fixed Length Sliding Between Perpendicular Axes (Locus of a Point)

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Miscellaneous Q4, 5 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: A rod of length $12\text{ cm}$ moves with its ends always touching the coordinate axes. Determine the equation of the locus of a point $P$ on the rod, which is $3\text{ cm}$ from the end in contact with the $x$-axis. Name the conic section represented.

            y
            |
          B * (0, b)
            | \
            |   \
            |     * P(x, y)  (PB = 9 cm)
            |       \
            |         \  (PA = 3 cm)
            O----------*---------> x
                      A (a, 0)
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Parameterize the position of the rod
  • Let the ends of the rod touching the coordinate axes be $A(a, 0)$ on the $x$-axis and $B(0, b)$ on the $y$-axis.
  • The length of the rod is $AB = 12\text{ cm}$.
  • By the Pythagorean theorem in right-angled $\triangle OAB$:

$$ a^2 + b^2 = 12^2 = 144 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for setting up $a^2 + b^2 = 144$)*
  • Step 2: Apply the Section Formula
  • Point $P(x, y)$ is on the rod $AB$ such that $AP = 3\text{ cm}$.
  • Since total length $AB = 12\text{ cm}$, the remaining segment $PB = 12 - 3 = 9\text{ cm}$.
  • Thus, point $P$ divides the line segment joining $A(a, 0)$ and $B(0, b)$ internally in the ratio:

$$ m_1 : m_2 = AP : PB = 3 : 9 = 1 : 3 $$

  • By internal section formula:

$$ x = \frac{m_1 x_B + m_2 x_A}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{1(0) + 3(a)}{1 + 3} = \frac{3a}{4} $$

$$ y = \frac{m_1 y_B + m_2 y_A}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{1(b) + 3(0)}{1 + 3} = \frac{b}{4} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\frac{1}{2}\text{ Marks}$ for correctly setting up internal ratio $1:3$ and section formulas)*
  • Step 3: Express $a$ and $b$ in terms of $x$ and $y$
  • From the coordinate equations:

$$ a = \frac{4x}{3} \quad \text{and} \quad b = 4y $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for expressing $a, b$ in terms of $x, y$)*
  • Step 4: Substitute into the geometric constraint equation
  • Substitute $a = \frac{4x}{3}$ and $b = 4y$ into Eq (1):

$$ \left(\frac{4x}{3}\right)^2 + (4y)^2 = 144 $$

$$ \frac{16x^2}{9} + 16y^2 = 144 $$

  • Divide both sides by $144$:

$$ \frac{16x^2}{9 \times 144} + \frac{16y^2}{144} = 1 $$

$$ \frac{x^2}{9 \times 9} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1 \implies \frac{x^2}{81} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1 $$

  • Conclusion: The locus of point $P$ is an ellipse with semi-major axis $a_0 = 9$ along the $x$-axis and semi-minor axis $b_0 = 3$ along the $y$-axis. (CBSE Step: $1\frac{1}{2}\text{ Marks}$ for algebraic simplification to $\frac{x^2}{81} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$ and identifying the curve as an ellipse)

Question C.1: Exemplar Analytical MCQ — Vertex from General Parabola

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar Objective, 1 Mark]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [High-Yield]

Problem Statement: The vertex of the parabola $x^2 + 8x + 12y + 4 = 0$ is located at: * (a) $(-4, 1)$ * (b) $(4, -1)$ * (c) $(-4, -1)$ * (d) $(1, -4)$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Complete the square in $x$
  • Given equation:

$$ x^2 + 8x = -12y - 4 $$

Question C.2: Exemplar Subjective Problem — Hyperbola Foci & Conjugate Latus Rectum

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar Subjective, 4 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: Find the equation of the hyperbola whose foci are $(0, \pm \sqrt{10})$ and which passes through the point $(2, 3)$. Determine its eccentricity and length of latus rectum.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Identify standard form
  • The foci are on the $y$-axis at $(0, \pm c)$ where $c = \sqrt{10}$.
  • Standard equation of hyperbola with transverse axis along $y$-axis:

$$ \frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1 $$

  • Hyperbolic focal relation:

$$ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \implies a^2 + b^2 = (\sqrt{10})^2 = 10 \implies b^2 = 10 - a^2 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for standard vertical hyperbola equation and relation $b^2 = 10 - a^2$)*
  • Step 2: Substitute the known point $(2, 3)$
  • Point $(2, 3)$ lies on the hyperbola:

$$ \frac{3^2}{a^2} - \frac{2^2}{b^2} = 1 \implies \frac{9}{a^2} - \frac{4}{b^2} = 1 $$

  • Substitute $b^2 = 10 - a^2$:

$$ \frac{9}{a^2} - \frac{4}{10 - a^2} = 1 $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for substitution)*
  • Step 3: Solve the rational algebraic equation
  • Clear denominators by multiplying by $a^2(10 - a^2)$:

$$ 9(10 - a^2) - 4a^2 = a^2(10 - a^2) $$

$$ 90 - 9a^2 - 4a^2 = 10a^2 - a^4 $$

$$ 90 - 13a^2 = 10a^2 - a^4 $$

$$ a^4 - 23a^2 + 90 = 0 $$

  • Factorize the quadratic in $a^2$:

$$ (a^2 - 18)(a^2 - 5) = 0 $$

  • Thus, $a^2 = 18$ or $a^2 = 5$.
  • Since $b^2 = 10 - a^2$, and $b^2$ must be strictly positive:
    • If $a^2 = 18 \implies b^2 = 10 - 18 = -8 < 0$ (impossible).
    • If $a^2 = 5 \implies b^2 = 10 - 5 = 5 > 0$ (valid!).
  • Therefore:

$$ a^2 = 5 \quad \text{and} \quad b^2 = 5 $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for solving quadratic, rejecting invalid root, and obtaining $a^2 = 5, b^2 = 5$)*
  • Step 4: Formulate equation, eccentricity, and latus rectum
  • Equation of the Hyperbola:

$$ \frac{y^2}{5} - \frac{x^2}{5} = 1 \quad \text{or} \quad y^2 - x^2 = 5 $$

*(This is a rectangular/equilateral hyperbola)*.
  • Eccentricity:

$$ e = \frac{c}{a} = \frac{\sqrt{10}}{\sqrt{5}} = \sqrt{\frac{10}{5}} = \sqrt{2} $$

  • Length of Latus Rectum:

$$ \text{LLR} = \frac{2b^2}{a} = \frac{2(5)}{\sqrt{5}} = 2\sqrt{5}\text{ units} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for final equation, $e = \sqrt{2}$, and $\text{LLR} = 2\sqrt{5}$)*

Question C.3: Exemplar HOTS Subjective — Equal Focal Chords of Parabola

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar HOTS, 5 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: Show that the locus of the mid-point of focal chords of the parabola $y^2 = 4ax$ is another parabola whose latus rectum is half that of the given parabola.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Parameterize the endpoints of a focal chord
  • Let the parabola be $y^2 = 4ax$. Focus is $S(a, 0)$.
  • Let the endpoints of any chord passing through the focus be $P(a t_1^2, 2a t_1)$ and $Q(a t_2^2, 2a t_2)$.
  • Since chord $PQ$ passes through $S(a, 0)$, points $P$, $S$, and $Q$ are collinear.
  • Slope of $PS$ = Slope of $QS$:

$$ \frac{2at_1 - 0}{at_1^2 - a} = \frac{2at_2 - 0}{at_2^2 - a} \implies \frac{2t_1}{t_1^2 - 1} = \frac{2t_2}{t_2^2 - 1} $$

$$ t_1(t_2^2 - 1) = t_2(t_1^2 - 1) \implies t_1 t_2^2 - t_1 = t_1^2 t_2 - t_2 $$

$$ t_1 t_2(t_2 - t_1) + (t_2 - t_1) = 0 \implies (t_2 - t_1)(t_1 t_2 + 1) = 0 $$

  • Since $P \neq Q \implies t_1 \neq t_2$, we must have:

$$ t_1 t_2 = -1 \implies t_2 = -\frac{1}{t_1} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\frac{1}{2}\text{ Marks}$ for deriving condition of focal chord $t_1 t_2 = -1$)*
  • Step 2: Express coordinates of the midpoint $(h, k)$
  • Let $M(h, k)$ be the midpoint of the focal chord $PQ$:

$$ h = \frac{a t_1^2 + a t_2^2}{2} = \frac{a}{2}(t_1^2 + t_2^2) \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

$$ k = \frac{2a t_1 + 2a t_2}{2} = a(t_1 + t_2) \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for midpoint formulas in terms of parameters)*
  • Step 3: Relate $h$ and $k$ by eliminating parameters
  • Square Eq (2):

$$ k^2 = a^2(t_1 + t_2)^2 = a^2(t_1^2 + t_2^2 + 2t_1 t_2) $$

  • Substitute $t_1 t_2 = -1$:

$$ k^2 = a^2\left(t_1^2 + t_2^2 - 2\right) $$

  • From Eq (1), $t_1^2 + t_2^2 = \frac{2h}{a}$. Substitute this in:

$$ k^2 = a^2\left(\frac{2h}{a} - 2\right) = a^2 \cdot \frac{2}{a}(h - a) $$

$$ k^2 = 2a(h - a) $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\frac{1}{2}\text{ Marks}$ for algebraic elimination of $t_1, t_2$ to reach $k^2 = 2a(h - a)$)*
  • Step 4: Locus conclusion and comparison of Latus Rectum
  • Replacing $(h, k)$ with current coordinates $(x, y)$:

$$ y^2 = 2a(x - a) $$

  • This is a parabola with vertex at $(a, 0)$ opening to the right.
  • Comparing with standard form $Y^2 = 4A X$:

$$ 4A = 2a \implies \text{Latus Rectum of locus} = 2a $$

  • Original parabola $y^2 = 4ax$ has latus rectum $= 4a$.
  • Therefore:

$$ \text{Latus Rectum of Locus} = 2a = \frac{1}{2}(4a) = \frac{1}{2} \times (\text{Latus Rectum of given parabola}) $$

  • Hence Proved. (CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for final locus equation, calculating latus rectum $2a$, and establishing the $\frac{1}{2}$ ratio)

Question C.4: Exemplar HOTS Subjective — Maximum Area Triangle in an Ellipse

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar HOTS, 5 Marks]</span> | <span class="star-icon">⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐</span> [Must-Know]

Problem Statement: Find the maximum possible area of an isosceles triangle inscribed in the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$ with its vertex at one end of the major axis $(a, 0)$ and its base parallel to the minor axis (the $y$-axis).

                 y
                 |       B (a cos θ, b sin θ)
                 |      *
                 |     /|
                 |    / |
      -----------+---*--+-----* A(a, 0)-----> x
                 |    \ |
                 |     \|
                 |      *
                 |       C (a cos θ, -b sin θ)
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution & CBSE Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Parameterize the vertices using eccentric angle $\theta$
  • One vertex of the isosceles triangle is fixed at $A(a, 0)$.
  • The base is perpendicular to the $x$-axis, so by symmetry, the other two vertices $B$ and $C$ must have the same $x$-coordinate and opposite $y$-coordinates.
  • Using parametric coordinates for the ellipse ($x = a\cos\theta, y = b\sin\theta$):

$$ B(a\cos\theta, b\sin\theta) \quad \text{and} \quad C(a\cos\theta, -b\sin\theta) $$

where $\theta \in (0, \pi)$ for distinct non-degenerate vertices.
*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for parametric assignment of $B$ and $C$)*
  • Step 2: Formulate base and altitude
  • The base of $\triangle ABC$ is the vertical segment $BC$:

$$ \text{Base } = |y_B - y_C| = b\sin\theta - (-b\sin\theta) = 2b\sin\theta $$

  • The altitude $h$ is the horizontal distance from vertex $A(a, 0)$ to the chord $BC$ (line $x = a\cos\theta$):

$$ \text{Altitude } h = a - a\cos\theta = a(1 - \cos\theta) $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for expressions for Base and Altitude)*
  • Step 3: Formulate Area function
  • Area of the triangle:

$$ \Delta(\theta) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{Base} \times \text{Altitude} = \frac{1}{2}(2b\sin\theta)(a(1 - \cos\theta)) $$

$$ \Delta(\theta) = ab\sin\theta(1 - \cos\theta) = ab(\sin\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta) = ab\left(\sin\theta - \frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta\right) $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for simplifying $\Delta(\theta) = ab\left(\sin\theta - \frac{1}{2}\sin 2\theta\right)$)*
  • Step 4: Maximize the area function using trigonometric conditions
  • Differentiating with respect to $\theta$:

$$ \frac{d\Delta}{d\theta} = ab(\cos\theta - \cos 2\theta) $$

  • Setting $\frac{d\Delta}{d\theta} = 0$:

$$ \cos 2\theta = \cos\theta \implies 2\cos^2\theta - 1 = \cos\theta $$

$$ 2\cos^2\theta - \cos\theta - 1 = 0 $$

  • Factoring:

$$ (2\cos\theta + 1)(\cos\theta - 1) = 0 $$

  • Roots:
    • $\cos\theta = 1 \implies \theta = 0$ (degenerate triangle, $\text{Area} = 0$).
    • $\cos\theta = -\frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = \frac{2\pi}{3} = 120^\circ$.
  • Check second derivative:

$$ \frac{d^2\Delta}{d\theta^2} = ab(-\sin\theta + 2\sin 2\theta) $$

$$ \text{At } \theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}: \quad \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \quad \sin\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} $$

$$ \frac{d^2\Delta}{d\theta^2} = ab\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + 2\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\right) = ab\left(-\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) < 0 \quad (\text{Local Maximum!}) $$

*(CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for critical angle $\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$ and second-derivative test)*
  • Step 5: Compute Maximum Area
  • Substitute $\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$ into $\Delta(\theta)$:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}, \quad \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} $$

$$ \Delta_{\max} = ab\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\left(1 - \left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right) = ab\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}ab\text{ sq. units} $$

  • Final Result: The maximum area of the inscribed isosceles triangle is $\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}ab\text{ sq. units}$. (CBSE Step: $1\text{ Mark}$ for final answer $\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}ab$)

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 The General Second-Degree Discriminant Shortcut

The general second-degree equation is:

$$ Ax^2 + 2Hxy + By^2 + 2Gx + 2Fy + C = 0 $$

Evaluate the discriminant determinant:

$$ \Delta = \begin{vmatrix} A & H & G \\ H & B & F \\ G & F & C \end{vmatrix} = ABC + 2FGH - AF^2 - BG^2 - CH^2 $$

  • If $\Delta = 0$: Degenerate conic (pair of straight lines, point, or parallel lines).
  • If $\Delta \neq 0$ (Non-degenerate conics):
  • Circle: $H = 0$ and $A = B \neq 0$.
  • Parabola: $H^2 - AB = 0$.
  • Ellipse: $H^2 - AB < 0$ (Real ellipse if $\Delta$ and $(A+B)$ have opposite signs).
  • Hyperbola: $H^2 - AB > 0$.
  • Rectangular Hyperbola: $H^2 - AB > 0$ and $A + B = 0$.

CBSE Speed Hack: In NCERT Class 11, cross-product terms ($xy$) are absent ($H = 0$). Hence, examine the signs of coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ directly: * Same sign & equal coefficients $\implies$ Circle * Only one quadratic term present ($x^2$ or $y^2$, never both) $\implies$ Parabola * Same sign & unequal coefficients $\implies$ Ellipse * Opposite signs $\implies$ Hyperbola

1.2 Parabolic Quick Invariants & Focal Length Extraction

For any shifted parabola in Cartesian form:

$$ (y - k)^2 = 4a(x - h) \quad \text{or} \quad (x - h)^2 = 4a(y - k) $$

  • Latus Rectum ($LR$): Always equals $|4a|$. Read it directly as the absolute value of the coefficient of the first-degree linear term.
  • Focal Distance Shortcut:
  • For $y^2 = 4ax$: Distance of any point $P(x_1, y_1)$ on the curve to focus $S(a,0)$ is:

$$ SP = x_1 + a $$

  • For $x^2 = 4ay$:

$$ SP = y_1 + a $$

(Avoid using the distance formula $\sqrt{(x_1 - a)^2 + y_1^2}$; use the directrix definition $SP = PM$ directly to save 2 minutes).

1.3 Ellipse & Hyperbola: Relation Between $a, b, c, e$

Never re-derive eccentricity from scratch during standard questions:

  • Ellipse:

$$ c^2 = a^2 - b^2 \implies b^2 = a^2(1 - e^2) \implies e = \sqrt{1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}}{a} = \frac{c}{a} $$

  • Length of Latus Rectum:

$$ LR = \frac{2b^2}{a} = 2 \cdot \frac{a^2 - c^2}{a} = 2a(1 - e^2) $$

  • Distance between Foci: $2c = 2ae$.

  • Distance between Directrices: $\frac{2a}{e}$.

  • Hyperbola:

$$ c^2 = a^2 + b^2 \implies b^2 = a^2(e^2 - 1) \implies e = \sqrt{1 + \frac{b^2}{a^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}{a} = \frac{c}{a} $$

  • Length of Latus Rectum:

$$ LR = \frac{2b^2}{a} = 2a(e^2 - 1) $$

1.4 The "Completing the Square" Fast Algorithm

When given expanded second-degree equations like $4x^2 + 9y^2 - 16x + 54y + 61 = 0$: 1. Group variables and factor out leading coefficients immediately:

$$ 4(x^2 - 4x) + 9(y^2 + 6y) = -61 $$

  1. Add $A \cdot (p/2)^2$ and $B \cdot (q/2)^2$ to the right side in one step:

$$ 4(x - 2)^2 + 9(y + 3)^2 = -61 + 4(4) + 9(9) = -61 + 16 + 81 = 36 $$

  1. Divide by the RHS constant ($36$):

$$ \frac{(x - 2)^2}{9} + \frac{(y + 3)^2}{4} = 1 $$

Center $(h, k) = (2, -3)$, $a^2 = 9 \implies a = 3$, $b^2 = 4 \implies b = 2$.

1.5 Parametric Coordinates Quick Checks

Use parametric forms to verify point inclusion, chord lengths, or tangent validations: * Parabola $y^2 = 4ax \implies (at^2, 2at)$ * Ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \implies (a\cos\theta, b\sin\theta)$ * Hyperbola $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \implies (a\sec\theta, b\tan\theta)$ * Circle $x^2 + y^2 = r^2 \implies (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

2.1 The Major/Minor Axis Orientation Trap (Horizontal vs. Vertical)

  • The Error: Assuming $a^2$ is always under $x^2$ in an ellipse.
  • The Reality: In an ellipse, $a > b$ by definition in NCERT.
  • If the denominator under $x^2$ is larger: Major axis lies along the x-axis ($\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$).
  • If the denominator under $y^2$ is larger: Major axis lies along the y-axis ($\frac{x^2}{b^2} + \frac{y^2}{a^2} = 1$).
  • Hyperbola Difference: In a hyperbola, $a^2$ is always the denominator of the positive term, regardless of whether $a > b$, $a < b$, or $a = b$.
  • $\frac{x^2}{a^2} - \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \implies$ Transverse axis along x-axis, foci $(\pm c, 0)$.
  • $\frac{y^2}{a^2} - \frac{x^2}{b^2} = 1 \implies$ Transverse axis along y-axis, foci $(0, \pm c)$.
  • Board Penalty: Mixing up horizontal and vertical orientations results in a $-1$ mark deduction for standard identification, propagating to complete zero marks for subsequent coordinates (vertices, foci, directrices).

2.2 Extraneous Solutions & Square Root Sign Ambiguities

  • When solving for $a$ or $b$ from given conditions (e.g., $a^2 = 16$):

$$ a = 4 \quad (\text{Since } a \text{ represents semi-axis length, } a > 0) $$

Writing $a = \pm 4$ as a semi-axis length shows conceptual weakness. While coordinates of vertices are $(\pm a, 0)$, the geometric length $a$ must be stated as strictly positive ($a > 0$). * Focal Parameter $a$ in Parabolas: In $y^2 = -12x$, students mistakenly write $4a = -12 \implies a = -3$. * Correct NCERT Method: Standard form is $y^2 = -4ax$. Therefore, $4a = 12 \implies a = 3$. Focus is $(-a, 0) = (-3, 0)$, Directrix is $x = a \implies x = 3$. Setting $a < 0$ causes sign errors across directrix and focus calculations (deduction: $\frac{1}{2}$ mark).

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

  1. Focal Distance Property of Ellipse (String Property): The sum of the distances of any point $P(x, y)$ on an ellipse from its two foci $S$ and $S'$ is constant and equal to the length of the major axis:

$$ SP + S'P = 2a $$

NCERT Proof Hint: Follows directly from $SP = e \cdot PM$ and $S'P = e \cdot PM'$, where $PM + PM' = \frac{2a}{e}$.

  1. Focal Distance Property of Hyperbola: The absolute difference of the distances of any point $P(x, y)$ on a hyperbola from its two foci $S$ and $S'$ is constant and equal to the length of the transverse axis:

$$ |SP - S'P| = 2a $$

  1. Eccentricity Regimes:
  2. Circle: $e = 0$
  3. Parabola: $e = 1$
  4. Ellipse: $0 < e < 1$
  5. Hyperbola: $e > 1$
  6. Rectangular Hyperbola: $e = \sqrt{2}$

  7. Pair of intersecting straight lines: $e = \infty$

  8. Degenerate Conics (Intersection of Plane with Double Right Circular Cone): Let $\alpha$ be the semi-vertical angle of the cone and $\beta$ be the angle made by the intersecting plane with the vertical axis of the cone:

  9. $\beta = 90^\circ \implies$ Circle
  10. $\alpha < \beta < 90^\circ \implies$ Ellipse
  11. $\beta = \alpha \implies$ Parabola (plane is parallel to generator/slant edge)
  12. $0 \le \beta < \alpha \implies$ Hyperbola (plane cuts both nappes)

  13. If the plane passes through the vertex ($V$):

    • $\beta > \alpha \implies$ Single point (degenerate ellipse)
    • $\beta = \alpha \implies$ Single straight line (coincident lines / degenerate parabola)
    • $\beta < \alpha \implies$ Pair of intersecting straight lines (degenerate hyperbola)

  14. Latus Rectum as Double Ordinate: The latus rectum is a chord perpendicular to the major/principal axis passing through one of the foci. It is not just any chord through the focus (which is merely a focal chord).

For standard circle:

$$ x^2 + y^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 $$

  • Real Circle: $g^2 + f^2 - c > 0$

  • Imaginary Circle (Non-existent locus): $g^2 + f^2 - c < 0$

1. Pedagogical Theory, Core Definitions & Geometric Intuition

1.1 Generation of a Double Right Circular Hollow Cone ⭐⭐⭐

Let $l$ be a fixed straight line in three-dimensional space, and let $m$ be another line intersecting $l$ at a fixed point $V$ at an acute angle $\alpha$ ($0^\circ < \alpha < 90^\circ$).

  • Axis ($l$): The fixed straight line passing vertically through $V$.
  • Generator ($m$): The revolving line that traces the surface.
  • Vertex ($V$): The fixed point of intersection of the generator and the axis.
  • Semi-Vertical Angle ($\alpha$): The constant acute angle between the generator $m$ and the axis $l$.

When line $m$ rotates completely about line $l$ keeping $\alpha$ constant: - The geometric figure traced out is a Double Right Circular Hollow Cone. - The cone consists of two infinite portions called nappes: - Upper Nappe: The portion extending infinitely above $V$. - Lower Nappe: The portion extending infinitely below $V$.

           \     /
            \   /       <-- Upper Nappe
             \ /
              V         <-- Vertex
             / \
            /   \       <-- Lower Nappe
           /     \
             |
             | Axis (l)

1.2 The Cutting Plane and Sectioning Angle ($\beta$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let an intersecting plane $\pi$ cut through the double cone. - Let $\beta$ be the angle formed between the cutting plane $\pi$ and the vertical axis $l$ of the cone, such that:

$$ 0^\circ \le \beta \le 90^\circ $$

The curves obtained by the intersection of a plane with a double right circular cone are called Conic Sections (or simply Conics).

Depending on whether the cutting plane passes through the vertex $V$ or avoids $V$, conics are partitioned into two fundamental classes: 1. Non-Degenerate Conic Sections: The cutting plane does not pass through $V$. 2. Degenerate Conic Sections: The cutting plane passes directly through $V$.

1.3 Non-Degenerate Conic Sections ($V \notin \pi$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Case Condition on $\beta$ Section Formed Geometric Behavior
I $\beta = 90^\circ$ Circle Plane is perpendicular to axis $l$. Intersects only one nappe in a completely closed, radially symmetric curve.
II $\alpha < \beta < 90^\circ$ Ellipse Plane is tilted relative to the axis but steeper than the generator. Cuts completely across one nappe; curve is closed and bounded.
III $\beta = \alpha$ Parabola Plane is strictly parallel to one generator line. Cuts only one nappe; curve is open, unbounded, and extends infinitely.
IV $0^\circ \le \beta < \alpha$ Hyperbola Plane is parallel to the axis or tilted at an angle smaller than $\alpha$. Cuts both the upper and lower nappes; produces two distinct, unbounded branches. When $\beta = 0^\circ$ (plane parallel to axis), a rectangular hyperbola can be formed depending on $\alpha$.

1.4 Degenerate Conic Sections ($V \in \pi$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

When the cutting plane contains the vertex $V$, the resulting intersection collapses into singular/degenerate figures:

Case Condition on $\beta$ Degenerate Section Explanation
I $\alpha < \beta \le 90^\circ$ A Single Point The plane intersects the cone only at the isolated vertex $V$.
II $\beta = \alpha$ A Single Straight Line The plane becomes tangent to the cone along one generator line through $V$ (often viewed as two coincident lines).
III $0^\circ \le \beta < \alpha$ A Pair of Intersecting Lines The plane slices through both nappes, intersecting along two distinct generators that cross at $V$.

2. Rigorous Mathematical Proofs & Coordinate Derivations

Proof 2.1: Analytical Derivation of the Cone Equation & Its Planar Sections ⭐⭐⭐⭐

1. Given

  • Vertex $V$ at the origin $(0, 0, 0)$.
  • Axis $l$ aligned with the $z$-axis (unit vector $\mathbf{k} = \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle$).
  • Semi-vertical angle $\alpha$, where $0 < \alpha < \frac{\pi}{2}$.

2. To Prove

  1. The equation of the double cone is:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = z^2 \tan^2\alpha $$

  1. The intersection with plane $z = k$:
  2. For $k \neq 0$: A non-degenerate circle.
  3. For $k = 0$: A degenerate point conic $(0, 0, 0)$.
  4. Slicing with an inclined plane $y \sin\theta + z \cos\theta = p$ reproduces:
  5. An ellipse if $\theta < \frac{\pi}{2} - \alpha$
  6. A parabola if $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} - \alpha$
  7. A hyperbola if $\theta > \frac{\pi}{2} - \alpha$

3. Step-by-Step Algebraic Proof

Step A: Derivation of the Double Cone Equation Let $P(x, y, z)$ be any point on the cone surface. The position vector of $P$ relative to $V(0,0,0)$ is:

$$ \mathbf{r} = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k} $$

The unit vector along the vertical axis is $\mathbf{\hat{a}} = \mathbf{k}$. By definition, the line segment $VP$ makes a constant angle $\alpha$ with the axis $\mathbf{k}$:

$$ \cos\alpha = \frac{\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{\hat{a}}}{\|\mathbf{r}\| \|\mathbf{\hat{a}}\|} = \frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}} $$

Squaring both sides:

$$ \cos^2\alpha = \frac{z^2}{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

$$ (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)\cos^2\alpha = z^2 $$

$$ (x^2 + y^2)\cos^2\alpha = z^2(1 - \cos^2\alpha) = z^2\sin^2\alpha $$

Dividing both sides by $\cos^2\alpha$ (since $0 < \alpha < \frac{\pi}{2} \implies \cos\alpha \neq 0$):

$$ x^2 + y^2 = z^2 \tan^2\alpha $$

Step B: Slicing by a Plane Perpendicular to the Axis ($\beta = 90^\circ$) Let the plane be $\pi: z = k$ (where $k$ is a constant distance from $V$).

Substitute $z = k$ into the cone equation:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = k^2 \tan^2\alpha $$

  • Subcase B.1 ($k \neq 0$, plane does not pass through $V$): $k^2 \tan^2\alpha = R^2 > 0$, where $R = |k|\tan\alpha$.

$$ x^2 + y^2 = R^2 $$

This is the standard equation of a Circle with center $(0,0,k)$ and radius $R = |k|\tan\alpha$.

  • Subcase B.2 ($k = 0$, plane passes through $V$):

$$ x^2 + y^2 = 0 $$

Over $\mathbb{R}$, this forces $x = 0$ and $y = 0$. The intersection is the unique point $(0, 0, 0)$ (Degenerate Point Circle).

Step C: Slicing by a General Inclined Plane Passing through Vertex $V$ ($p = 0$) Let a plane passing through $V(0,0,0)$ make an angle $\beta$ with the axis ($z$-axis). Without loss of generality, orient the coordinate system such that the normal vector lies in the $y$-$z$ plane. The equation of the plane containing the origin is:

$$ y \cos\beta - z \sin\beta = 0 \implies z = y \cot\beta $$

Substitute $z = y \cot\beta$ into the cone equation $x^2 + y^2 = z^2 \tan^2\alpha$:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = (y \cot\beta)^2 \tan^2\alpha $$

$$ x^2 + y^2\left(1 - \frac{\cot^2\beta}{\cot^2\alpha}\right) = 0 $$

$$ x^2 + y^2\left(\frac{\sin^2\beta\cos^2\alpha - \cos^2\beta\sin^2\alpha}{\sin^2\beta\cos^2\alpha}\right) = 0 $$

$$ x^2 + y^2\left(\frac{\sin(\beta - \alpha)\sin(\beta + \alpha)}{\sin^2\beta\cos^2\alpha}\right) = 0 $$

Let $\lambda = \dfrac{\sin(\beta - \alpha)\sin(\beta + \alpha)}{\sin^2\beta\cos^2\alpha}$. The equation becomes:

$$ x^2 + \lambda y^2 = 0 $$

Now analyze the sign of $\lambda$ based on $\beta$ ($0 \le \beta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$):

  1. If $\beta > \alpha$:
  2. Since $0 < \alpha < \beta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$, we have $(\beta - \alpha) > 0$ and $(\beta + \alpha) > 0$.
  3. Thus, $\sin(\beta - \alpha) > 0$ and $\sin(\beta + \alpha) > 0 \implies \lambda > 0$.
  4. The equation $x^2 + \lambda y^2 = 0$ has only one real solution: $x = 0, y = 0 \implies z = 0$.

  5. Result: A Single Point $(0,0,0)$.

  6. If $\beta = \alpha$:

  7. $\sin(\beta - \alpha) = \sin(0) = 0 \implies \lambda = 0$.
  8. The equation reduces to $x^2 = 0 \implies x = 0$.
  9. Along with $z = y\cot\alpha$, this defines the line $x = 0, z = y\cot\alpha$.

  10. Result: A Single Straight Line (tangent generator).

  11. If $\beta < \alpha$:

  12. Here $(\beta - \alpha) < 0 \implies \sin(\beta - \alpha) < 0$, while $\sin(\beta + \alpha) > 0$.
  13. Thus, $\lambda < 0$. Let $\lambda = -\mu^2$ where $\mu > 0$.
  14. The equation becomes:

$$ x^2 - \mu^2 y^2 = 0 \implies (x - \mu y)(x + \mu y) = 0 $$

  • This represents two distinct planes intersecting along lines through the origin:

$$ x = \mu y \quad \text{and} \quad x = -\mu y $$

  • Result: A Pair of Distinct Intersecting Straight Lines passing through $V$.

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: The General Second-Degree Polynomial & Degeneracy Condition ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

1. General Equation

Every conic section in the Cartesian plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the locus of points satisfying the general second-degree equation:

$$ S(x, y) \equiv ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0 $$

2. The Discriminants

Define the Locus Discriminant ($\Delta$) and the Quadratic Discriminant ($D$):

$$ \Delta = \det \begin{bmatrix} a & h & g \\ h & b & f \\ g & f & c \end{bmatrix} = abc + 2fgh - af^2 - bg^2 - ch^2 $$

$$ D = h^2 - ab $$

3. High-Yield Worked Examples & NCERT Exemplar Problems

Problem 3.1: Identifying Geometric Conic Types from Angle Conditions

Question: A right circular cone has a semi-vertical angle $\alpha = 35^\circ$. A cutting plane $\pi$ intersects the cone. Determine the nature of the cross-section in each of the following independent cases: 1. The plane makes an angle of $45^\circ$ with the axis of the cone and does not contain the vertex. 2. The plane makes an angle of $35^\circ$ with the axis and passes through the vertex. 3. The plane is parallel to the axis of the cone and does not pass through the vertex. 4. The plane makes an angle of $20^\circ$ with the axis and passes through the vertex.

Solution & Step-by-Step Breakdown:

Given semi-vertical angle $\alpha = 35^\circ$. Let $\beta$ be the angle between the cutting plane and the axis.

  • Part 1: $\beta = 45^\circ$, plane does not contain vertex ($V \notin \pi$).
  • Comparison: $\alpha < \beta < 90^\circ$ since $35^\circ < 45^\circ < 90^\circ$.
  • Because $V \notin \pi$ and $\beta > \alpha$, the cutting plane cuts completely across one nappe.

  • Conclusion: Ellipse. (Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Part 2: $\beta = 35^\circ$, plane contains vertex ($V \in \pi$).

  • Comparison: $\beta = \alpha = 35^\circ$.
  • Because $V \in \pi$ and $\beta = \alpha$, the plane is tangent along a generator.

  • Conclusion: A Single Straight Line (coincident lines). (Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Part 3: Plane is parallel to the axis $\implies \beta = 0^\circ$; $V \notin \pi$.

  • Comparison: $0^\circ \le \beta < \alpha$ since $0^\circ < 35^\circ$.
  • Because $V \notin \pi$, the plane intersects both the upper and lower nappes.

  • Conclusion: Hyperbola. (Marking: 1 Mark)

  • Part 4: $\beta = 20^\circ$, plane contains vertex ($V \in \pi$).

  • Comparison: $0^\circ \le \beta < \alpha$ since $20^\circ < 35^\circ$.
  • Because the plane contains $V$ and cuts both nappes, the cross-section consists of two lines meeting at $V$.
  • Conclusion: Pair of Intersecting Straight Lines. (Marking: 1 Mark)

Problem 3.2: Analytical Detection of Degenerate vs Non-Degenerate Conics

Question: Consider the equation:

$$ 2x^2 + 7xy + 3y^2 - 4x - 7y + 2 = 0 $$

  1. Prove analytically using determinants whether the curve is degenerate or non-degenerate.
  2. If degenerate, resolve it into its constituent linear equations.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify coefficients Comparing with standard form $ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0$:

$$ a = 2, \quad 2h = 7 \implies h = \frac{7}{2}, \quad b = 3 $$

$$ 2g = -4 \implies g = -2, \quad 2f = -7 \implies f = -\frac{7}{2}, \quad c = 2 $$

Step 2: Evaluate the 3x3 Discriminant $\Delta$

$$ \Delta = \det \begin{bmatrix} a & h & g \\ h & b & f \\ g & f & c \end{bmatrix} = \det \begin{bmatrix} 2 & \frac{7}{2} & -2 \\ \frac{7}{2} & 3 & -\frac{7}{2} \\ -2 & -\frac{7}{2} & 2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Multiply rows/factor out scalar $\frac{1}{2}$ for simpler computation:

$$ \Delta = 2\left[3(2) - \left(-\frac{7}{2}\right)^2\right] - \frac{7}{2}\left[\frac{7}{2}(2) - (-2)\left(-\frac{7}{2}\right)\right] + (-2)\left[\frac{7}{2}\left(-\frac{7}{2}\right) - 3(-2)\right] $$

Calculate term-by-term: 1. First term:

$$ 2\left[6 - \frac{49}{4}\right] = 2\left[-\frac{25}{4}\right] = -\frac{25}{2} $$

  1. Second term:

$$ \frac{7}{2}(2) - (-2)\left(-\frac{7}{2}\right) = 7 - 7 = 0 \implies -\frac{7}{2}(0) = 0 $$

  1. Third term:

$$ -2\left[-\frac{49}{4} + 6\right] = -2\left[-\frac{25}{4}\right] = \frac{25}{2} $$

Sum:

$$ \Delta = -\frac{25}{2} + 0 + \frac{25}{2} = 0 $$

Since $\Delta = 0$, the given equation represents a Degenerate Conic Section. (Scoring: 2 Marks)

Step 3: Determine the sub-type Evaluate $D = h^2 - ab$:

$$ D = \left(\frac{7}{2}\right)^2 - (2)(3) = \frac{49}{4} - 6 = \frac{25}{4} > 0 $$

Since $\Delta = 0$ and $h^2 - ab > 0$, the equation represents a Pair of Intersecting Straight Lines. (Scoring: 1 Mark)

Step 4: Factorization into linear components Consider the quadratic terms:

$$ 2x^2 + 7xy + 3y^2 = (2x + y)(x + 3y) $$

Let the complete factors be:

$$ (2x + y + c_1)(x + 3y + c_2) = 0 $$

Expanding and equating linear and constant terms:

$$ 2x^2 + 7xy + 3y^2 + (c_1 + 2c_2)x + (3c_1 + c_2)y + c_1 c_2 = 0 $$

Equating coefficients: 1. $c_1 + 2c_2 = -4$ 2. $3c_1 + c_2 = -7$ 3. $c_1 c_2 = 2$

From (2), $c_2 = -7 - 3c_1$. Substitute into (1):

$$ c_1 + 2(-7 - 3c_1) = -4 \implies c_1 - 14 - 6c_1 = -4 \implies -5c_1 = 10 \implies c_1 = -2 $$

Then:

$$ c_2 = -7 - 3(-2) = -1 $$

Check product condition: $c_1 c_2 = (-2)(-1) = 2$ (consistent).

Thus, the degenerate conic splits into the two intersecting lines:

$$ (2x + y - 2) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad (x + 3y - 1) = 0 $$

(Scoring: 2 Marks)

Problem 3.3: NCERT Exemplar Critical Thinking

Question: Under what condition on $k$ does the equation $x^2 - y^2 - 2x + 1 = 0$ represent: 1. A pair of straight lines? 2. What are the equations of these lines and their point of intersection?

Solution:

Rewrite the given equation by completing the square in $x$:

$$ (x^2 - 2x + 1) - y^2 = 0 \implies (x - 1)^2 - y^2 = 0 $$

Using the difference of two squares $A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A + B)$:

$$ [(x - 1) - y][(x - 1) + y] = 0 $$

$$ (x - y - 1)(x + y - 1) = 0 $$

  • The equation represents two distinct linear equations:

$$ L_1: x - y - 1 = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad L_2: x + y - 1 = 0 $$

  • Solving simultaneously: Adding the two equations: $2x - 2 = 0 \implies x = 1$. Substituting into $L_1$: $1 - y - 1 = 0 \implies y = 0$.
  • Conclusion: The locus is a degenerate hyperbola consisting of a pair of straight lines intersecting at $(1, 0)$.

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Trap 1: Confusing Axis Angle ($\beta$) with Generator Angle ($\alpha$)

  • Mistake: Students frequently write $\beta = \alpha$ gives an ellipse or circle.
  • Correction:
  • $\beta$ is measured between the cutting plane and the central cone axis.
  • If $\beta = \alpha$, the plane is oriented at the exact angle of the generator $\implies$ Parabola.
  • If $\beta = 90^\circ$ (perpendicular to axis) $\implies$ Circle.

Trap 2: Neglecting the Vertex Position ($V \in \pi$ vs $V \notin \pi$)

  • Mistake: Assuming $\beta < \alpha$ ALWAYS produces a hyperbola.
  • Correction:
  • If $V \notin \pi$, it produces a Hyperbola.
  • If $V \in \pi$, it produces a Pair of Intersecting Lines.

Trap 3: Sign of $h^2 - ab$ vs Sign of $\Delta$

  • Mistake: Seeing $h^2 - ab > 0$ and immediately concluding the curve is a hyperbola without verifying $\Delta$.
  • Correction: Always evaluate $\Delta$ first!
  • If $\Delta \neq 0$ and $h^2 - ab > 0 \implies$ Hyperbola.
  • If $\Delta = 0$ and $h^2 - ab > 0 \implies$ Pair of Intersecting Lines.

Trap 4: Treating Coincident Lines as a Non-Conic

  • Mistake: Writing "no solution" or "undefined conic" when $\Delta = 0$ and $h^2 - ab = 0$.
  • Correction: This represents Parallel or Coincident Straight Lines (e.g., $(x - 2)^2 = 0 \implies x = 2$), which is a valid degenerate conic.

5.1 The "BE-PA-HY" Master Mnemonic

Relate $\beta$ (plane angle) with $\alpha$ (semi-vertical angle):

$$ \beta > \alpha \longrightarrow Ellipse (closed) $$

$$ \beta = \alpha \longrightarrow Parabola (boundary) $$

$$ \beta < \alpha \longrightarrow Hyperbola (two nappes) $$

Memory Hook: As the plane tilts downward from vertical ($90^\circ \to 0^\circ$):
Circle $\to$ Ellipse $\to$ Parabola $\to$ Hyperbola.
The eccentricity $e$ increases simultaneously: $0 \to e < 1 \to e = 1 \to e > 1$.
