Module 1: The Need for Complex Numbers, Integral Powers of $i$, and Algebra of Complex Numbers

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 The Need for Complex Numbers ⭐⭐⭐

In the realm of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, every square of a real quantity is non-negative:

$$ \forall x \in \mathbb{R},\quad x^2 \ge 0 $$

Consequently, simple algebraic polynomial equations such as:

$$ x^2 + 1 = 0 \iff x^2 = -1 $$

possess no real solutions. When solving the general quadratic equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ ($a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}, a \neq 0$), the quadratic formula yields:

$$ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a}, \quad \text{where } D = b^2 - 4ac $$

When $D < 0$, the square root of a strictly negative real number is undefined within $\mathbb{R}$.

To achieve algebraic closure (ensuring every non-constant polynomial has a root), the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler (1748) introduced the symbol $i$ (iota), defined by the property:

$$ i^2 = -1 \implies i = \sqrt{-1} $$

This formal extension enlarges the real number field $\mathbb{R}$ to the complex number field $\mathbb{C}$.

1.2 Formal Definition of a Complex Number ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

A complex number $z$ is defined as an ordered pair of real numbers $(a, b)$, written algebraically in canonical form as:

$$ z = a + ib \quad (\text{or } a + bi) $$

where: * $a \in \mathbb{R}$ is called the Real Part of $z$, denoted by $\text{Re}(z)$ or $\Re(z)$. * $b \in \mathbb{R}$ is called the Imaginary Part of $z$, denoted by $\text{Im}(z)$ or $\Im(z)$.

Crucial Conceptual Distinction: $\text{Im}(z)$ is a purely real number; it is the real scalar coefficient multiplying $i$, not $ib$. For $z = 3 - 4i$: $\text{Re}(z) = 3$, and $\text{Im}(z) = -4$ (NOT $-4i$).

Classification:

  1. Purely Real: $z$ is purely real if and only if $\text{Im}(z) = 0 \iff z = a$. Thus, $\mathbb{R} \subset \mathbb{C}$.
  2. Purely Imaginary: $z$ is purely imaginary if and only if $\text{Re}(z) = 0$ and $\text{Im}(z) \neq 0 \iff z = ib$ ($b \neq 0$).
  3. Zero Complex Number: $0 + 0i = 0$ is simultaneously purely real and purely imaginary.

1.3 Geometric Interpretation: The Argand Plane (Complex Plane) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Every complex number $z = a + ib$ corresponds uniquely to an ordered pair $(a, b) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. * The Horizontal Axis represents $\text{Re}(z)$ and is called the Real Axis. * The Vertical Axis represents $\text{Im}(z)$ and is called the Imaginary Axis. * Addition of two complex numbers corresponds to standard vector addition (Parallelogram Law of Vectors) in the plane.

1.4 Equality of Two Complex Numbers ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Two complex numbers $z_1 = a + ib$ and $z_2 = c + id$ are defined to be equal if and only if their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are independently equal:

$$ z_1 = z_2 \iff \big(\text{Re}(z_1) = \text{Re}(z_2) \quad \text{and} \quad \text{Im}(z_1) = \text{Im}(z_2)\big) $$

$$ a + ib = c + id \iff a = c \quad \text{and} \quad b = d $$

The Ordering Non-Existence Theorem: Inequalities like $z_1 > z_2$ or $z_1 < z_2$ are strictly undefined and meaningless in $\mathbb{C}$ unless both $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{R}$ (i.e., $\text{Im}(z_1) = \text{Im}(z_2) = 0$). Statements such as $2 + 3i > 1 + 2i$ are mathematically invalid.

2. Rigorous Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 2.1: Cyclic Periodicity of Integral Powers of $i$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Let $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. The powers of $i$ form a cyclic group of order 4 under multiplication:

$$ \{i^0, i^1, i^2, i^3\} = \{1, i, -1, -i\} $$

For any integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$ i^{4k} = 1, \quad i^{4k+1} = i, \quad i^{4k+2} = -1, \quad i^{4k+3} = -i $$

Proof: By definition:

$$ i^1 = i $$

$$ i^2 = -1 $$

$$ i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = (-1) \cdot i = -i $$

$$ i^4 = (i^2)^2 = (-1)^2 = 1 $$

Now consider any arbitrary integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. By Euclid's Division Lemma, dividing $n$ by $4$ produces unique integers $k$ (quotient) and $r$ (remainder) such that:

$$ n = 4k + r, \quad \text{where } r \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\} $$

Using laws of exponents:

$$ i^n = i^{4k + r} = (i^4)^k \cdot i^r = (1)^k \cdot i^r = 1 \cdot i^r = i^r $$

  • Case 1: $r = 0 \implies i^n = i^0 = 1$
  • Case 2: $r = 1 \implies i^n = i^1 = i$
  • Case 3: $r = 2 \implies i^n = i^2 = -1$
  • Case 4: $r = 3 \implies i^n = i^3 = -i$

For negative exponents ($n < 0$), let $n = -m$ where $m \in \mathbb{N}$:

$$ i^{-1} = \frac{1}{i} = \frac{1 \cdot i}{i \cdot i} = \frac{i}{i^2} = \frac{i}{-1} = -i $$

Applying the same division lemma to $-m$ demonstrates that periodicity of 4 holds over all $\mathbb{Z}$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2.2: Sum of Four Consecutive Integral Powers of $i$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: For any integer $n \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$ i^n + i^{n+1} + i^{n+2} + i^{n+3} = 0 $$

Proof: Given: An expression of four consecutive integral powers: $S = i^n + i^{n+1} + i^{n+2} + i^{n+3}$.
To Prove: $S = 0$.

Factoring out the lowest power $i^n$:

$$ S = i^n \left(1 + i + i^2 + i^3\right) $$

Substitute the evaluated fundamental values $i^2 = -1$ and $i^3 = -i$:

$$ S = i^n \left(1 + i + (-1) + (-i)\right) $$

Group real and imaginary terms inside the bracket:

$$ S = i^n \left[(1 - 1) + (i - i)\right] = i^n [0 + 0] = i^n \cdot 0 = 0 $$

Hence, the sum of any four consecutive integral powers of $i$ is identically zero. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2.3: Failure of Product Rule $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ for Simultaneous Negative Reals ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: The identity $\sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ is valid if and only if at least one of the real numbers $a$ or $b$ is non-negative ($a \ge 0$ or $b \ge 0$). If both $a < 0$ and $b < 0$, then:

$$ \sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} = -\sqrt{|a||b|} \neq \sqrt{ab} $$

Proof: Let $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that $a = -x$ and $b = -y$, where $x > 0$ and $y > 0$. Then $|a| = x$ and $|b| = y$.

By the definition of the imaginary unit:

$$ \sqrt{a} = \sqrt{-x} = \sqrt{x \cdot (-1)} = \sqrt{x} \cdot i $$

$$ \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{-y} = \sqrt{y \cdot (-1)} = \sqrt{y} \cdot i $$

Multiplying the two expressions:

$$ \text{LHS} = \sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} = (\sqrt{x} \cdot i) \cdot (\sqrt{y} \cdot i) = (\sqrt{x}\sqrt{y}) \cdot i^2 $$

Since $x, y > 0$, standard real arithmetic applies: $\sqrt{x}\sqrt{y} = \sqrt{xy}$. Given $i^2 = -1$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \sqrt{xy} \cdot (-1) = -\sqrt{xy} = -\sqrt{|a||b|} $$

Now examine the standard radicand product:

$$ \text{RHS} = \sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{(-x)(-y)} = \sqrt{xy} $$

Comparing both sides:

$$ \text{LHS} = -\sqrt{xy} \neq +\sqrt{xy} = \text{RHS} $$

Thus, $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} \neq \sqrt{ab}$ when $a < 0$ and $b < 0$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2.4: Field Properties of Complex Algebra ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $z_1 = a + ib$, $z_2 = c + id$, $z_3 = e + if \in \mathbb{C}$.

1. Addition:

  • Definition: $z_1 + z_2 = (a + c) + i(b + d)$
  • Additive Identity: $0 = 0 + 0i \implies z + 0 = z$
  • Additive Inverse: For $z = a + ib$, $-z = -a + i(-b)$ such that $z + (-z) = 0$.

2. Multiplication:

  • Definition:

$$ z_1 z_2 = (a + ib)(c + id) = ac + iad + ibc + i^2 bd = (ac - bd) + i(ad + bc) $$

  • Multiplicative Identity: $1 = 1 + 0i \implies z \cdot 1 = z$
  • Multiplicative Inverse ($z^{-1}$): For any non-zero complex number $z = a + ib \neq 0$ (meaning $a^2 + b^2 \neq 0$):

$$ z^{-1} = \frac{1}{a + ib} = \frac{a - ib}{(a + ib)(a - ib)} = \frac{a - ib}{a^2 - (ib)^2} = \frac{a - ib}{a^2 + b^2} = \left(\frac{a}{a^2 + b^2}\right) - i\left(\frac{b}{a^2 + b^2}\right) $$

3. Distributivity of Multiplication over Addition:

$$ z_1(z_2 + z_3) = z_1 z_2 + z_1 z_3 $$

SECTION 1: Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Definitions

1.1 The Modulus of a Complex Number

NCERT Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Core Foundation / Universal Applicability)

Definition

Let $z = a + ib$ be a complex number where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $i = \sqrt{-1}$.
The modulus (or absolute value) of $z$, denoted by $|z|$, is defined as the non-negative real number:

$$ |z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{(\operatorname{Re}(z))^2 + (\operatorname{Im}(z))^2} $$

Geometric Interpretation (Argand Plane)

In the complex Argand plane, any complex number $z = a + ib$ is represented by a unique ordered pair $P(a, b)$. By the Euclidean distance formula, the distance from the origin $O(0, 0)$ to the point $P(a, b)$ is:

$$ OP = \sqrt{(a - 0)^2 + (b - 0)^2} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} $$

Hence, geometrically, $|z|$ represents the Euclidean distance of the point $z$ from the origin $O(0,0)$.

          Im (Imaginary Axis)
                 ^
                 |          P(a, b) = z
                 |         /|
                 |        / |
                 |  |z|  /  |
                 |      /   | b = Im(z)
                 |     /    |
                 |    /     |
                 |   / θ    |
       ----------+--+-------+--------> Re (Real Axis)
                 O     a = Re(z)
                 |

Fundamental Real-Number Inequalities & Properties

For any $z = a + ib$: 1. $|z| \ge 0$, and $|z| = 0 \iff a = 0 \text{ and } b = 0 \iff z = 0$. 2. $-|z| \le \operatorname{Re}(z) \le |z|$ because $- \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \le a \le \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$. 3. $-|z| \le \operatorname{Im}(z) \le |z|$ because $- \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \le b \le \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$. 4. $|z| = |-z| = |\bar{z}| = |-\bar{z}| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.

1.2 The Conjugate of a Complex Number

NCERT Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Algebraic Workhorse)

Definition

Let $z = a + ib$ where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$. The complex conjugate of $z$, denoted by $\bar{z}$ (read as "$z$-bar"), is defined as:

$$ \bar{z} = a - ib $$

That is, the sign of the imaginary part is reversed, while the real part remains invariant.

Geometric Interpretation

In the Argand plane, $z = (a, b)$ and $\bar{z} = (a, -b)$.
Geometrically, $\bar{z}$ is the reflection (or mirror image) of the point $z$ across the real axis (horizontal axis).

          Im (Imaginary Axis)
                 ^
                 |          P(a, b) = z
                 |          |
                 |          | b
                 |          |
       ----------+----------+--------> Re (Real Axis)
                 |     a    |
                 |          | -b
                 |          |
                 |          P'(a, -b) = z̄
                 |

Analytical Extraction of Components

From $z = a + ib$ and $\bar{z} = a - ib$, adding and subtracting yield:

$$ \operatorname{Re}(z) = \frac{z + \bar{z}}{2} $$

$$ \operatorname{Im}(z) = \frac{z - \bar{z}}{2i} $$

Criteria for Real and Purely Imaginary Numbers

  1. $z$ is purely real $\iff \operatorname{Im}(z) = 0 \iff z = \bar{z}$.
  2. $z$ is purely imaginary $\iff \operatorname{Re}(z) = 0 \iff z = -\bar{z}$ (or $z + \bar{z} = 0$).

1.3 The Fundamental Relation Connecting Modulus and Conjugate

NCERT Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Crucial Lemma)

Let $z = a + ib$. Taking the product of $z$ and its conjugate $\bar{z}$:

$$ z \bar{z} = (a + ib)(a - ib) = a^2 - (ib)^2 = a^2 - i^2 b^2 $$

Since $i^2 = -1$:

$$ z \bar{z} = a^2 + b^2 $$

Recalling $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \implies |z|^2 = a^2 + b^2$, we obtain the identity:

$$ z \bar{z} = |z|^2 = |\bar{z}|^2 $$

1.4 The Multiplicative Inverse of a Complex Number

NCERT Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Standard 2/3-Mark Exam Question)

Definition

For any non-zero complex number $z = a + ib \neq 0$ (i.e., $a^2 + b^2 \neq 0$), the multiplicative inverse of $z$, denoted by $z^{-1}$ or $\frac{1}{z}$, is the unique complex number $w$ such that:

$$ z \cdot w = 1 + 0i = 1 $$

Derivation via Modulus-Conjugate Relation

Using the fundamental identity $z \bar{z} = |z|^2$: Since $z \neq 0$, $|z| > 0$, so we can divide both sides by $|z|^2$:

$$ z \cdot \left( \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2} \right) = 1 $$

By uniqueness of the multiplicative inverse in a field:

$$ z^{-1} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2} = \frac{a - ib}{a^2 + b^2} = \left( \frac{a}{a^2 + b^2} \right) + i \left( \frac{-b}{a^2 + b^2} \right) $$

1.5 Algebraic Identities in $\mathbb{C}$

NCERT Priority: ⭐⭐⭐ (Direct Structural Analogy with $\mathbb{R}$)

The field of complex numbers $(\mathbb{C}, +, \cdot)$ satisfies all the ring and field axioms present in $(\mathbb{R}, +, \cdot)$, specifically: - Commutativity: $z_1 + z_2 = z_2 + z_1$ and $z_1 z_2 = z_2 z_1$ - Associativity: $(z_1 + z_2) + z_3 = z_1 + (z_2 + z_3)$ and $(z_1 z_2) z_3 = z_1 (z_2 z_3)$ - Distributivity: $z_1(z_2 + z_3) = z_1 z_2 + z_1 z_3$

Consequently, all standard polynomial binomial identities hold identically for any $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$: 1. $(z_1 + z_2)^2 = z_1^2 + 2z_1 z_2 + z_2^2$ 2. $(z_1 - z_2)^2 = z_1^2 - 2z_1 z_2 + z_2^2$ 3. $(z_1 + z_2)^3 = z_1^3 + 3z_1^2 z_2 + 3z_1 z_2^2 + z_2^3$ 4. $(z_1 - z_2)^3 = z_1^3 - 3z_1^2 z_2 + 3z_1 z_2^2 - z_2^3$ 5. $z_1^2 - z_2^2 = (z_1 - z_2)(z_1 + z_2)$

SECTION 2: Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: Conjugate of a Sum and Difference

$$ \overline{z_1 \pm z_2} = \bar{z}_1 \pm \bar{z}_2 $$

  • Given: Two complex numbers $z_1 = a_1 + ib_1$ and $z_2 = a_2 + ib_2$, where $a_1, b_1, a_2, b_2 \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • To Prove: $\overline{z_1 + z_2} = \bar{z}_1 + \bar{z}_2$ and $\overline{z_1 - z_2} = \bar{z}_1 - \bar{z}_2$.

Proof: Consider the sum $z_1 + z_2$:

$$ z_1 + z_2 = (a_1 + ib_1) + (a_2 + ib_2) = (a_1 + a_2) + i(b_1 + b_2) $$

Applying the definition of the complex conjugate:

$$ \overline{z_1 + z_2} = (a_1 + a_2) - i(b_1 + b_2) $$

Rearranging terms using commutativity and associativity in $\mathbb{R}$:

$$ \overline{z_1 + z_2} = (a_1 - ib_1) + (a_2 - ib_2) $$

By definition, $a_1 - ib_1 = \bar{z}_1$ and $a_2 - ib_2 = \bar{z}_2$. Therefore:

$$ \overline{z_1 + z_2} = \bar{z}_1 + \bar{z}_2 $$

Similarly, for the difference:

$$ z_1 - z_2 = (a_1 - a_2) + i(b_1 - b_2) $$

$$ \overline{z_1 - z_2} = (a_1 - a_2) - i(b_1 - b_2) = (a_1 - ib_1) - (a_2 - ib_2) = \bar{z}_1 - \bar{z}_2 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Conjugate of a Product

$$ \overline{z_1 z_2} = \bar{z}_1 \bar{z}_2 $$

  • Given: $z_1 = a_1 + ib_1$ and $z_2 = a_2 + ib_2$, where $a_1, b_1, a_2, b_2 \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • To Prove: $\overline{z_1 z_2} = \bar{z}_1 \cdot \bar{z}_2$.

Proof: Compute the product $z_1 z_2$:

$$ z_1 z_2 = (a_1 + ib_1)(a_2 + ib_2) = a_1 a_2 + i a_1 b_2 + i b_1 a_2 + i^2 b_1 b_2 $$

Using $i^2 = -1$:

$$ z_1 z_2 = (a_1 a_2 - b_1 b_2) + i(a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_1) $$

Taking the conjugate of the product:

$$ \overline{z_1 z_2} = (a_1 a_2 - b_1 b_2) - i(a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_1) \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)} $$

Now evaluate the product of individual conjugates $\bar{z}_1 \bar{z}_2$:

$$ \bar{z}_1 = a_1 - ib_1, \quad \bar{z}_2 = a_2 - ib_2 $$

$$ \bar{z}_1 \bar{z}_2 = (a_1 - ib_1)(a_2 - ib_2) = a_1 a_2 - i a_1 b_2 - i b_1 a_2 + i^2 b_1 b_2 $$

Using $i^2 = -1$:

$$ \bar{z}_1 \bar{z}_2 = a_1 a_2 - i(a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_1) - b_1 b_2 = (a_1 a_2 - b_1 b_2) - i(a_1 b_2 + a_2 b_1) \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)} $$

Comparing (Equation 1) and (Equation 2):

$$ \overline{z_1 z_2} = \bar{z}_1 \bar{z}_2 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: Conjugate of a Quotient

$$ \overline{\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)} = \frac{\bar{z}_1}{\bar{z}_2}, \quad z_2 \neq 0 $$

  • Given: $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$ with $z_2 \neq 0$.
  • To Prove: $\overline{\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)} = \frac{\bar{z}_1}{\bar{z}_2}$.

Proof: Let $w = \frac{z_1}{z_2}$. Then:

$$ z_1 = w \cdot z_2 $$

Take the conjugate on both sides:

$$ \bar{z}_1 = \overline{w \cdot z_2} $$

By Theorem 2 ($\overline{u \cdot v} = \bar{u} \cdot \bar{v}$):

$$ \bar{z}_1 = \bar{w} \cdot \bar{z}_2 $$

Since $z_2 \neq 0$, $|z_2| > 0 \implies \bar{z}_2 \neq 0$. Dividing both sides by $\bar{z}_2$:

$$ \bar{w} = \frac{\bar{z}_1}{\bar{z}_2} $$

Substituting back $w = \frac{z_1}{z_2}$:

$$ \overline{\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)} = \frac{\bar{z}_1}{\bar{z}_2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 4: Modulus of a Product

$$ |z_1 z_2| = |z_1| |z_2| $$

  • Given: $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$.
  • To Prove: $|z_1 z_2| = |z_1| |z_2|$.

Proof: Recall the property $|w|^2 = w \bar{w}$ for any complex number $w$.
Let $w = z_1 z_2$. Then:

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = (z_1 z_2) \overline{(z_1 z_2)} $$

Using Theorem 2 ($\overline{z_1 z_2} = \bar{z}_1 \bar{z}_2$):

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = (z_1 z_2)(\bar{z}_1 \bar{z}_2) $$

By associativity and commutativity of multiplication in $\mathbb{C}$:

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = (z_1 \bar{z}_1)(z_2 \bar{z}_2) $$

Since $z_1 \bar{z}_1 = |z_1|^2$ and $z_2 \bar{z}_2 = |z_2|^2$:

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 |z_2|^2 = (|z_1| |z_2|)^2 $$

Since modulus is non-negative by definition ($|z_1 z_2| \ge 0$ and $|z_1||z_2| \ge 0$), taking the principal square root of both sides gives:

$$ |z_1 z_2| = |z_1| |z_2| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 5: Modulus of a Quotient

$$ \left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}, \quad z_2 \neq 0 $$

  • Given: $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$ with $z_2 \neq 0$.
  • To Prove: $\left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$.

Proof: Let $q = \frac{z_1}{z_2}$. Then:

$$ z_1 = q \cdot z_2 $$

Taking the modulus of both sides:

$$ |z_1| = |q \cdot z_2| $$

Applying Theorem 4 ($|u \cdot v| = |u| |v|$):

$$ |z_1| = |q| \cdot |z_2| $$

Since $z_2 \neq 0$, $|z_2| \neq 0$. Dividing both sides by $|z_2|$:

$$ |q| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|} $$

Substituting back $q = \frac{z_1}{z_2}$:

$$ \left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 6: The Triangle Inequality in $\mathbb{C}$

$$ |z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2| $$

  • Given: $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$.
  • To Prove: $|z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2|$.

Proof: Consider $|z_1 + z_2|^2$:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = (z_1 + z_2) \overline{(z_1 + z_2)} $$

Using $\overline{z_1 + z_2} = \bar{z}_1 + \bar{z}_2$:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = (z_1 + z_2)(\bar{z}_1 + \bar{z}_2) $$

Expanding via distributivity:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = z_1 \bar{z}_1 + z_1 \bar{z}_2 + z_2 \bar{z}_1 + z_2 \bar{z}_2 $$

Substitute $z_1 \bar{z}_1 = |z_1|^2$ and $z_2 \bar{z}_2 = |z_2|^2$:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 + (z_1 \bar{z}_2 + z_2 \bar{z}_1) $$

Notice that:

$$ \overline{z_1 \bar{z}_2} = \bar{z}_1 \bar{\bar{z}}_2 = \bar{z}_1 z_2 = z_2 \bar{z}_1 $$

For any complex number $w$, $w + \bar{w} = 2 \operatorname{Re}(w)$. Setting $w = z_1 \bar{z}_2$:

$$ z_1 \bar{z}_2 + z_2 \bar{z}_1 = w + \bar{w} = 2 \operatorname{Re}(z_1 \bar{z}_2) $$

Thus:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 + 2 \operatorname{Re}(z_1 \bar{z}_2) $$

Recall the real inequality $\operatorname{Re}(w) \le |w|$. Therefore:

$$ \operatorname{Re}(z_1 \bar{z}_2) \le |z_1 \bar{z}_2| = |z_1| |\bar{z}_2| = |z_1| |z_2| $$

Substitute this upper bound into the equation:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 \le |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 + 2 |z_1| |z_2| $$

Recognizing the perfect square on the right side:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 \le (|z_1| + |z_2|)^2 $$

Since both $|z_1 + z_2| \ge 0$ and $|z_1| + |z_2| \ge 0$, taking the principal square root of both sides preserves the inequality:

$$ |z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 7: The Parallelogram Law Identity

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 + |z_1 - z_2|^2 = 2(|z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2) $$

  • Given: $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$.
  • To Prove: $|z_1 + z_2|^2 + |z_1 - z_2|^2 = 2(|z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2)$.

Proof: From the expansion in Theorem 6:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 + z_1 \bar{z}_2 + z_2 \bar{z}_1 \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)} $$

Similarly, evaluate $|z_1 - z_2|^2$:

$$ |z_1 - z_2|^2 = (z_1 - z_2)\overline{(z_1 - z_2)} = (z_1 - z_2)(\bar{z}_1 - \bar{z}_2) $$

$$ |z_1 - z_2|^2 = z_1 \bar{z}_1 - z_1 \bar{z}_2 - z_2 \bar{z}_1 + z_2 \bar{z}_2 $$

$$ |z_1 - z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 - (z_1 \bar{z}_2 + z_2 \bar{z}_1) \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)} $$

Adding (Equation 1) and (Equation 2) directly:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 + |z_1 - z_2|^2 = \left[ |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 + (z_1 \bar{z}_2 + z_2 \bar{z}_1) \right] + \left[ |z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 - (z_1 \bar{z}_2 + z_2 \bar{z}_1) \right] $$

The cross terms $(z_1 \bar{z}_2 + z_2 \bar{z}_1)$ cancel out:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 + |z_1 - z_2|^2 = 2|z_1|^2 + 2|z_2|^2 = 2(|z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Geometric Note: In the parallelogram formed by $0, z_1, z_2,$ and $z_1 + z_2$, the diagonals are $z_1 + z_2$ and $z_1 - z_2$. This identity shows that the sum of the squares of the lengths of the diagonals equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the four sides.

1. PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & GEOMETRIC INTUITION

1.1 The Need for a 2-Dimensional Representation

In real analysis, real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ are represented as points on a one-dimensional continuum—the real number line. However, a complex number $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is fundamentally an ordered pair of real numbers:

$$ z = x + iy \iff (x, y) \in \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} $$

Because it requires two independent real degrees of freedom (the real component and the imaginary component), a single real line is insufficient.

Jean-Robert Argand (1806) and Carl Friedrich Gauss formalized the representation of complex numbers as points on a two-dimensional Cartesian plane. This plane is formally termed the Argand Plane, the Complex Plane, or the Gauss Plane.

1.2 Core Definitions and Terminology (Strict NCERT Alignment)

Definition 1: The Argand Plane ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

An Argand Plane is a two-dimensional Cartesian plane where: 1. The horizontal axis represents the real part of the complex number, designated as the Real Axis ($\text{Re}$-axis). 2. The vertical axis represents the imaginary part of the complex number, designated as the Imaginary Axis ($\text{Im}$-axis). 3. Each complex number $z = x + iy$ corresponds to a unique ordered pair $(x, y)$, and conversely, every point $P(x, y)$ in the plane corresponds uniquely to a complex number $z = x + iy$. This establishes a bijective mapping (one-to-one and onto) between $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Definition 2: Modulus of a Complex Number ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $z = x + iy$ where $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$. The modulus of $z$, denoted by $|z|$, is defined as the non-negative real number:

$$ |z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} $$

Geometric Interpretation: $|z|$ represents the Euclidean distance of the point $P(x, y)$ from the origin $O(0, 0)$ in the Argand plane.

Definition 3: Conjugate of a Complex Number ⭐⭐⭐⭐

For $z = x + iy$, the complex conjugate of $z$ is denoted by $\bar{z}$ and defined as:

$$ \bar{z} = x - iy $$

Geometric Interpretation: $\bar{z}$ is the reflection (or mirror image) of the point $P(x, y)$ across the real axis, representing the point $(x, -y)$.

Definition 4: Argument (or Amplitude) of a Complex Number ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $P(x, y)$ be the point representing a non-zero complex number $z = x + iy$. Let $OP = r = |z|$. The angle $\theta$ which the line segment $OP$ makes with the positive direction of the real axis in the anticlockwise sense is called the argument (or amplitude) of $z$, denoted by:

$$ \theta = \arg(z) \quad \text{or} \quad \text{amp}(z) $$

Because $\theta + 2k\pi$ ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$) satisfies the same directional cosine and sine values, the argument is multi-valued.

Definition 5: Principal Argument ($\text{Arg}(z)$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

To ensure uniqueness, the value of $\theta$ is restricted to the half-open interval:

$$ -\pi < \theta \le \pi \quad \text{or} \quad \theta \in (-\pi, \pi] $$

This uniquely defined value is called the Principal Argument of $z$, denoted with a capital letter as $\text{Arg}(z)$ (or simply $\arg(z)$ under NCERT conventions).

Note on Origin: For $z = 0 = 0 + 0i$, the line segment $OP$ degenerates into a single point. Hence, the argument of zero is undefined.

1.4 Polar and Euler Representations ⭐⭐⭐⭐

1. Polar Form (Modulus-Amplitude Form)

Any non-zero complex number $z = x + iy$ can be written in polar form as:

$$ x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta $$

$$ z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) $$

where $r = |z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} > 0$ and $\theta = \text{Arg}(z) \in (-\pi, \pi]$.

2. Euler's Formula

$$ e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \implies z = r e^{i\theta} $$

2. RIGOROUS DERIVATIONS AND STEP-BY-STEP PROOFS

Theorem 1: Relation Between Modulus and Conjugate ($z\bar{z} = |z|^2$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
For any complex number $z \in \mathbb{C}$, the product of $z$ and its conjugate $\bar{z}$ is purely real and equal to the square of its modulus:

$$ z\bar{z} = |z|^2 $$

Given:
Let $z = x + iy$, where $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$.

To Prove:

$$ z\bar{z} = |z|^2 $$

Proof:
By definition of the complex conjugate:

$$ \bar{z} = x - iy $$

Multiplying $z$ and $\bar{z}$:

$$ z\bar{z} = (x + iy)(x - iy) $$

Using the algebraic identity $(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$:

$$ z\bar{z} = x^2 - (iy)^2 $$

Since $i^2 = -1$, we evaluate the imaginary term:

$$ (iy)^2 = i^2 y^2 = (-1)y^2 = -y^2 $$

Substituting this back into the product:

$$ z\bar{z} = x^2 - (-y^2) = x^2 + y^2 $$

Now, by definition of the modulus:

$$ |z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} $$

Squaring both sides:

$$ |z|^2 = x^2 + y^2 $$

Comparing the two expressions:

$$ z\bar{z} = |z|^2 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Multiplicative Property of Modulus ($|z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2|$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
The modulus of the product of two complex numbers is equal to the product of their individual moduli.

Given:
Two complex numbers $z_1 = x_1 + i y_1$ and $z_2 = x_2 + i y_2$, where $x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2 \in \mathbb{R}$.

To Prove:

$$ |z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2| $$

Proof:
Method 1 (Algebraic):
Compute the product $z_1 z_2$:

$$ z_1 z_2 = (x_1 + i y_1)(x_2 + i y_2) = (x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2) + i(x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1) $$

Here, the real part is $\text{Re}(z_1 z_2) = x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2$ and the imaginary part is $\text{Im}(z_1 z_2) = x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1$.

By the definition of modulus:

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = (x_1 x_2 - y_1 y_2)^2 + (x_1 y_2 + x_2 y_1)^2 $$

Expanding both squares using $(a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$ and $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$:

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = (x_1^2 x_2^2 - 2x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2 + y_1^2 y_2^2) + (x_1^2 y_2^2 + 2x_1 y_2 x_2 y_1 + x_2^2 y_1^2) $$

Notice that the cross-terms cancel:

$$ -2x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2 + 2x_1 y_2 x_2 y_1 = 0 $$

Rearranging and grouping the remaining terms:

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = x_1^2 x_2^2 + y_1^2 y_2^2 + x_1^2 y_2^2 + x_2^2 y_1^2 $$

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = x_1^2(x_2^2 + y_2^2) + y_1^2(x_2^2 + y_2^2) $$

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = (x_1^2 + y_1^2)(x_2^2 + y_2^2) $$

By definition of modulus:

$$ |z_1|^2 = x_1^2 + y_1^2 \quad \text{and} \quad |z_2|^2 = x_2^2 + y_2^2 $$

Therefore:

$$ |z_1 z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 |z_2|^2 = (|z_1||z_2|)^2 $$

Taking the principal (positive) square root on both sides (since modulus is non-negative):

$$ |z_1 z_2| = |z_1||z_2| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: The Triangle Inequality ($|z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2|$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
For any two complex numbers $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$, the modulus of their sum is at most the sum of their individual moduli:

$$ |z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2| $$

Prerequisites:
1. $z + \bar{z} = 2\text{Re}(z)$ 2. $\text{Re}(z) \le |\text{Re}(z)| \le |z|$ 3. $\overline{z_1 + z_2} = \bar{z}_1 + \bar{z}_2$

Proof:
Consider the expression $|z_1 + z_2|^2$. Using Theorem 1 ($|w|^2 = w\bar{w}$):

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = (z_1 + z_2)\overline{(z_1 + z_2)} $$

Using the additive property of conjugates ($\overline{w_1 + w_2} = \bar{w}_1 + \bar{w}_2$):

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = (z_1 + z_2)(\bar{z}_1 + \bar{z}_2) $$

Expanding the product using the distributive property:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = z_1\bar{z}_1 + z_1\bar{z}_2 + z_2\bar{z}_1 + z_2\bar{z}_2 $$

Substituting $z_1\bar{z}_1 = |z_1|^2$ and $z_2\bar{z}_2 = |z_2|^2$:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 + (z_1\bar{z}_2 + z_2\bar{z}_1) + |z_2|^2 $$

Notice that:

$$ \overline{z_1\bar{z}_2} = \bar{z}_1\bar{\bar{z}}_2 = \bar{z}_1 z_2 = z_2\bar{z}_1 $$

Therefore, the sum $z_1\bar{z}_2 + z_2\bar{z}_1$ is of the form $w + \bar{w}$ where $w = z_1\bar{z}_2$.
Since $w + \bar{w} = 2\text{Re}(w)$:

$$ z_1\bar{z}_2 + z_2\bar{z}_1 = 2\text{Re}(z_1\bar{z}_2) $$

Hence:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 = |z_1|^2 + 2\text{Re}(z_1\bar{z}_2) + |z_2|^2 $$

Since the real part of any complex number is always less than or equal to its modulus ($\text{Re}(w) \le |w|$):

$$ \text{Re}(z_1\bar{z}_2) \le |z_1\bar{z}_2| = |z_1||\bar{z}_2| $$

Since $|\bar{z}_2| = |z_2|$:

$$ \text{Re}(z_1\bar{z}_2) \le |z_1||z_2| $$

Substituting this inequality:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 \le |z_1|^2 + 2|z_1||z_2| + |z_2|^2 $$

The right-hand side is a perfect square:

$$ |z_1 + z_2|^2 \le (|z_1| + |z_2|)^2 $$

Taking the positive square root on both sides:

$$ |z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2| $$

Geometric Interpretation in Argand Plane:
In the Argand plane, let $O$ be the origin $(0,0)$, point $A$ represent $z_1$, and point $B$ represent $z_1 + z_2$.
Then $\vec{OA} = z_1$, $\vec{AB} = z_2$, and $\vec{OB} = z_1 + z_2$.
In $\triangle OAB$, the length of any side cannot exceed the sum of the lengths of the other two sides:

$$ OB \le OA + AB \implies |z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2| $$

Equality holds if and only if $O, A, B$ are collinear and $A, B$ lie on the same ray originating from $O$ (i.e., $\text{Arg}(z_1) = \text{Arg}(z_2)$).

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 4: Argument of Products and Quotients ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
Let $z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$ with $z_1 \neq 0, z_2 \neq 0$. Then: 1. $\arg(z_1 z_2) = \arg(z_1) + \arg(z_2) + 2k\pi$ 2. $\arg\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right) = \arg(z_1) - \arg(z_2) + 2k\pi$
where $k \in \{-1, 0, 1\}$ is chosen such that the resulting angle belongs to $(-\pi, \pi]$.

Proof of (1):
Let $z_1 = r_1(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1)$ and $z_2 = r_2(\cos\theta_2 + i\sin\theta_2)$, where $\theta_1 = \arg(z_1)$ and $\theta_2 = \arg(z_2)$.
Multiplying $z_1$ and $z_2$:

$$ z_1 z_2 = \left[r_1(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1)\right] \cdot \left[r_2(\cos\theta_2 + i\sin\theta_2)\right] $$

$$ z_1 z_2 = r_1 r_2 \left[(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2) + i(\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2)\right] $$

Applying trigonometric addition formulae:

$$ \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = \cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 $$

$$ \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = \sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2 $$

We get:

$$ z_1 z_2 = r_1 r_2 \left[\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) + i\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)\right] $$

This expression is in standard polar form with modulus $R = r_1 r_2$ and angle $\Theta = \theta_1 + \theta_2$.
To bring $\Theta$ into the principal interval $(-\pi, \pi]$, we add an appropriate integer multiple of $2\pi$:

$$ \text{Arg}(z_1 z_2) = \theta_1 + \theta_2 + 2k\pi = \text{Arg}(z_1) + \text{Arg}(z_2) + 2k\pi \quad (k \in \{-1, 0, 1\}) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

1. Pedagogical Theory, Foundations & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 The Historical & Structural Crisis of the Real Number Line

In the field of real numbers $(\mathbb{R}, +, \cdot)$, the operation of squaring any non-zero element $x \in \mathbb{R}$ is strictly positive:

$$ \forall x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}, \quad x^2 > 0 $$

Consequently, simple polynomial equations of the form:

$$ x^2 + 1 = 0 \iff x^2 = -1 $$

possess no solution in $\mathbb{R}$. For centuries, this was viewed as a defect of the equation rather than the incompleteness of the number system.

To resolve this defect, the imaginary unit $\iota$ (iota) was formally introduced by Leonhard Euler (1777) such that:

$$ \iota^2 = -1 \implies \iota = \sqrt{-1} $$

By adjoining $\iota$ to the field of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, we construct the field of Complex Numbers $\mathbb{C}$:

$$ \mathbb{C} = \{ z = x + \iota y \mid x, y \in \mathbb{R}, \; \iota = \sqrt{-1} \} $$

1.2 The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (Gauss, 1799) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem Statement:
Every non-constant single-variable polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root.

Direct Corollary (Factor Theorem Application)

Every polynomial $P_n(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$ of degree $n \ge 1$ ($a_n \neq 0$) with complex (or real) coefficients factors completely over $\mathbb{C}$ into exactly $n$ linear factors:

$$ P_n(x) = a_n(x - \alpha_1)(x - \alpha_2)\cdots(x - \alpha_n) $$

where $\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \dots, \alpha_n \in \mathbb{C}$ are the roots (counted with algebraic multiplicity).

Pedagogical Consequence for Quadratic Equations ($n = 2$)

A quadratic equation:

$$ ax^2 + bx + c = 0, \quad a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}, \; a \neq 0 $$

has exactly two roots in the complex number field $\mathbb{C}$.

1.3 Classification of Roots via the Discriminant $\Delta$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The Discriminant is defined as:

$$ D = \Delta = b^2 - 4ac $$

2. Rigorous Proofs and Mathematical Derivations

Proof 2.1: Derivation of the Complex Quadratic Formula via Completing the Square ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem Statement

Given the general quadratic equation:

$$ ax^2 + bx + c = 0, \quad a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}, \; a \neq 0 $$

under the condition that the discriminant $D = b^2 - 4ac < 0$, derive the explicit roots in $\mathbb{C}$.

Given

  • Quadratic equation: $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$
  • $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a \neq 0$
  • Condition: $D = b^2 - 4ac < 0 \implies 4ac - b^2 > 0$

To Prove

The solutions are:

$$ x = \frac{-b \pm \iota \sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} $$

Step-by-Step Derivation

Step 1: Normalization of Leading Coefficient
Since $a \neq 0$, divide both sides of the equation by $a$:

$$ x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{c}{a} = 0 $$

Step 2: Transposition of Constant Term
Subtract $\dfrac{c}{a}$ from both sides:

$$ x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a} $$

Step 3: Completing the Square
To make the left-hand side a perfect square trinomial of the form $\left(x + \dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2$, add $\left(\dfrac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \dfrac{b^2}{4a^2}$ to both sides:

$$ x^2 + 2\left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)x + \left(\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a} $$

Step 4: Combining and Simplifying the Algebraic Terms
Condense the left-hand side and bring the right-hand side over a common denominator $4a^2$:

$$ \left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2} $$

Step 5: Factorization of the Negative Discriminant
Since $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, we can express $(b^2 - 4ac)$ as $-(4ac - b^2)$, where $(4ac - b^2) > 0$:

$$ \left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{-(4ac - b^2)}{4a^2} = (-1) \cdot \frac{4ac - b^2}{4a^2} $$

Step 6: Extraction of Square Roots in $\mathbb{C}$
Substitute $\iota^2 = -1$:

$$ \left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \iota^2 \cdot \frac{4ac - b^2}{4a^2} $$

Taking the square root on both sides:

$$ x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm \sqrt{\iota^2 \cdot \frac{4ac - b^2}{4a^2}} = \pm \frac{\iota \sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} $$

Step 7: Isolation of $x$

$$ x = -\frac{b}{2a} \pm \frac{\iota \sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} = \frac{-b \pm \iota \sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} $$

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: The Complex Conjugate Root Theorem ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem Statement

Let $P(x) = ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ be a quadratic equation with real coefficients ($a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$, $a \neq 0$). If a complex number $z_0 = u + \iota v$ ($u, v \in \mathbb{R}, v \neq 0$) is a root of the equation, then its complex conjugate $\overline{z_0} = u - \iota v$ must also be a root.

Given

  • $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ with $a \neq 0$
  • $z_0 = u + \iota v$ ($v \neq 0$) such that $P(z_0) = a z_0^2 + b z_0 + c = 0$

To Prove

$$ P(\overline{z_0}) = a (\overline{z_0})^2 + b (\overline{z_0}) + c = 0 $$

Fundamental Lemmas of Complex Conjugation

For any $z, z_1, z_2 \in \mathbb{C}$: 1. $\overline{z_1 + z_2} = \overline{z_1} + \overline{z_2}$ (Additive Homomorphism) 2. $\overline{z_1 \cdot z_2} = \overline{z_1} \cdot \overline{z_2} \implies \overline{z^n} = (\overline{z})^n$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$ (Multiplicative Homomorphism) 3. For any $k \in \mathbb{R}$, $\overline{k} = k$ (Real Fixed Points)

Step-by-Step Proof

Step 1: Apply the Root Condition
Since $z_0$ is a root of $P(x) = 0$:

$$ a z_0^2 + b z_0 + c = 0 $$

Step 2: Take the Conjugate of the Entire Equation

$$ \overline{a z_0^2 + b z_0 + c} = \overline{0} $$

Step 3: Distribute the Conjugation Operator Over Sums
Using Lemma 1:

$$ \overline{a z_0^2} + \overline{b z_0} + \overline{c} = 0 $$

Step 4: Distribute Over Products and Real Coefficients
Using Lemma 2 and Lemma 3, since $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$ \overline{a} = a, \quad \overline{b} = b, \quad \overline{c} = c $$

$$ \overline{a z_0^2} = \overline{a} \cdot \overline{z_0^2} = a \cdot (\overline{z_0})^2 $$

$$ \overline{b z_0} = \overline{b} \cdot \overline{z_0} = b \cdot \overline{z_0} $$

Step 5: Substitute Back into the Equation

$$ a (\overline{z_0})^2 + b (\overline{z_0}) + c = 0 $$

This statement proves that $\overline{z_0}$ satisfies the original equation $P(x) = 0$.
Therefore, $\overline{z_0} = u - \iota v$ is a root.

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: Verification of Vieta's Relations for Conjugate Complex Roots ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given

Roots $\alpha = u + \iota v$ and $\beta = \overline{\alpha} = u - \iota v$, where:

$$ u = -\frac{b}{2a}, \quad v = \frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}, \quad (4ac - b^2 > 0) $$

To Prove

  1. $\alpha + \beta = -\dfrac{b}{a}$
  2. $\alpha \beta = \dfrac{c}{a}$

Proof of Sum of Roots

$$ \alpha + \beta = (u + \iota v) + (u - \iota v) = 2u $$

Substitute $u = -\dfrac{b}{2a}$:

$$ \alpha + \beta = 2\left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right) = -\frac{b}{a} $$

Proof of Product of Roots

$$ \alpha \beta = (u + \iota v)(u - \iota v) = u^2 - (\iota v)^2 = u^2 - \iota^2 v^2 = u^2 + v^2 $$

Substitute $u$ and $v$:

$$ \alpha \beta = \left(-\frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} + \frac{4ac - b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2 + 4ac - b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{4ac}{4a^2} = \frac{c}{a} $$

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

💡 Example 3.4: Determining Unknown Real Parameters via Equality
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: If $(x - iy)(3 + 5i)$ is the complex conjugate of $-6 - 24i$, find the real numbers $x$ and $y$.

Detailed Solution: * Step 1: The conjugate of $-6 - 24i$ is defined by flipping the sign of the imaginary component:

$$ \overline{-6 - 24i} = -6 + 24i \quad \text{[0.5 Mark]} $$

  • Step 2: Expand the LHS:

$$ (x - iy)(3 + 5i) = 3x + 5xi - 3yi - 5yi^2 = (3x + 5y) + i(5x - 3y) \quad \text{[1.5 Marks]} $$

  • Step 3: Equate LHS to the conjugate via complex equality:

$$ (3x + 5y) + i(5x - 3y) = -6 + 24i $$

Equating Real and Imaginary components:

$$ \begin{cases} 3x + 5y = -6 & \text{--- (Eq. 1)} \\ 5x - 3y = 24 & \text{--- (Eq. 2)} \end{cases} \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

  • Step 4: Solve the simultaneous linear system: Multiply (Eq. 1) by 3 and (Eq. 2) by 5:

$$ 9x + 15y = -18 $$

$$ 25x - 15y = 120 $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Algebraic Pitfalls

Trap 1: The False Radical Fallacy

  • The Error: A student writes:

$$ -1 = i^2 = \sqrt{-1}\cdot\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{1} = 1 \implies -1 = 1 $$

  • Why it loses marks: The product rule $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ is mathematically illegal when both $a < 0$ and $b < 0$ (Theorem 2.3).
  • Remedial Strategy: Always isolate $i$ first: $\sqrt{-a} = i\sqrt{a}$ for $a > 0$ before applying algebraic products.

Trap 2: The Sign Error in Multiplicative Inversion

  • The Error: Writing the reciprocal of $a + ib$ as $\frac{1}{a+ib} = \frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{ib}$.
  • Why it loses marks: Field inversion in $\mathbb{C}$ does not distribute across addition.
  • Remedial Strategy: Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $(a - ib)$ to produce the positive real denominator $a^2 + b^2$:

$$ \frac{1}{a + ib} = \frac{a - ib}{a^2 + b^2} $$

Trap 3: Imaginary Unit Inclusion in $\text{Im}(z)$

  • The Error: Given $z = 7 - 9i$, writing $\text{Im}(z) = -9i$.
  • Why it loses marks: By definition, for $z = x + iy$, $\text{Im}(z) = y \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • Remedial Strategy: $\text{Im}(z)$ is strictly the real coefficient $-9$.

Trap 4: Illegal Inequality Comparisons

  • The Error: Concluding that $(3 + 4i) > (1 + 2i)$ because $|3+4i| = 5 > \sqrt{5}$.
  • Why it loses marks: The complex number field $\mathbb{C}$ cannot be endowed with an order relation compatible with its field operations (unlike $\mathbb{R}$).
  • Remedial Strategy: Never write $z_1 > z_2$ or $z_1 < z_2$ unless an operator that yields a real value (such as modulus $|z|$) is applied.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Shortcuts

5.1 The Divisibility-by-4 Rule for Large Powers

Since $100$ is divisible by $4$, any positive integer is congruent modulo 4 to its last two digits:

$$ i^{38749287391823} \equiv i^{23} = i^{4(5)+3} = i^3 = -i $$

You only need to inspect the last two digits to find the remainder.

5.2 Common Structural Values

Memorizing these high-frequency algebraic fractions accelerates simplification in competitive exams (JEE Main / Advanced / BITSAT):

$$ \frac{1+i}{1-i} = \frac{(1+i)^2}{1 - i^2} = \frac{1 + 2i - 1}{2} = \frac{2i}{2} = i $$

$$ \frac{1-i}{1+i} = \frac{(1-i)^2}{1 - i^2} = \frac{1 - 2i - 1}{2} = \frac{-2i}{2} = -i $$

$$ \frac{1}{i} = -i $$

$$ (1+i)^2 = 2i \qquad (1-i)^2 = -2i $$

5.3 Smallest Positive Integer Exponent Pattern

A frequent high-weight problem asks for the minimum $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that:

$$ \left(\frac{1+i}{1-i}\right)^n = 1 $$

Using Shortcut 5.2:

$$ \left(\frac{1+i}{1-i}\right)^n = 1 \iff i^n = 1 $$

The smallest positive integer $n \in \mathbb{N}$ satisfying $i^n = 1$ is:

$$ n = 4 $$

5.4 Block Summation Principle

For any series involving consecutive powers of $i$:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{N} i^k $$

  1. Divide $N$ by 4 to find the remainder $r = N \pmod 4$.
  2. The sum reduces to the sum of the first $r$ terms (or the last $r$ terms):

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{N} i^k = \sum_{k=1}^{r} i^k $$

  • Example: For $\sum_{k=1}^{102} i^k$, since $102 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$, the sum is simply:

$$ i^1 + i^2 = i - 1 = -1 + i $$

💡 Example 3: Algebraic Conjugate Proof (NCERT Exemplar)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: If $(x + iy)^3 = u + iv$, prove that:

$$ \frac{u}{x} + \frac{v}{y} = 4(x^2 - y^2) $$

[NCERT Exemplar / High Board Frequency ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ | 4 Marks]

Solution: - Step 1: Expand the Left-Hand Side using Binomial Identity Given $(x + iy)^3 = u + iv$. Using $(A + B)^3 = A^3 + 3A^2 B + 3AB^2 + B^3$:

$$ (x + iy)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2(iy) + 3x(iy)^2 + (iy)^3 $$

$$ = x^3 + 3ix^2 y + 3x(i^2 y^2) + i^3 y^3 $$

Using $i^2 = -1$ and $i^3 = -i$:

$$ (x + iy)^3 = x^3 + 3ix^2 y - 3xy^2 - i y^3 $$

$$ = (x^3 - 3xy^2) + i(3x^2 y - y^3) $$

(Scoring: 1.5 Marks)

  • Step 2: Equate Real and Imaginary Parts

$$ (x^3 - 3xy^2) + i(3x^2 y - y^3) = u + iv $$

Equating real parts:

$$ u = x^3 - 3xy^2 = x(x^2 - 3y^2) \implies \frac{u}{x} = x^2 - 3y^2 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Equating imaginary parts:

$$ v = 3x^2 y - y^3 = y(3x^2 - y^2) \implies \frac{v}{y} = 3x^2 - y^2 \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

(Scoring: 1.5 Marks)

  • Step 3: Add Equations (1) and (2)

$$ \frac{u}{x} + \frac{v}{y} = (x^2 - 3y^2) + (3x^2 - y^2) = 4x^2 - 4y^2 = 4(x^2 - y^2) $$

$$ \text{Hence Proved.} $$

(Scoring: 1.0 Mark)

$$ (x^2 + y^2)^2 = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{c^2 + d^2} $$

[NCERT Exemplar | 3 Marks]

Solution: - Step 1: Express Modulus on Both Sides Given:

$$ x - iy = \sqrt{\frac{a - ib}{c - id}} $$

Squaring both sides:

$$ (x - iy)^2 = \frac{a - ib}{c - id} $$

(Scoring: 1.0 Mark)

  • Step 2: Apply Modulus to Both Sides

$$ \left|(x - iy)^2\right| = \left|\frac{a - ib}{c - id}\right| $$

Using $|z^2| = |z|^2$ and $\left|\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right| = \frac{|z_1|}{|z_2|}$:

$$ |x - iy|^2 = \frac{|a - ib|}{|c - id|} $$

(Scoring: 1.0 Mark)

  • Step 3: Calculate the Absolute Values

$$ |x - iy| = \sqrt{x^2 + (-y)^2} = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \implies |x - iy|^2 = x^2 + y^2 $$

💡 Example 5: Unimodular Modulus Reduction
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are different complex numbers with $|\beta| = 1$, find the value of:

$$ \left| \frac{\beta - \alpha}{1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta} \right| $$

[NCERT Miscellaneous Exercise, Q17 / ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ | 4 Marks]

Solution: - Step 1: Set Up Using the Property $|z|^2 = z \bar{z}$ Let $E = \left| \frac{\beta - \alpha}{1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta} \right|$. Then:

$$ E^2 = \left| \frac{\beta - \alpha}{1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta} \right|^2 = \left( \frac{\beta - \alpha}{1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta} \right) \overline{\left( \frac{\beta - \alpha}{1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta} \right)} $$

(Scoring: 1.0 Mark)

  • Step 2: Distribute the Conjugate Over Quotient, Difference, and Product

$$ \overline{\left( \frac{\beta - \alpha}{1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta} \right)} = \frac{\bar{\beta} - \bar{\alpha}}{\overline{1} - \overline{\bar{\alpha}\beta}} = \frac{\bar{\beta} - \bar{\alpha}}{1 - \alpha \bar{\beta}} $$

Therefore:

$$ E^2 = \left( \frac{\beta - \alpha}{1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta} \right) \left( \frac{\bar{\beta} - \bar{\alpha}}{1 - \alpha \bar{\beta}} \right) $$

(Scoring: 1.0 Mark)

  • Step 3: Expand Numerator and Denominator Numerator:

$$ N = (\beta - \alpha)(\bar{\beta} - \bar{\alpha}) = \beta \bar{\beta} - \beta \bar{\alpha} - \alpha \bar{\beta} + \alpha \bar{\alpha} $$

Since $\beta \bar{\beta} = |\beta|^2$ and $\alpha \bar{\alpha} = |\alpha|^2$:

$$ N = |\beta|^2 - \beta \bar{\alpha} - \alpha \bar{\beta} + |\alpha|^2 $$

Given $|\beta| = 1 \implies |\beta|^2 = 1$:

$$ N = 1 - \beta \bar{\alpha} - \alpha \bar{\beta} + |\alpha|^2 $$

Denominator:

$$ D = (1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta)(1 - \alpha \bar{\beta}) = 1 - \alpha \bar{\beta} - \bar{\alpha}\beta + (\bar{\alpha}\beta)(\alpha \bar{\beta}) $$

Rearranging the last term:

$$ (\bar{\alpha}\beta)(\alpha \bar{\beta}) = (\alpha \bar{\alpha})(\beta \bar{\beta}) = |\alpha|^2 |\beta|^2 = |\alpha|^2(1) = |\alpha|^2 $$

So:

$$ D = 1 - \alpha \bar{\beta} - \bar{\alpha}\beta + |\alpha|^2 $$

(Scoring: 1.5 Marks)

  • Step 4: Evaluate the Quotient Notice that $N = D$:

$$ E^2 = \frac{1 - \beta \bar{\alpha} - \alpha \bar{\beta} + |\alpha|^2}{1 - \alpha \bar{\beta} - \bar{\alpha}\beta + |\alpha|^2} = 1 $$

Since $E \ge 0$, taking the square root gives:

$$ E = \left| \frac{\beta - \alpha}{1 - \bar{\alpha}\beta} \right| = 1 $$

(Scoring: 0.5 Mark)

SECTION 4: Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap 1: The Multiplicative Distributivity of the Radical Trap

  • Incorrect Step:

$$ \sqrt{-a} \cdot \sqrt{-b} = \sqrt{(-a)(-b)} = \sqrt{ab} $$

  • Why It Fails: The identity $\sqrt{u} \cdot \sqrt{v} = \sqrt{uv}$ is valid in real analysis if and only if at least one of $u$ or $v$ is non-negative. When both are negative, applying it produces the false equality $-1 = 1$.
  • Correct Formulation:

$$ \sqrt{-a} \cdot \sqrt{-b} = (i\sqrt{a})(i\sqrt{b}) = i^2 \sqrt{ab} = -\sqrt{ab} $$

Trap 2: The "Sign of Imaginary Part" in Non-Standard Order

  • Incorrect Step: For $z = -3i + 4$, a student rapidly writes $\bar{z} = -3i - 4$.
  • Why It Fails: The student mechanically negated the second term without checking which term is imaginary. Conjugation targets the imaginary part only.
  • Correct Formulation: Rewrite in standard form $z = 4 - 3i \implies \bar{z} = 4 + 3i$.

Trap 3: Treating Complex Inequality Comparisons as Valid

  • Incorrect Step: Writing $2 + 3i > 1 + 4i$ or $z_1 \ge z_2$.
  • Why It Fails: The field of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ is not an ordered field. Statements like $z_1 > z_2$ are undefined unless both numbers are strictly real.
  • Rule: Inequalities only apply to real-valued functions of complex numbers, such as $|z_1| > |z_2|$ or $\operatorname{Re}(z_1) > \operatorname{Re}(z_2)$.

Trap 4: Expanding Ratios Without Modulus Properties

  • Inefficient / Error-Prone Method: When asked to find $|z|$ for $z = \frac{(1+2i)^3}{(2-i)^2}$, students often expand the numerator into $A + iB$, expand the denominator into $C + iD$, multiply by the conjugate $(C - iD)$, simplify the entire fraction, and finally calculate $\sqrt{\text{Re}^2 + \text{Im}^2}$.
  • Examiner Trap: This wastes valuable exam time and increases algebraic errors with high powers of $i$.
  • Correct Approach: Apply $|z_1 / z_2| = |z_1| / |z_2|$ and $|w^n| = |w|^n$ immediately:

$$ |z| = \frac{|1+2i|^3}{|2-i|^2} = \frac{(\sqrt{1+4})^3}{(\sqrt{4+1})^2} = \frac{(\sqrt{5})^3}{(\sqrt{5})^2} = \sqrt{5} $$

Trap 5: Forgetting to Conjugate All Terms in Equations

  • Incorrect Step: Conjugating an equation $z^2 + 2\bar{z} + 3i = 0$ as $\bar{z}^2 + 2z + 3i = 0$.
  • Why It Fails: The student forgot to conjugate the coefficient $3i$.
  • Correct Step: $\overline{z^2 + 2\bar{z} + 3i} = 0 \implies \bar{z}^2 + 2z - 3i = 0$.

Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the modulus and principal argument of each of the following complex numbers, and represent them in polar form: 1. $z_1 = 1 + i\sqrt{3}$ 2. $z_2 = -1 + i\sqrt{3}$ 3. $z_3 = -\sqrt{3} - i$ 4. $z_4 = 1 - i$

Detailed Step-by-Step Solutions:

Part 1: $z_1 = 1 + i\sqrt{3}$

  • Step 1: Identify components:
    $x = 1$, $y = \sqrt{3}$.
  • Step 2: Calculate Modulus:

$$ r = |z_1| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \sqrt{1^2 + (\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{1 + 3} = \sqrt{4} = 2 $$

  • Step 3: Calculate Reference Angle $\alpha$:

$$ \tan\alpha = \left|\frac{y}{x}\right| = \left|\frac{\sqrt{3}}{1}\right| = \sqrt{3} \implies \alpha = \frac{\pi}{3} $$

  • Step 4: Determine Quadrant & Principal Argument $\theta$:
    Since $x > 0$ and $y > 0$, point $(1, \sqrt{3})$ lies in Quadrant I.

$$ \theta = \alpha = \frac{\pi}{3} $$

  • Step 5: Polar Form:

$$ z_1 = 2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{3}\right) $$

Part 2: $z_2 = -1 + i\sqrt{3}$

  • Step 1: Identify components:
    $x = -1$, $y = \sqrt{3}$.
  • Step 2: Calculate Modulus:

$$ r = |z_2| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + (\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{1 + 3} = 2 $$

  • Step 3: Calculate Reference Angle $\alpha$:

$$ \tan\alpha = \left|\frac{\sqrt{3}}{-1}\right| = \sqrt{3} \implies \alpha = \frac{\pi}{3} $$

  • Step 4: Determine Quadrant & Principal Argument $\theta$:
    Since $x < 0$ and $y > 0$, point $(-1, \sqrt{3})$ lies in Quadrant II.

$$ \theta = \pi - \alpha = \pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3} $$

  • Step 1: Identify components:
    $x = -\sqrt{3}$, $y = -1$.
  • Step 2: Calculate Modulus:

$$ r = |z_3| = \sqrt{(-\sqrt{3})^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2 $$

  • Step 3: Calculate Reference Angle $\alpha$:

$$ \tan\alpha = \left|\frac{-1}{-\sqrt{3}}\right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \implies \alpha = \frac{\pi}{6} $$

  • Step 4: Determine Quadrant & Principal Argument $\theta$:
    Since $x < 0$ and $y < 0$, point $(-\sqrt{3}, -1)$ lies in Quadrant III.

$$ \theta = -(\pi - \alpha) = -\left(\pi - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = -\frac{5\pi}{6} $$

$$ z_3 = 2\left(\cos\left(-\frac{5\pi}{6}\right) + i\sin\left(-\frac{5\pi}{6}\right)\right) $$

💡 Example 2: Conversion of an Unsimplified Quotient to Polar Form ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

(CBSE Board Standard / NCERT Miscellaneous)

Problem: Convert the complex number $z = \frac{1 + 7i}{(2 - i)^2}$ into polar form.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Step 1: Simplify the Denominator:

$$ (2 - i)^2 = 2^2 - 2(2)(i) + i^2 = 4 - 4i + (-1) = 3 - 4i $$

  • Step 2: Express $z$ in standard form $x + iy$ by rationalizing the denominator:

$$ z = \frac{1 + 7i}{3 - 4i} $$

Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, $(3 + 4i)$:

$$ z = \frac{(1 + 7i)(3 + 4i)}{(3 - 4i)(3 + 4i)} $$

Numerator expansion:

$$ (1 + 7i)(3 + 4i) = 1(3) + 1(4i) + 7i(3) + 7i(4i) = 3 + 4i + 21i + 28i^2 $$

Since $i^2 = -1$:

$$ \text{Numerator} = 3 + 25i + 28(-1) = 3 - 28 + 25i = -25 + 25i $$

Denominator expansion:

$$ (3 - 4i)(3 + 4i) = 3^2 - (4i)^2 = 9 - 16(-1) = 9 + 16 = 25 $$

Therefore:

$$ z = \frac{-25 + 25i}{25} = \frac{-25}{25} + i\frac{25}{25} = -1 + i $$

  • Step 3: Calculate Modulus $r$:

$$ r = |z| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt{2} $$

  • Step 4: Calculate Argument $\theta$:

$$ \tan\alpha = \left|\frac{y}{x}\right| = \left|\frac{1}{-1}\right| = 1 \implies \alpha = \frac{\pi}{4} $$

Here $x = -1 < 0$ and $y = 1 > 0$, so $z$ lies in Quadrant II.

$$ \theta = \pi - \alpha = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4} $$

  • Step 5: Final Polar Representation:

$$ z = \sqrt{2}\left(\cos\frac{3\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{3\pi}{4}\right) $$

Board Exam Scoring Breakdown:

  • Expanding denominator to $3 - 4i$: [0.5 Mark]
  • Multiplying by conjugate and simplifying to $-1 + i$: [1.0 Mark]
  • Correct modulus $r = \sqrt{2}$: [0.5 Mark]
  • Reference angle $\alpha$ and quadrant deduction for $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{4}$: [1.0 Mark]
  • Correct final polar representation: [1.0 Mark]
  • Total: 4 Marks
💡 Example 3: NCERT Exemplar Advanced Problem ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: If $|z_1| = |z_2| = \dots = |z_n| = 1$, prove that:

$$ \left|\frac{1}{z_1} + \frac{1}{z_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{z_n}\right| = |z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_n| $$

Step-by-Step Proof:

  • Step 1: Relate the modulus and conjugate of unit complex numbers:
    Given that for each $k \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$:

$$ |z_k| = 1 \implies |z_k|^2 = 1 $$

From Theorem 1, we know $z_k \bar{z}_k = |z_k|^2$. Therefore:

$$ z_k \bar{z}_k = 1 \implies \frac{1}{z_k} = \bar{z}_k $$

  • Step 2: Substitute into the given expression:

$$ \frac{1}{z_1} + \frac{1}{z_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{z_n} = \bar{z}_1 + \bar{z}_2 + \dots + \bar{z}_n $$

  • Step 3: Apply Conjugate Properties:
    The conjugate of a sum is the sum of the conjugates:

$$ \bar{z}_1 + \bar{z}_2 + \dots + \bar{z}_n = \overline{z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_n} $$

  • Step 4: Take the modulus of both sides:

$$ \left|\frac{1}{z_1} + \frac{1}{z_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{z_n}\right| = \left|\overline{z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_n}\right| $$

  • Step 5: Apply Modulus of Conjugate Property ($|\bar{w}| = |w|$):
    Let $w = z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_n$. Then:

$$ |\bar{w}| = |w| \implies \left|\overline{z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_n}\right| = |z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_n| $$

  • Conclusion:

$$ \left|\frac{1}{z_1} + \frac{1}{z_2} + \dots + \frac{1}{z_n}\right| = |z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_n| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

💡 Example 4: Locus Problem in the Argand Plane ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Find the locus of the point $z$ in the Argand plane if $\left|\frac{z - 3i}{z + 3i}\right| = 1$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  • Method 1: Geometric Approach
    The condition can be rewritten as:

$$ \frac{|z - 3i|}{|z + 3i|} = 1 \implies |z - 3i| = |z - (-3i)| $$

$|z - z_0|$ represents the Euclidean distance from $z$ to $z_0$.
Let $A$ be the point representing $3i$, which is $(0, 3)$.
Let $B$ be the point representing $-3i$, which is $(0, -3)$.
The condition states:

$$ \text{Distance}(P, A) = \text{Distance}(P, B) $$

The locus of a point equidistant from two fixed points $A$ and $B$ is the perpendicular bisector of the segment $AB$.
$A(0, 3)$ and $B(0, -3)$ lie on the imaginary axis; their midpoint is $(0, 0)$.
The perpendicular bisector of the segment along the vertical axis is the horizontal axis (the Real Axis).
Hence, the locus is the Real Axis ($y = 0$, or $\text{Im}(z) = 0$).

  • Method 2: Algebraic Verification
    Let $z = x + iy$ where $x, y \in \mathbb{R}$.

$$ z - 3i = x + i(y - 3) \implies |z - 3i|^2 = x^2 + (y - 3)^2 $$

$$ z + 3i = x + i(y + 3) \implies |z + 3i|^2 = x^2 + (y + 3)^2 $$

Given $|z - 3i| = |z + 3i| \implies |z - 3i|^2 = |z + 3i|^2$:

$$ x^2 + (y - 3)^2 = x^2 + (y + 3)^2 $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 - 6y + 9 = x^2 + y^2 + 6y + 9 $$

Subtracting $x^2 + y^2 + 9$ from both sides:

$$ -6y = 6y \implies 12y = 0 \implies y = 0 $$

Since $y = \text{Im}(z) = 0$, the locus is the entire Real Axis.

4. EXAMINER TRAPS & FREQUENT STUDENT PITFALLS

Pitfall 1: Blind Usage of $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)$

  • The Error: A student calculating $\text{Arg}(-1 - i\sqrt{3})$ writes:

$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{-\sqrt{3}}{-1}\right) = \tan^{-1}(\sqrt{3}) = \frac{\pi}{3} \quad \text{[FATAL ERROR]} $$

  • Why it is wrong: The angle $\frac{\pi}{3}$ lies in the First Quadrant, whereas the point $(-1, -\sqrt{3})$ lies in the Third Quadrant. The standard inverse tangent function $\tan^{-1}(u)$ has a principal range of $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, so it completely fails to distinguish Quadrant I from Quadrant III, and Quadrant II from Quadrant IV.
  • The Correct Protocol: Always calculate the reference angle $\alpha = \tan^{-1}\left|\frac{y}{x}\right| = \frac{\pi}{3}$. Then, check the signs: $x < 0, y < 0 \implies$ Quadrant III $\implies \theta = -(\pi - \alpha) = -\left(\pi - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{2\pi}{3}$.

Pitfall 2: Confusing the Non-Standard Polar Format

  • The Trap: An exam question presents:

$$ z = 2\left(\sin\frac{\pi}{6} + i\cos\frac{\pi}{6}\right) $$

Students often rush and state $r = 2, \theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$. This receives zero marks. * Why it is wrong: The standard polar form is strictly:

$$ z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) $$

Here, the cosine and sine positions are interchanged. * The Correct Resolution: Use co-function identities $\sin\phi = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \phi\right)$ and $\cos\phi = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \phi\right)$:

$$ \sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \cos\frac{\pi}{3} $$

$$ \cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \sin\frac{\pi}{3} $$

Thus, $z = 2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{3} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{3}\right)$, and the true argument is $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3}$.

Pitfall 3: Negative Modulus Fallacy

  • The Trap: A question gives $z = -3\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$. Students write $r = -3, \theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
  • Why it is wrong: By definition, the modulus $r = |z|$ is a distance, so $r \ge 0$ always. $r$ can never be negative.
  • The Correct Resolution: Absorb the negative sign inside the trigonometric functions using $-1 = \cos(\pm\pi) + i\sin(\pm\pi)$ or $- \cos\theta = \cos(\pi - \theta)$:

$$ -3\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = 3\left(-\cos\frac{\pi}{4} - i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right) $$

Here, both real and imaginary parts are negative (Quadrant III):

$$ -\cos\frac{\pi}{4} = \cos\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right), \quad -\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = \sin\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right) $$

$$ z = 3\left(\cos\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right) + i\sin\left(-\frac{3\pi}{4}\right)\right) \implies r = 3, \theta = -\frac{3\pi}{4} $$

Pitfall 4: Boundary Value Inclusion at $-\pi$

  • The Trap: Writing $\text{Arg}(-5) = -\pi$.
  • Why it is wrong: The standard principal interval defined by NCERT is:

$$ -\pi < \theta \le \pi $$

The value $-\pi$ is open (excluded), while $\pi$ is closed (included). * The Correct Value: For any negative real number $z = -a$ ($a > 0$):

$$ \text{Arg}(z) = \pi $$

💡 Example 4 (Advanced Exemplar): Equation with Purely Imaginary Coefficients ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem: Solve for $x$:

$$ x^2 - 2\iota x + 3 = 0 $$

Crucial Classroom Note: The coefficients here are not all real ($b = -2\iota \notin \mathbb{R}$). Therefore, the Conjugate Root Theorem does not apply. The roots will not necessarily be conjugates of each other.

Detailed Solution

  1. Identify Coefficients:
    $a = 1, \; b = -2\iota, \; c = 3$.
  2. Compute Discriminant:

$$ D = b^2 - 4ac = (-2\iota)^2 - 4(1)(3) = 4\iota^2 - 12 = 4(-1) - 12 = -16 $$

  1. Square Root of Discriminant:

$$ \sqrt{D} = \sqrt{-16} = \sqrt{16 \cdot (-1)} = 4\iota $$

  1. Apply Quadratic Formula:

$$ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{D}}{2a} = \frac{-(-2\iota) \pm 4\iota}{2(1)} = \frac{2\iota \pm 4\iota}{2} = \iota \pm 2\iota $$

  1. Separate Roots:

$$ x_1 = \iota + 2\iota = 3\iota $$

$$ x_2 = \iota - 2\iota = -\iota $$

Verification via Factorization

$$ (x - 3\iota)(x - (-\iota)) = (x - 3\iota)(x + \iota) = x^2 + \iota x - 3\iota x - 3\iota^2 = x^2 - 2\iota x - 3(-1) = x^2 - 2\iota x + 3 = 0 $$

(Verified correct. Notice that $3\iota$ and $-\iota$ are not conjugates).

💡 Example 5 (Challenging Mixed Structure): Evaluating Symmetric Forms ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: If $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the complex roots of the equation $x^2 - 2x + 5 = 0$, evaluate: 1. $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$ 2. $\dfrac{\alpha}{\beta} + \dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}$ 3. $\alpha^3 + \beta^3$

Detailed Solution

  1. Find Roots Directly:

$$ a = 1, \; b = -2, \; c = 5 $$

$$ D = (-2)^2 - 4(1)(5) = 4 - 20 = -16 $$

$$ x = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \frac{2 \pm 4\iota}{2} = 1 \pm 2\iota $$

Let $\alpha = 1 + 2\iota$ and $\beta = 1 - 2\iota$.

  1. Using Vieta's Formulas (Fastest Method):

$$ \alpha + \beta = -\frac{b}{a} = 2, \quad \alpha\beta = \frac{c}{a} = 5 $$

  1. Evaluation (1): $\alpha^2 + \beta^2$

$$ \alpha^2 + \beta^2 = (\alpha + \beta)^2 - 2\alpha\beta = (2)^2 - 2(5) = 4 - 10 = -6 $$

  1. Evaluation (2): $\dfrac{\alpha}{\beta} + \dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}$

$$ \frac{\alpha}{\beta} + \frac{\beta}{\alpha} = \frac{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}{\alpha\beta} = \frac{-6}{5} $$

  1. Evaluation (3): $\alpha^3 + \beta^3$

$$ \alpha^3 + \beta^3 = (\alpha + \beta)^3 - 3\alpha\beta(\alpha + \beta) = (2)^3 - 3(5)(2) = 8 - 30 = -22 $$

4. Examiner Traps, Common Student Pitfalls & Diagnostic Analysis

Trap 1: The Invalid Fallacy of Radical Multiplication $\sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$

  • The Error: A student computes $\sqrt{-4} \cdot \sqrt{-9}$ as follows:

$$ \sqrt{-4} \cdot \sqrt{-9} = \sqrt{(-4) \cdot (-9)} = \sqrt{36} = 6 \quad [INCORRECT] $$

  • The Mathematical Reality:
    The identity $\sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ holds if and only if at least one of $a$ or $b$ is non-negative ($a \ge 0$ or $b \ge 0$).
    When both $a < 0$ and $b < 0$:

$$ \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = (\iota \sqrt{|a|})(\iota \sqrt{|b|}) = \iota^2 \sqrt{|a||b|} = -\sqrt{|a||b|} $$

  • Correct Working:

$$ \sqrt{-4} \cdot \sqrt{-9} = (2\iota)(3\iota) = 6\iota^2 = -6 \quad [CORRECT] $$

Trap 2: Double Negative Confusion under the Radical Sign

  • The Error: When applying $x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$ with $D < 0$: Students frequently write:

$$ \sqrt{-16} = -\sqrt{16} = -4 \quad [FATAL ERROR] $$

or:

$$ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{-(4ac - b^2)}}{2a} = \frac{-b - \sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} $$

  • Examiner Insight: $\sqrt{-k}$ is undefined in $\mathbb{R}$. It must explicitly be factored as:

$$ \sqrt{-k} = \sqrt{-1 \cdot k} = \sqrt{-1} \cdot \sqrt{k} = \iota\sqrt{k} \quad (\text{for } k > 0) $$

Trap 3: Blind Assumption of the Conjugate Pair Theorem

  • The Error: A student is given:

$$ x^2 - (2 + \iota)x + (1 + \iota) = 0 $$

They find one root is $1$, and immediately claim the other root is $\overline{1} = 1$ or that if one root is $\iota$, the other must be $-\iota$. * Examiner Insight: Roots are complex conjugates only when all coefficients $a, b, c$ are real. If any coefficient has a non-zero imaginary component, solve using the general quadratic formula or factorization.

Trap 4: Loss of $\pm$ in Intermediate Square Root Steps

  • The Error: Writing:

$$ (x - 2)^2 = -9 \implies x - 2 = 3\iota \implies x = 2 + 3\iota $$

(Losing the second root $2 - 3\iota$). * Examiner Insight: A degree-2 polynomial equation must have two roots. Writing only one root results in an immediate loss of 50% of the allocated marks.

5. Speed Hacks, Mental Shortcuts & Golden Takeaways

Hack 1: The Monic Parity Mental Root Calculator

For any standard monic quadratic equation ($a = 1$):

$$ x^2 + 2px + q = 0 \quad (\text{where } p^2 < q) $$

  1. Real Part: Simply the negated half of the linear coefficient:

$$ \operatorname{Re}(x) = -p $$

  1. Imaginary Part: The square root of the difference $(q - p^2)$:

$$ \operatorname{Im}(x) = \pm \sqrt{q - p^2} $$

  1. Direct Root Assembly:

$$ x = -p \pm \iota \sqrt{q - p^2} $$

  • Mental Drill: Solve $x^2 + 6x + 25 = 0$:
  • Half of $+6$ is $3$; negated $\implies -3$.
  • $25 - 3^2 = 25 - 9 = 16$.
  • $\sqrt{16} = 4$.
  • Roots: $x = -3 \pm 4\iota$ (Solved in under 3 seconds!)

Hack 2: Sum of Squares Factorization Shortcut

In $\mathbb{R}$, the sum of two squares cannot be factored: $A^2 + B^2 \neq (A+B)(A-B)$.
In $\mathbb{C}$, using $\iota^2 = -1 \implies +B^2 = -(\iota^2 B^2) = -(\iota B)^2$:

$$ A^2 + B^2 = (A + \iota B)(A - \iota B) $$

Rapid Factorization Applications

  • $x^2 + 9 = x^2 - (3\iota)^2 = (x + 3\iota)(x - 3\iota) = 0 \implies x = \pm 3\iota$
  • $x^2 + 7 = x^2 - (\iota\sqrt{7})^2 = (x + \iota\sqrt{7})(x - \iota\sqrt{7}) = 0 \implies x = \pm \iota\sqrt{7}$
  • $4x^2 + 25 = (2x + 5\iota)(2x - 5\iota) = 0 \implies x = \pm \frac{5}{2}\iota$

Hack 3: Modulus of Complex Roots of Real Quadratics

For any quadratic equation $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ with $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $D < 0$: Since the roots $\alpha, \beta$ are complex conjugates ($\beta = \overline{\alpha}$):

$$ |\alpha|^2 = \alpha \cdot \overline{\alpha} = \alpha \beta = \frac{c}{a} $$

$$ \implies |\alpha| = |\beta| = \sqrt{\frac{c}{a}} $$

  • Application: Without finding the roots of $3x^2 - 4x + 12 = 0$, what is the distance of its roots from the origin in the Argand plane?

$$ |z| = \sqrt{\frac{c}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{12}{3}} = \sqrt{4} = 2 \text{ units} $$

Question A.1: Case-Based / Passage-Integrated Question (Alternating Current & Impedance Analysis)

Tags: [CBSE Competency Pattern, 4 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Passage: In electrical engineering, alternating current ($AC$) circuits containing resistors, inductors, and capacitors are analyzed using complex numbers (traditionally denoted using $j = \sqrt{-1}$ in engineering, or $i$ in pure mathematics). The total opposition to the flow of alternating current is called Impedance ($Z$), represented as a complex number:

$$ Z = R + iX $$

where $R \ge 0$ is the electrical resistance (real part) and $X$ is the reactance (imaginary part). When two circuit elements with impedances $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ are connected in series, the total equivalent impedance is:

$$ Z_{\text{series}} = Z_1 + Z_2 $$

When connected in parallel, the total equivalent impedance $Z_{\text{parallel}}$ satisfies:

$$ \frac{1}{Z_{\text{parallel}}} = \frac{1}{Z_1} + \frac{1}{Z_2} \implies Z_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{Z_1 Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2} $$

The magnitude $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + X^2}$ represents the absolute impedance in ohms ($\Omega$), and the phase angle $\theta = \arg(Z)$ determines the phase shift between voltage and current.

An electrical testing laboratory tests two circuit branches having impedances:

$$ Z_1 = 3 + 4i \quad \text{and} \quad Z_2 = 1 - 2i \quad (\text{in ohms } \Omega) $$

Based on the above information, answer the following sub-questions: 1. Find the modulus and conjugate of the impedance $Z_1$. (1 Mark) 2. If the two branches are connected in series, find the magnitude of the equivalent impedance $|Z_{\text{series}}|$. (1 Mark) 3. If the branches are connected in parallel, determine the equivalent impedance $Z_{\text{parallel}}$ in the standard form $A + iB$. Hence, state whether the circuit behaves with inductive reactance ($B > 0$) or capacitive reactance ($B < 0$). (2 Marks)

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Marking Rubric

Part 1: - Given $Z_1 = 3 + 4i$. - Modulus:

$$ |Z_1| = \sqrt{\text{Re}(Z_1)^2 + \text{Im}(Z_1)^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5\ \Omega $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Conjugate:

$$ \overline{Z_1} = 3 - 4i\ \Omega $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark]

Part 2: - For series connection:

$$ Z_{\text{series}} = Z_1 + Z_2 = (3 + 4i) + (1 - 2i) = (3 + 1) + i(4 - 2) = 4 + 2i $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Magnitude:

$$ |Z_{\text{series}}| = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{16 + 4} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}\ \Omega \approx 4.47\ \Omega $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark]

Part 3: - For parallel connection:

$$ Z_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{Z_1 Z_2}{Z_1 + Z_2} = \frac{(3 + 4i)(1 - 2i)}{(3 + 4i) + (1 - 2i)} $$

  • Expanding numerator:

$$ (3 + 4i)(1 - 2i) = 3(1) - 6i + 4i - 8i^2 = 3 - 2i - 8(-1) = 11 - 2i $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Denominator:

$$ Z_1 + Z_2 = 4 + 2i $$

  • Rationalising the denominator by multiplying numerator and denominator by the complex conjugate $(4 - 2i)$:

$$ Z_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{11 - 2i}{4 + 2i} \times \frac{4 - 2i}{4 - 2i} = \frac{(11)(4) - 22i - 8i + 4i^2}{4^2 - (2i)^2} $$

$$ = \frac{44 - 30i + 4(-1)}{16 - 4(-1)} = \frac{40 - 30i}{20} $$

$$ = \frac{40}{20} - i\frac{30}{20} = 2 - \frac{3}{2}i\ \Omega $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $1$ Mark] - Reactance identification: Comparing with $A + iB$, here $B = -\frac{3}{2} < 0$. Therefore, the circuit behaves with capacitive reactance ($B < 0$).

Item 1: Property of Radicals over Complex Numbers

  • Reason (R): For any two negative real numbers $a = -x$ and $b = -y$ (where $x, y > 0$), $\sqrt{-x}\sqrt{-y} = (i\sqrt{x})(i\sqrt{y}) = i^2\sqrt{xy} = -\sqrt{xy} \neq \sqrt{(-x)(-y)}$.

Item 2: Conjugate Roots of Quadratic Equations

Tags: [CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

  • Reason (R): Complex non-real roots of a polynomial equation with real coefficients always occur in conjugate pairs.

Model Solution & CBSE Marking Rubric

  • Evaluation of Assertion (A): The coefficients $1, p, q$ are real. By the Conjugate Root Theorem, non-real complex roots must occur in conjugate pairs. Thus, if $2 + i\sqrt{3}$ is a root, $2 - i\sqrt{3}$ is definitely the other root. Hence, (A) is True.
    [CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark]

Item 3: Order Relations in Complex Numbers

Tags: [CBSE Competency Pattern, 1 Mark] | ⭐⭐⭐ [Core]

  • Assertion (A): The inequality $3 + 4i > 2 + 3i$ is a meaningful and true mathematical statement.
  • Reason (R): The set of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ cannot be ordered; inequality relations ($<, \le, >, \ge$) are undefined between non-real complex numbers.

Model Solution & CBSE Marking Rubric

  • Evaluation of Assertion (A): Order relations ($>, <$) are not defined on the field of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ because $\mathbb{C}$ cannot be made into an ordered field compatible with field operations ($i^2 = -1 < 0$, which contradicts $x^2 \ge 0$ for any element in an ordered field).     [CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark]
  • Evaluation of Reason (R): The set of complex numbers has no order structure compatible with its ring/field properties. Hence, inequalities are undefined for numbers with non-zero imaginary parts. Thus, (R) is True.

Problem A.3.1: Locus of a Complex Variable

Tags: [CBSE HOTS, 4 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Statement: Let $z = x + iy$ be a non-zero complex number such that:

$$ \left| \frac{z - 3i}{z + 3i} \right| = 1 $$

  1. Determine algebraically the locus of $z$ in the Argand plane.
  2. If the condition is modified to $\left| \frac{z - 3i}{z + 3i} \right| = 2$, prove that the locus represents a circle. Find its center and radius.
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Marking Rubric

Part 1: - Given:

$$ \left| \frac{z - 3i}{z + 3i} \right| = 1 \implies \frac{|z - 3i|}{|z + 3i|} = 1 \implies |z - 3i| = |z + 3i| $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Geometrically, this represents points equidistant from $(0, 3)$ and $(0, -3)$. - Algebraically, let $z = x + iy$:

$$ |x + i(y - 3)| = |x + i(y + 3)| $$

$$ \sqrt{x^2 + (y - 3)^2} = \sqrt{x^2 + (y + 3)^2} $$

  • Squaring both sides:

$$ x^2 + y^2 - 6y + 9 = x^2 + y^2 + 6y + 9 $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $1$ Mark] - Simplifying:

$$ -6y = 6y \implies 12y = 0 \implies y = 0 $$

  • Conclusion: The locus of $z$ is the line $y = 0$, which is the entire Real Axis (X-axis) in the Argand plane.
    [CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark]

Part 2: - Given:

$$ \left| \frac{z - 3i}{z + 3i} \right| = 2 \implies |z - 3i| = 2|z + 3i| $$

  • Squaring both sides:

$$ |z - 3i|^2 = 4|z + 3i|^2 $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Substitute $z = x + iy$:

$$ x^2 + (y - 3)^2 = 4\left[ x^2 + (y + 3)^2 \right] $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 - 6y + 9 = 4(x^2 + y^2 + 6y + 9) $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 - 6y + 9 = 4x^2 + 4y^2 + 24y + 36 $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Grouping terms:

$$ 3x^2 + 3y^2 + 30y + 27 = 0 $$

  • Dividing throughout by $3$:

$$ x^2 + y^2 + 10y + 9 = 0 $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Completing the square:

$$ x^2 + (y + 5)^2 - 25 + 9 = 0 \implies x^2 + (y + 5)^2 = 16 = 4^2 $$

  • This is the standard equation of a circle:

$$ (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2 $$

  • Center: $(0, -5)$ or $-5i$ on the imaginary axis.
  • Radius: $r = \sqrt{16} = 4$.
    [CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark]

Problem A.3.2: Minimum Value via Triangle Inequality

Tags: [CBSE HOTS, 3 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Statement: If $|z - 2 + i| \le 2$, find the maximum and minimum values of $|z|$.

Model Solution & CBSE Marking Rubric

  • Recall the reverse triangle inequalities for complex numbers:

$$ ||z_1| - |z_2|| \le |z_1 + z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2| $$

  • We write $z$ by adding and subtracting $(2 - i)$:

$$ z = (z - 2 + i) + (2 - i) $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $1$ Mark] - Let $u = z - 2 + i$ and $v = 2 - i$. - Modulus of $v$:

$$ |v| = |2 - i| = \sqrt{2^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 1} = \sqrt{5} $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - For maximum value:

$$ |z| = |u + v| \le |u| + |v| \le 2 + \sqrt{5} $$

Thus, $\max(|z|) = \sqrt{5} + 2$.
[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - For minimum value:

$$ |z| = |v - (-u)| \ge ||v| - |u|| $$

Since $|v| = \sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$ and $|u| \le 2$, we have $|v| > |u|$. Therefore:

$$ |z| \ge |v| - |u| \ge \sqrt{5} - 2 $$

Thus, $\min(|z|) = \sqrt{5} - 2$.
[CBSE Step Marking: $1$ Mark]

Statement: If $(a + ib) = \frac{(x + i)^2}{2x^2 + 1}$, prove that:

$$ a^2 + b^2 = \frac{(x^2 + 1)^2}{(2x^2 + 1)^2} $$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Marking Rubric

  • Given equation:

$$ a + ib = \frac{(x + i)^2}{2x^2 + 1} \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

  • Method: Using Complex Conjugates and Modulus Properties Taking the complex conjugate on both sides of equation (1):

$$ \overline{a + ib} = \overline{\left( \frac{(x + i)^2}{2x^2 + 1} \right)} $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $1$ Mark] - Since $x$ is real, $2x^2 + 1$ is real, so $\overline{2x^2 + 1} = 2x^2 + 1$. Using $\overline{(w^2)} = (\overline{w})^2$:

$$ a - ib = \frac{(\overline{x + i})^2}{2x^2 + 1} = \frac{(x - i)^2}{2x^2 + 1} \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $1$ Mark] - Multiplying equation (1) and equation (2):

$$ (a + ib)(a - ib) = \left( \frac{(x + i)^2}{2x^2 + 1} \right) \left( \frac{(x - i)^2}{2x^2 + 1} \right) $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $1$ Mark] - On the Left Hand Side:

$$ (a + ib)(a - ib) = a^2 - (ib)^2 = a^2 - i^2 b^2 = a^2 + b^2 $$

  • On the Right Hand Side:

$$ \frac{[(x + i)(x - i)]^2}{(2x^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{[x^2 - i^2]^2}{(2x^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{(x^2 + 1)^2}{(2x^2 + 1)^2} $$

  • Combining both sides:

$$ a^2 + b^2 = \frac{(x^2 + 1)^2}{(2x^2 + 1)^2} $$

Hence Proved.

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Step 3: Equating real and imaginary parts:

$$ Z_1 = \overline{W} \implies (3x + 5y) + i(5x - 3y) = -6 + 24i $$

$$ 3x + 5y = -6 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Equating imaginary parts:

$$ 5x - 3y = 24 \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Step 4: Solve the linear system: Multiply (1) by 3 and (2) by 5:

$$ 9x + 15y = -18 $$

$$ 25x - 15y = 120 $$

Adding the two equations:

$$ = x^3 + 3ix^2y - 3xy^2 - iy^3 $$

Question C.1: Complex Reciprocal and Unit Modulus

Tags: [NCERT Exemplar, 4 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Statement: If $|z| = 1$ and $z \neq -1$, show that the complex number $w = \frac{z - 1}{z + 1}$ is purely imaginary. What can you say if $z = 1$?

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Marking Rubric

  • Method 1: Algebraic definition ($w + \overline{w} = 0$) A complex number $w$ is purely imaginary if and only if $\text{Re}(w) = 0 \iff w + \overline{w} = 0$.
    [CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark]
  • Given $w = \frac{z - 1}{z + 1}$. Compute the conjugate $\overline{w}$:

$$ \overline{w} = \overline{\left( \frac{z - 1}{z + 1} \right)} = \frac{\overline{z - 1}}{\overline{z + 1}} = \frac{\overline{z} - 1}{\overline{z} + 1} $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $1$ Mark] - Since $|z| = 1 \implies |z|^2 = z\overline{z} = 1 \implies \overline{z} = \frac{1}{z}$. Substitute $\overline{z} = \frac{1}{z}$ into the expression for $\overline{w}$:

$$ \overline{w} = \frac{\frac{1}{z} - 1}{\frac{1}{z} + 1} = \frac{\frac{1 - z}{z}}{\frac{1 + z}{z}} = \frac{1 - z}{1 + z} = -\left( \frac{z - 1}{z + 1} \right) = -w $$

[CBSE Step Marking: $1\frac{1}{2}$ Marks] - Since $\overline{w} = -w$, we have:

$$ w + \overline{w} = 0 \implies 2\text{Re}(w) = 0 \implies \text{Re}(w) = 0 $$

Hence, $w$ is purely imaginary.
[CBSE Step Marking: $\frac{1}{2}$ Mark] - Analysis for $z = 1$: If $z = 1$, then $|z| = 1$ holds.

$$ w = \frac{1 - 1}{1 + 1} = \frac{0}{2} = 0 $$

The number $0$ can be written as $0 + 0i$. It has real part zero ($\text{Re}(0) = 0$), so it is technically purely imaginary (as well as purely real).

Therefore:

$$ \sqrt{D} = \pm(4 - \sqrt{2}i) $$

Senior Examiner's Guide to Speed Hacks, Traps, Board Pitfalls & High-Yield Truths

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 Fast Evaluation of Higher Powers of $i$

Never divide by long division on paper. Use last two digits mental arithmetic modulo 4: - $i^{4k} = 1$ - $i^{4k+1} = i$ - $i^{4k+2} = -1$ - $i^{4k+3} = -i$ - Consecutive 4-term zero rule: Any 4 consecutive powers of $i$ sum to zero:

$$ \sum_{r=k}^{k+3} i^r = i^k(1 + i + i^2 + i^3) = i^k(1 + i - 1 - i) = 0 \quad (\forall k \in \mathbb{Z}) $$

  • Quick Evaluation of Negative Exponents: $i^{-n} = i^{(-n \pmod 4)}$.
    Example: $i^{-35} = i^{-35 + 36} = i^1 = i$.

1.2 The "Unimodal" Binomial Shortcuts $(1 \pm i)$

Powers of $(1 \pm i)$ frequently appear in CBSE and entrance formats: - $(1 + i)^2 = 1 + 2i - 1 = 2i$ - $(1 - i)^2 = 1 - 2i - 1 = -2i$ - Hence:

$$ \left(\frac{1+i}{1-i}\right) = \frac{(1+i)^2}{1 - i^2} = \frac{2i}{2} = i \implies \left(\frac{1+i}{1-i}\right)^n = i^n $$

$$ \left(\frac{1-i}{1+i}\right) = -i \implies \left(\frac{1-i}{1+i}\right)^n = (-i)^n $$

  • For large powers:

$$ (1+i)^{4n} = \left[(1+i)^2\right]^{2n} = (2i)^{2n} = (-4)^n $$

1.3 Direct Square Root Formula of a Complex Number

Instead of setting $(x + iy)^2 = a + ib$ and solving simultaneous quadratic equations (which takes 3–4 minutes and invites algebraic errors), use the direct formula:

$$ \sqrt{a + ib} = \pm \left[ \sqrt{\frac{|z| + a}{2}} + i \cdot \operatorname{sgn}(b) \sqrt{\frac{|z| - a}{2}} \right] $$

where $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and $\operatorname{sgn}(b) = 1$ if $b > 0$, $-1$ if $b < 0$.

Board Exam Tip: In subjective board steps, state: "Let $\sqrt{a+ib} = x + iy$." Equate real and imaginary parts ($x^2 - y^2 = a$, $2xy = b$), use the identity $(x^2 + y^2)^2 = (x^2 - y^2)^2 + (2xy)^2 = |z|^2 \implies x^2 + y^2 = |z|$, and then jump straight to the values of $x^2$ and $y^2$. This saves 6 algebraic steps while retaining 100% step marks.

1.4 Modulus Distribution Shortcut for Products and Quotients

When asked to find the modulus of a bulky fraction, never rationalize first:

$$ z = \frac{(a_1 + ib_1)(a_2 + ib_2)\cdots}{(c_1 + id_1)(c_2 + id_2)\cdots} \implies |z| = \frac{|a_1 + ib_1||a_2 + ib_2|\cdots}{|c_1 + id_1||c_2 + id_2|\cdots} $$

Example: If $z = \frac{(1+3i)(2-i)}{(3+4i)}$, then:

$$ |z| = \frac{\sqrt{1^2+3^2}\sqrt{2^2+(-1)^2}}{\sqrt{3^2+4^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{10}\sqrt{5}}{5} = \frac{\sqrt{50}}{5} = \sqrt{2} $$

1.5 Rotation and Geometry Shortcuts

  • Multiplication by $i$: Rotates the complex vector counter-clockwise by $90^\circ$ ($\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians) without changing magnitude.
  • Multiplication by $-i$: Rotates clockwise by $90^\circ$.
  • Distance between points: $|z_1 - z_2|$ is the Euclidean distance between $P(z_1)$ and $Q(z_2)$.
  • Perpendicular Bisector: $|z - z_1| = |z - z_2|$ represents the perpendicular bisector of the line segment joining $z_1$ and $z_2$.
  • Circle: $|z - z_0| = r$ represents a circle with center $z_0$ and radius $r$.

1.6 Quadratic Equations with Real Coefficients & Imaginary Roots

For $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$ ($a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $D = b^2 - 4ac < 0$): - Roots are strictly complex conjugates:

$$ x = \frac{-b \pm i\sqrt{4ac - b^2}}{2a} = \frac{-b \pm i\sqrt{|D|}}{2a} $$

  • Symmetric Functions of Roots: Use Newton's Sums for fast evaluation of higher powers: If $S_n = \alpha^n + \beta^n$, then for $ax^2 + bx + c = 0$:

$$ a S_n + b S_{n-1} + c S_{n-2} = 0 $$

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

2.1 The Fatal Fallacy: $\sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$

  • Trap: Writing $\sqrt{-16} \cdot \sqrt{-25} = \sqrt{(-16)(-25)} = \sqrt{400} = 20$.
  • Correction: The rule $\sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$ holds if and only if at least one of $a$ or $b$ is non-negative.
  • Correct Step:

$$ \sqrt{-16} \cdot \sqrt{-25} = (4i) \cdot (5i) = 20i^2 = -20 $$

Examiner Penalty: Complete deduction (0/1 or 0/2 marks) for that specific step.

2.2 Quadrant Errors in Principal Argument ($\operatorname{Arg}(z)$)

CBSE defines the principal argument $\theta = \operatorname{Arg}(z)$ strictly in the open-closed interval:

$$ -\pi < \theta \le \pi $$

Many students calculate $\alpha = \tan^{-1}\left|\frac{y}{x}\right|$ and blindly write $\theta = \alpha$. - Examiner Grading Grid for $\theta$: - Quadrant I ($x > 0, y > 0$): $\theta = \alpha$ - Quadrant II ($x < 0, y > 0$): $\theta = \pi - \alpha$ - Quadrant III ($x < 0, y < 0$): $\theta = -(\pi - \alpha) = \alpha - \pi$ (NOT $\pi + \alpha$ or $\frac{5\pi}{4}$, which violates the range) - Quadrant IV ($x > 0, y < 0$): $\theta = -\alpha$ - Boundary Cases: - Positive Real Axis ($x > 0, y = 0$): $\theta = 0$ - Negative Real Axis ($x < 0, y = 0$): $\theta = \pi$ (Note: $\theta = -\pi$ is NOT accepted since $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$) - Positive Imaginary Axis ($x = 0, y > 0$): $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ - Negative Imaginary Axis ($x = 0, y < 0$): $\theta = -\frac{\pi}{2}$

2.3 The "Missing $\pm$" in Square Roots

When finding $\sqrt{z}$, writing only the positive branch results in an automatic 1-mark deduction. A complex number has two square roots additive inverses of each other:

$$ z^{1/2} = \pm (x + iy) $$

2.4 Confusing "Purely Imaginary" and "Purely Real"

  • If a question states: "Show that $\frac{z-1}{z+1}$ is purely imaginary", students often forget the condition $\operatorname{Re}(w) = 0$ and instead solve $w = -w$, which can lead to circular logic.
  • Examiner standard:
  • Purely real $\iff \operatorname{Im}(z) = 0 \iff z = \bar{z}$
  • Purely imaginary $\iff \operatorname{Re}(z) = 0 \iff z = -\bar{z}$ (with $z \ne 0$ if non-zero is specified).

2.5 Extraneous Roots in Modulus Equations

When solving equations containing $|z|$, e.g., $z^2 + |z| = 0$: - Students substitute $z = x + iy$ to get $(x+iy)^2 + \sqrt{x^2+y^2} = 0$, equate imaginary parts $2xy = 0$, giving $x=0$ or $y=0$. - Trap: Forgetting to back-substitute and check if extraneous roots were introduced by algebraic manipulation or sign conflicts (e.g., $x^2 \ge 0$ while $\sqrt{x^2+y^2} \ge 0$).

2.6 Board Step-Marking Scheme Breakdown (4-Mark / 5-Mark Question)

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

  1. Inequality of Complex Numbers Does Not Exist:
  2. The field of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ is not an ordered field.
  3. Statements like $z_1 > z_2$ or $2 + 3i > 1 + 2i$ are mathematically meaningless.

  4. Inequalities are strictly valid only for their moduli: $|z_1| > |z_2|$ (since $|z| \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$).

  5. Triangle Inequality & Reverse Triangle Inequality:

  6. $||z_1| - |z_2|| \le |z_1 \pm z_2| \le |z_1| + |z_2|$
  7. Equality for $|z_1 + z_2| = |z_1| + |z_2|$ holds if and only if $z_1$ and $z_2$ are collinear with the origin and lie on the same ray from the origin ($\operatorname{Arg}(z_1) = \operatorname{Arg}(z_2)$).

  8. Equality for $|z_1 - z_2| = |z_1| + |z_2|$ holds if and only if $\operatorname{Arg}(z_1) - \operatorname{Arg}(z_2) = \pm \pi$.

  9. Conjugate Distribution Properties:

  10. $\overline{z_1 \pm z_2} = \bar{z_1} \pm \bar{z_2}$
  11. $\overline{z_1 z_2} = \bar{z_1} \cdot \bar{z_2}$
  12. $\overline{\left(\frac{z_1}{z_2}\right)} = \frac{\bar{z_1}}{\bar{z_2}}$ ($z_2 \ne 0$)
  13. $z \bar{z} = |z|^2 = |\bar{z}|^2$
  14. $z + \bar{z} = 2\operatorname{Re}(z)$

  15. $z - \bar{z} = 2i\operatorname{Im}(z)$

  16. Multiplicative Inverse:

$$ z^{-1} = \frac{\bar{z}}{|z|^2} = \frac{a - ib}{a^2 + b^2} $$

Always write out both real and imaginary components explicitly: $\left(\frac{a}{a^2+b^2}\right) + i\left(\frac{-b}{a^2+b^2}\right)$.

  1. Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (NCERT Core Statement):

    "A polynomial equation of degree $n$ has exactly $n$ roots in $\mathbb{C}$ (counting multiplicities)."

  2. If coefficients are real ($a_i \in \mathbb{R}$), complex roots occur in conjugate pairs ($p + iq$ and $p - iq$).
  3. If coefficients are complex, roots do not necessarily occur in conjugate pairs.

Argument of Product/Quotient $\operatorname{Arg}(z_1 z_2) = \operatorname{Arg}(z_1) + \operatorname{Arg}(z_2) + 2k\pi$ $k \in \{0, 1, -1\}$ chosen to keep angle in $(-\pi, \pi]$
Parallelogram Law $|z_1 + z_2|^2 + |z_1 - z_2|^2 = 2(|z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2)$ Sum of squares of diagonals = sum of squares of sides
Square Root of $z$ $\pm \left[\sqrt{\frac{\|z\|+x}{2}} + i \cdot \operatorname{sgn}(y)\sqrt{\frac{\|z\|-x}{2}}\right]$ If $y=0, x<0$, roots are $\pm i\sqrt{\|x\|}$
