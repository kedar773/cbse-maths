1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Definition and Sense of an Angle (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

In elementary geometry, an angle is defined statically as the union of two non-collinear rays sharing a common endpoint. In higher mathematics and trigonometry, an angle is considered dynamically as a measure of rotation.

  • Initial Side: The original ray before rotation begins.
  • Terminal Side: The ray in its final position after the rotation is completed.
  • Vertex: The fixed pivot point around which the ray rotates.

Sense of Rotation:

  1. Positive Angle: Generated when the initial ray rotates in an anticlockwise (counterclockwise) direction.
  2. Negative Angle: Generated when the initial ray rotates in a clockwise direction.
Positive Angle (Anticlockwise)        Negative Angle (Clockwise)
          Terminal Side                         Initial Side
             /                                     \
            /  +θ                                   \  -θ
           /____                                     \____
     Vertex    Initial Side                    Vertex    Terminal Side

Unlike Euclidean geometry, where angle measures are strictly restricted to the interval $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$, dynamic trigonometry allows angles of arbitrary real magnitude ($(-\infty, \infty)$) by considering multiple complete revolutions ($360^\circ \times n$) in either direction.

1.2 Systems of Angle Measurement (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

A. Sexagesimal System (English / Degree Measure)

The complete rotation of a ray from its initial position back to itself is partitioned into $360$ equal intervals called degrees ($^\circ$). * $1 \text{ complete revolution} = 360^\circ$ * $1 \text{ right angle} = 90^\circ$ * $1^\circ (\text{one degree}) = 60' (\text{sixty minutes of arc})$ * $1' (\text{one minute}) = 60'' (\text{sixty seconds of arc})$

B. Circular System (Radian Measure)

The radian is the standard international unit of angular measurement used across pure mathematics, calculus, and physics.

Definition (Radian): One radian (denoted as $1\text{ rad}$ or $1^c$) is the measure of the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc whose length is equal to the radius of that circle.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: Radian is a Constant Angle (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:

The magnitude of one radian is constant, independent of the radius of the circle chosen.

Given:

A circle $C(O, r)$ with centre $O$ and radius $r$. Let an arc $AB$ of length $r$ subtend an angle $\angle AOB = 1\text{ rad}$ at the centre. Let $AC$ be a diameter such that $\angle AOC$ is a straight angle.

Construction:

Produce the radius $AO$ to $C$ to form diameter $AC$.

Proof:

From classical geometry, angles subtended at the centre of a circle are directly proportional to the lengths of their subtending arcs.

$$ \frac{\angle AOB}{\angle AOC} = \frac{\text{length of arc } AB}{\text{length of semicircular arc } ABC} $$

Substitute the geometric values: * $\angle AOB = 1\text{ rad}$ * $\angle AOC = 2\text{ right angles} = 180^\circ$ * $\text{length of arc } AB = r$ * $\text{length of semicircular arc } ABC = \frac{1}{2}(2\pi r) = \pi r$

Therefore:

$$ \frac{1\text{ rad}}{2\text{ right angles}} = \frac{r}{\pi r} $$

$$ \frac{1\text{ rad}}{2\text{ right angles}} = \frac{1}{\pi} $$

$$ 1\text{ rad} = \frac{2\text{ right angles}}{\pi} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} $$

Since both "right angle" ($90^\circ$) and $\pi$ are absolute mathematical constants, $1\text{ radian}$ is a fixed constant angle, completely independent of the radius $r$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Relation Between Arc Length, Radius, and Angle Subtended ($l = r\theta$) (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:

If an arc of length $l$ subtends an angle $\theta$ (in radians) at the centre of a circle of radius $r$, then:

$$ l = r\theta \quad \iff \quad \theta = \frac{l}{r} $$

Given:

A circle $C(O, r)$ with centre $O$ and radius $r$. * Arc $AB$ has length equal to $r$, so $\angle AOB = 1\text{ rad}$. * Arc $AP$ has length $l$, subtending an unknown angle $\angle AOP = \theta\text{ rad}$.

To Prove:

$$ \theta = \frac{l}{r} \quad (\text{where } \theta \text{ is strictly in radians}) $$

Proof:

Using the fundamental circle proportion theorem:

$$ \frac{\angle AOP}{\angle AOB} = \frac{\text{length of arc } AP}{\text{length of arc } AB} $$

Substitute the values:

$$ \frac{\theta\text{ rad}}{1\text{ rad}} = \frac{l}{r} $$

$$ \theta = \frac{l}{r} \implies l = r\theta $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: Fundamental Conversion Bridge Between Degrees and Radians (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

From Theorem 1:

$$ \pi\text{ radians} = 180^\circ $$

From this identity:

  1. Radian to Degree Conversion:

$$ 1\text{ rad} = \left(\frac{180}{\pi}\right)^\circ \approx 57^\circ 16' 22'' $$

Derivation of the numerical value ($\pi \approx \frac{22}{7}$):

$$ 1\text{ rad} = \frac{180}{22/7}^\circ = \left(\frac{1260}{22}\right)^\circ = \left(\frac{630}{11}\right)^\circ = 57^\circ + \left(\frac{3}{11}\right)^\circ $$

Convert $\left(\frac{3}{11}\right)^\circ$ to minutes:

$$ \frac{3}{11} \times 60' = \left(\frac{180}{11}\right)' = 16' + \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)' $$

Convert $\left(\frac{4}{11}\right)'$ to seconds:

$$ \frac{4}{11} \times 60'' = \left(\frac{240}{11}\right)'' \approx 21.8'' \approx 22'' $$

$$ \therefore 1\text{ rad} \approx 57^\circ 16' 22'' $$

  1. Degree to Radian Conversion:

$$ 1^\circ = \left(\frac{\pi}{180}\right)\text{ rad} \approx 0.01746\text{ rad} $$

Core Operational Formulas:

$$ \text{Radian Measure} = \frac{\pi}{180} \times \text{Degree Measure} $$

$$ \text{Degree Measure} = \frac{180}{\pi} \times \text{Radian Measure} $$

Theorem 4: Area of a Circular Sector (⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:

The area $A$ of a sector of a circle of radius $r$ containing a central angle $\theta$ (in radians) is given by:

$$ A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}lr $$

Proof:

The area of a sector is directly proportional to its central angle:

$$ \frac{\text{Area of Sector}}{\text{Total Area of Circle}} = \frac{\theta\text{ rad}}{2\pi\text{ rad}} $$

$$ \frac{A}{\pi r^2} = \frac{\theta}{2\pi} $$

$$ A = \frac{\theta}{2\pi} \cdot \pi r^2 = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta $$

Since $l = r\theta$:

$$ A = \frac{1}{2}r(r\theta) = \frac{1}{2}lr $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

3. High-Yield Examples, NCERT Exemplar & Application Problems

Problem 1 (Standard Degree Conversion with Minutes): Convert $-47^\circ 30'$ into Radian Measure. (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Convert minutes into fractional degrees.

$$ 30' = \left(\frac{30}{60}\right)^\circ = \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^\circ $$

$$ 47^\circ 30' = \left(47 + \frac{1}{2}\right)^\circ = \left(\frac{95}{2}\right)^\circ $$

$$ \therefore -47^\circ 30' = -\left(\frac{95}{2}\right)^\circ $$

Step 2: Apply the conversion formula $\text{Radian Measure} = \frac{\pi}{180} \times \text{Degree Measure}$.

$$ \text{Radian Measure} = -\left(\frac{95}{2} \times \frac{\pi}{180}\right) $$

Step 3: Simplify by canceling common factors ($\gcd(95, 180) = 5$).

$$ -\left(\frac{19}{2} \times \frac{\pi}{36}\right) = -\frac{19\pi}{72}\text{ rad} $$

Scoring Breakdown (CBSE Pattern - 2 Marks):

  • [0.5 Mark]: Correctly converting $30'$ to degrees and expressing angle as $\frac{95}{2}^\circ$.
  • [0.5 Mark]: Correctly stating and substituting into $\text{Radian} = \frac{\pi}{180} \times \text{Degree}$.
  • [1.0 Mark]: Correct arithmetic reduction to $-\frac{19\pi}{72}$ with proper sign.

Problem 2 (Fractional Radian to Degree-Minute-Second): Convert $6\text{ radians}$ into Degree Measure. (Take $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$) (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Apply the formula.

$$ \text{Degree Measure} = \text{Radian Measure} \times \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} $$

$$ \text{Degree Measure} = 6 \times \frac{180}{22/7}^\circ = \frac{6 \times 180 \times 7}{22}^\circ = \frac{3 \times 180 \times 7}{11}^\circ = \left(\frac{3780}{11}\right)^\circ $$

Step 2: Extract whole degrees via Euclidean division.

$$ 3780 = 11 \times 343 + 7 $$

$$ \left(\frac{3780}{11}\right)^\circ = 343^\circ + \left(\frac{7}{11}\right)^\circ $$

Step 3: Convert fractional degrees to minutes ($1^\circ = 60'$).

$$ \left(\frac{7}{11}\right)^\circ = \frac{7 \times 60}{11}' = \left(\frac{420}{11}\right)' $$

$$ 420 = 11 \times 38 + 2 $$

$$ \left(\frac{420}{11}\right)' = 38' + \left(\frac{2}{11}\right)' $$

Step 4: Convert fractional minutes to seconds ($1' = 60''$).

$$ \left(\frac{2}{11}\right)' = \frac{2 \times 60}{11}'' = \left(\frac{120}{11}\right)'' \approx 10.91'' \approx 11'' $$

Step 5: Combine components.

$$ 6\text{ radians} \approx 343^\circ 38' 11'' $$

Scoring Breakdown (CBSE Pattern - 3 Marks):

  • [1.0 Mark]: Correct formula and fractional expression $\frac{3780}{11}^\circ$.
  • [1.0 Mark]: Extraction of $343^\circ$ and conversion to minutes giving $38'$.
  • [1.0 Mark]: Conversion to seconds and rounded answer $343^\circ 38' 11''$.

Problem 3 (NCERT Exemplar - Arc Length Equivalence): If in two circles, arcs of the same length subtend angles $65^\circ$ and $110^\circ$ at the centre, find the ratio of their radii. (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Convert central angles into radians. Let the radii of the two circles be $r_1$ and $r_2$.

$$ \theta_1 = 65^\circ = 65 \times \frac{\pi}{180}\text{ rad} = \frac{13\pi}{36}\text{ rad} $$

$$ \theta_2 = 110^\circ = 110 \times \frac{\pi}{180}\text{ rad} = \frac{22\pi}{36}\text{ rad} = \frac{11\pi}{18}\text{ rad} $$

Step 2: Set up arc length equations. Let the common arc length be $l$.

$$ l = r_1 \theta_1 = r_2 \theta_2 $$

Step 3: Equate and solve for the ratio $\frac{r_1}{r_2}$.

$$ r_1 \left(\frac{13\pi}{36}\right) = r_2 \left(\frac{22\pi}{36}\right) $$

$$ \frac{r_1}{r_2} = \frac{22\pi / 36}{13\pi / 36} = \frac{22}{13} $$

$$ \therefore r_1 : r_2 = 22 : 13 $$

Problem 4 (Real-World Application): A wheel makes $360$ revolutions in one minute. Through how many radians does it turn in one second? (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Calculate revolutions per second.

$$ \text{Revolutions in } 60\text{ seconds} = 360 $$

$$ \text{Revolutions in } 1\text{ second} = \frac{360}{60} = 6 $$

Step 2: Convert revolutions to radians. One complete revolution equals $2\pi\text{ radians}$.

$$ \text{Angle turned in 1 second} = 6 \times 2\pi = 12\pi\text{ radians} $$

Problem 5 (Clock Hands Problem): Find the angle between the hour hand and the minute hand of a clock at 7:20 in both degrees and radians. (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Determine the angular velocity of clock hands. * The minute hand covers $360^\circ$ in $60\text{ min} \implies 6^\circ/\text{min}$. * The hour hand covers $360^\circ$ in $12\text{ hours} (720\text{ min}) \implies 0.5^\circ/\text{min}$.

Step 2: Calculate positions from the 12:00 mark at 7:20 ($t = 440\text{ minutes}$ total for hour hand, $20\text{ minutes}$ for minute hand). * Hour hand position: $7 \times 30^\circ + 20 \times 0.5^\circ = 210^\circ + 10^\circ = 220^\circ$. * Minute hand position: $20 \times 6^\circ = 120^\circ$.

Step 3: Compute the difference.

$$ \theta = |220^\circ - 120^\circ| = 100^\circ $$

Step 4: Convert to radians.

$$ \theta = 100 \times \frac{\pi}{180}\text{ rad} = \frac{5\pi}{9}\text{ rad} $$

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Transition from Acute Ratios to Real-Valued Functions (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

In secondary school (Class 10), trigonometry is restricted to right-angled triangles where an angle $\theta$ satisfies $0^\circ < \theta < 90^\circ$. Trigonometric ratios are defined as quotients of side lengths:

$$ \sin \theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}, \quad \cos \theta = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}}, \quad \tan \theta = \frac{\text{Opposite}}{\text{Adjacent}} $$

This framework cannot define expressions such as $\sin(150^\circ)$, $\cos(\pi)$, or $\tan\left(-\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)$.

In Higher Secondary Mathematics (Class 11 NCERT), trigonometry is generalized from geometric ratios to circular functions on the real line $\mathbb{R}$.

The Unit Circle Definition

Consider a Cartesian coordinate system with origin $O(0,0)$. Draw a unit circle:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = 1 $$

Let the circle intersect the positive $x$-axis at $A(1,0)$. Let $P(a, b)$ be any point on the circle such that the terminal ray $OP$ makes an angle of $x$ radians with the positive direction of the $x$-axis in the anti-clockwise sense.

Since radius $r = 1$, by arc length formula $s = r\theta$:

$$ \text{Length of arc } AP = 1 \cdot x = x \text{ units} $$

The coordinates $(a, b)$ of point $P$ define the fundamental circular functions:

$$ \cos x = a \quad (\text{the } x\text{-coordinate of } P) $$

$$ \sin x = b \quad (\text{the } y\text{-coordinate of } P) $$

Hence, every point on the unit circle has coordinates:

$$ P(x) = (\cos x, \sin x) $$

From these, the remaining circular functions are defined algebraically for real values of $x$:

$$ \tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{b}{a} \quad (a \neq 0) $$

$$ \cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{a}{b} \quad (b \neq 0) $$

$$ \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x} = \frac{1}{a} \quad (a \neq 0) $$

$$ \operatorname{cosec} x = \frac{1}{\sin x} = \frac{1}{b} \quad (b \neq 0) $$

  • Quadrant II (S): Sine (and its reciprocal $\operatorname{cosec}$) are positive.

  • Quadrant IV (C): Cosine (and its reciprocal $\sec$) are positive.

1.3 Periodicity and Quadrantal Values (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

A function $f(x)$ is periodic if there exists a positive real number $T$ such that:

$$ f(x + T) = f(x) \quad \forall x \in \text{Domain}(f) $$

The least such positive value $T$ is the fundamental period.

Since a complete revolution around the unit circle corresponds to an angle of $2\pi$ radians, the terminal side returns to the identical position:

$$ P(x + 2\pi) = P(x) \implies (\cos(x + 2\pi), \sin(x + 2\pi)) = (\cos x, \sin x) $$

Thus, $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are periodic with fundamental period $2\pi$:

$$ \sin(x + 2n\pi) = \sin x, \quad \cos(x + 2n\pi) = \cos x \quad (n \in \mathbb{Z}) $$

For tangent and cotangent:

$$ \tan(x + \pi) = \frac{\sin(x + \pi)}{\cos(x + \pi)} = \frac{-\sin x}{-\cos x} = \tan x $$

Hence, the fundamental period of $\tan x$ and $\cot x$ is $\pi$.

Quadrantal Angles

Points of intersection of the unit circle with coordinate axes: - For $x = 0$ (Ray along $+x$-axis): Point is $(1, 0) \implies \cos 0 = 1, \sin 0 = 0$ - For $x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ (Ray along $+y$-axis): Point is $(0, 1) \implies \cos \frac{\pi}{2} = 0, \sin \frac{\pi}{2} = 1$ - For $x = \pi$ (Ray along $-x$-axis): Point is $(-1, 0) \implies \cos \pi = -1, \sin \pi = 0$ - For $x = \frac{3\pi}{2}$ (Ray along $-y$-axis): Point is $(0, -1) \implies \cos \frac{3\pi}{2} = 0, \sin \frac{3\pi}{2} = -1$ - For $x = 2\pi$: Point is $(1, 0) \implies \cos 2\pi = 1, \sin 2\pi = 0$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: Fundamental Pythagorean Identity $\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem: For any real number $x$, $\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$.

Given: A unit circle centered at origin $O(0,0)$ defined by equation:

$$ u^2 + v^2 = 1 $$

An arc $AP$ of length $|x|$ on this circle subtending an angle $x$ radians at the origin, with $A = (1,0)$ and terminal point $P(a, b)$.

To Prove:

$$ \cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1 $$

Proof: 1. By the definition of circular functions on a unit circle:

$$ a = \cos x \quad \text{and} \quad b = \sin x $$

  1. Since $P(a, b)$ lies directly on the circumference of the unit circle $u^2 + v^2 = 1$, its coordinates must satisfy the circle's equation:

$$ a^2 + b^2 = 1 $$

  1. Substituting $a = \cos x$ and $b = \sin x$ into the equation:

$$ (\cos x)^2 + (\sin x)^2 = 1 \implies \cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Corollaries:

  1. Divide both sides by $\cos^2 x$ (where $\cos x \neq 0$, i.e., $x \neq (2n+1)\frac{\pi}{2}$):

$$ \frac{\cos^2 x}{\cos^2 x} + \frac{\sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} \implies 1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x $$

  1. Divide both sides by $\sin^2 x$ (where $\sin x \neq 0$, i.e., $x \neq n\pi$):

$$ \frac{\cos^2 x}{\sin^2 x} + \frac{\sin^2 x}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{1}{\sin^2 x} \implies \cot^2 x + 1 = \operatorname{cosec}^2 x $$

Proof 2.2: Even and Odd Parity: $\cos(-x) = \cos x$ and $\sin(-x) = -\sin x$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem: For any real number $x$: 1. $\cos(-x) = \cos x$ (Even Function) 2. $\sin(-x) = -\sin x$ (Odd Function)

Given: The unit circle $u^2 + v^2 = 1$. Let point $A(1,0)$ lie on the $u$-axis. - Let $P(a,b)$ be the point on the unit circle such that $\angle AOP = x$ (measured counterclockwise). - Let $Q(a', b')$ be the point on the unit circle such that $\angle AOQ = -x$ (measured clockwise).

To Prove: 1. $a' = a \implies \cos(-x) = \cos x$ 2. $b' = -b \implies \sin(-x) = -\sin x$

Construction: Draw a perpendicular line segment from $P$ to the $u$-axis intersecting the axis at point $M(a, 0)$. Extend $PM$ downwards to meet the circle at $Q$.

Proof: 1. In $\triangle OMP$ and $\triangle OMQ$: - $OM = OM$ (Common base) - $OP = OQ = 1$ (Radii of the unit circle) - $\angle POM = |\angle QOM| = |x|$ (By construction)

  1. By SAS Congruence Criterion:

$$ \triangle OMP \cong \triangle OMQ $$

  1. Corresponding parts of congruent triangles (CPCT):
  2. $MP = MQ$

  3. $\angle OMP = \angle OMQ = 90^\circ$

  4. The line segment $PQ$ is perpendicular to the $u$-axis and bisected by it. Therefore:

  5. The $u$-coordinate of $Q$ is identical to $P$:

$$ a' = a \implies \cos(-x) = \cos x $$

  • The $v$-coordinate of $Q$ has equal magnitude but opposite sign to that of $P$:

$$ b' = -b \implies \sin(-x) = -\sin x $$

  1. Parity of derived functions:

$$ \tan(-x) = \frac{\sin(-x)}{\cos(-x)} = \frac{-\sin x}{\cos x} = -\tan x \quad (\text{Odd}) $$

$$ \cot(-x) = \frac{\cos(-x)}{\sin(-x)} = \frac{\cos x}{-\sin x} = -\cot x \quad (\text{Odd}) $$

$$ \sec(-x) = \frac{1}{\cos(-x)} = \frac{1}{\cos x} = \sec x \quad (\text{Even}) $$

$$ \operatorname{cosec}(-x) = \frac{1}{\sin(-x)} = \frac{1}{-\sin x} = -\operatorname{cosec} x \quad (\text{Odd}) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: General Angle Reduction Formulae (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem:

$$ \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + x\right) = \cos x, \quad \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + x\right) = -\sin x $$

Proof via Coordinate Rotation: 1. Let point $P(\cos x, \sin x)$ be on the unit circle. 2. Rotating vector $\vec{OP}$ counter-clockwise by $\frac{\pi}{2}$ radians ($90^\circ$) yields vector $\vec{OQ}$ corresponding to the angle $\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$:

$$ Q = \left(\cos\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right), \sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right) $$

  1. In Cartesian plane geometry, rotating an arbitrary point $(a, b)$ counter-clockwise by $90^\circ$ around the origin maps it to $(-b, a)$:

$$ (a, b) \xrightarrow{+90^\circ} (-b, a) $$

  1. Since $a = \cos x$ and $b = \sin x$:

$$ \left(\cos\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right), \sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right) = (-\sin x, \cos x) $$

  1. Equating corresponding components:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + x\right) = -\sin x $$

$$ \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + x\right) = \cos x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Problem 4.1: Determining All Ratios Given One Value and Quadrant (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement: If $\cos x = -\frac{3}{5}$, and $x$ lies in the third quadrant ($x \in \left(\pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}\right)$), find the exact values of the other five trigonometric functions.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Calculate $\sin x$ using the fundamental identity:

$$ \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1 \implies \sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x $$

$$ \sin^2 x = 1 - \left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)^2 = 1 - \frac{9}{25} = \frac{16}{25} $$

$$ \sin x = \pm \sqrt{\frac{16}{25}} = \pm \frac{4}{5} $$

  1. Apply Quadrant Constraint: Since $x$ lies in the third quadrant, the $y$-coordinate is negative ($\sin x < 0$).

$$ \sin x = -\frac{4}{5} $$

  1. Compute the remaining ratios:

$$ \operatorname{cosec} x = \frac{1}{\sin x} = \frac{1}{-\frac{4}{5}} = -\frac{5}{4} $$

$$ \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x} = \frac{1}{-\frac{3}{5}} = -\frac{5}{3} $$

$$ \tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{-\frac{4}{5}}{-\frac{3}{5}} = \frac{4}{3} $$

$$ \cot x = \frac{1}{\tan x} = \frac{1}{\frac{4}{3}} = \frac{3}{4} $$

Verification via Signs:

  • Quadrant III: Only $\tan x$ and $\cot x$ should be positive.
  • Calculated: $\tan x = \frac{4}{3} > 0$, $\cot x = \frac{3}{4} > 0$. $\sin, \cos, \sec, \operatorname{cosec}$ are all negative. Fully consistent.

CBSE Scoring Breakdown:

  • Identity application and value substitution: 1 Mark
  • Correct sign justification using quadrant condition: 1 Mark
  • Correct values of all 5 functions: 2 Marks

Problem 4.2: Evaluating Large & Negative Argument Ratios (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement: Evaluate: 1. $\sin\left(-\frac{11\pi}{3}\right)$ 2. $\cot\left(-\frac{15\pi}{4}\right)$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Part 1: $\sin\left(-\frac{11\pi}{3}\right)$

  1. Apply parity of sine ($\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$):

$$ \sin\left(-\frac{11\pi}{3}\right) = -\sin\left(\frac{11\pi}{3}\right) $$

  1. Decompose the improper fraction relative to the nearest multiple of $2\pi$:

$$ \frac{11\pi}{3} = \frac{12\pi - \pi}{3} = 4\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = 2(2\pi) - \frac{\pi}{3} $$

  1. Use periodicity $\sin(2k\pi - \theta) = -\sin\theta$:

$$ \sin\left(4\pi - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} $$

  1. Substitute back:

$$ \sin\left(-\frac{11\pi}{3}\right) = -\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} $$

Part 2: $\cot\left(-\frac{15\pi}{4}\right)$

  1. Apply parity of cotangent ($\cot(-\theta) = -\cot\theta$):

$$ \cot\left(-\frac{15\pi}{4}\right) = -\cot\left(\frac{15\pi}{4}\right) $$

  1. Express with respect to a multiple of $\pi$ (since the period of cotangent is $\pi$):

$$ \frac{15\pi}{4} = \frac{16\pi - \pi}{4} = 4\pi - \frac{\pi}{4} $$

  1. Apply $\cot(k\pi - \theta) = -\cot\theta$:

$$ \cot\left(4\pi - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = -\cot\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = -1 $$

  1. Substitute back:

$$ \cot\left(-\frac{15\pi}{4}\right) = -(-1) = 1 $$

Problem 4.3: Analytical Domain and Range Determination (NCERT Exemplar) (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement: Find the domain and range of the real-valued function:

$$ f(x) = \frac{1}{2 - \sin 3x} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Condition for Domain: $f(x)$ is defined for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$ such that the denominator is non-zero:

$$ 2 - \sin 3x \neq 0 \implies \sin 3x \neq 2 $$

For all real $u$, the range of the sine function is $-1 \le \sin u \le 1$. Because $\sin 3x$ can never equal $2$, the denominator $2 - \sin 3x \ge 2 - 1 = 1 > 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. The denominator is never zero.

$$ \text{Domain of } f = \mathbb{R} $$

  1. Determining the Range: Let $y = f(x) = \frac{1}{2 - \sin 3x}$.

We establish bounds on $\sin 3x$:

$$ -1 \le \sin 3x \le 1 $$

Multiply the inequality by $-1$ (reversing inequality signs):

$$ 1 \ge -\sin 3x \ge -1 \iff -1 \le -\sin 3x \le 1 $$

Add $2$ across all components:

$$ 2 - 1 \le 2 - \sin 3x \le 2 + 1 \implies 1 \le 2 - \sin 3x \le 3 $$

Since all expressions are strictly positive, take the reciprocal (which reverses the inequalities):

$$ \frac{1}{3} \le \frac{1}{2 - \sin 3x} \le \frac{1}{1} $$

$$ \frac{1}{3} \le y \le 1 $$

$$ \text{Range of } f = \left[\frac{1}{3}, 1\right] $$

Problem 4.4: Range of General Linear Combination $a\cos x + b\sin x + c$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement: Prove that for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$ -\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \le a\cos x + b\sin x \le \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} $$

Hence, determine the maximum and minimum values of $g(x) = 3\sin x - 4\cos x + 7$.

Step-by-Step Derivation:

  1. Consider the expression $E = a\cos x + b\sin x$. Multiply and divide by $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ (assuming $a^2 + b^2 \neq 0$):

$$ E = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \left[ \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}\cos x + \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}}\sin x \right] $$

  1. Since $\left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}\right)^2 = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{a^2 + b^2} = 1$, there exists an auxiliary angle $\phi$ such that:

$$ \cos \phi = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} \quad \text{and} \quad \sin \phi = \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}} $$

  1. Substitute these into $E$:

$$ E = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} [\cos x \cos \phi + \sin x \sin \phi] = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \cos(x - \phi) $$

  1. For any angle $(x - \phi) \in \mathbb{R}$:

$$ -1 \le \cos(x - \phi) \le 1 $$

  1. Multiplying by the positive constant $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$:

$$ -\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} \le a\cos x + b\sin x \le \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} $$

  1. Application to $g(x) = 3\sin x - 4\cos x + 7$: Here $a = -4, b = 3$:

$$ \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{(-4)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} = 5 $$

Therefore:

$$ -5 \le 3\sin x - 4\cos x \le 5 $$

Add $7$ across the inequality:

$$ -5 + 7 \le 3\sin x - 4\cos x + 7 \le 5 + 7 \implies 2 \le g(x) \le 12 $$

  • $\text{Maximum value} = 12$
  • $\text{Minimum value} = 2$

5. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap 5.1: Neglecting the Dual Sign upon Taking Square Roots

  • Incorrect: When $\cos x = -\frac{3}{5}$, writing $\sin x = \sqrt{1 - \cos^2 x} = \frac{4}{5}$.
  • Trap: Forgetting that $\sqrt{u^2} = |u|$, so $\sin x = \pm \sqrt{1 - \cos^2 x}$. The quadrant of $x$ uniquely dictates whether the sign is $+$ or $-$. Always write out the quadrant condition explicitly before choosing the sign.

Trap 5.2: The "Even Function" Sign Slip

  • Mistake: Writing $\cos(-x) = -\cos x$ by wrongly extrapolating from algebra where $-f(x) \neq f(-x)$.
  • Trap: Cosine and Secant are even functions:

$$ \cos(-\theta) = \cos \theta, \quad \sec(-\theta) = \sec \theta $$

The negative sign inside cosine has no effect on the result: $\cos(-60^\circ) = \cos(60^\circ) = \frac{1}{2}$.

Trap 5.3: Domain Exclusion Points

  • Mistake: Writing the domain of $\tan x$ as $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{90^\circ\}$ or $\mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}$.
  • Trap: $\tan x$ is undefined at every odd multiple of $\frac{\pi}{2}$, both positive and negative:

$$ \text{Domain} = \mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{(2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{2} : n \in \mathbb{Z}\right\} $$

Writing only the principal point misses an infinite number of discontinuities and loses marks in CBSE exams.

Trap 5.4: Period of $\tan x$ vs. $\sin x$

  • Mistake: Assuming the fundamental period of all trigonometric functions is $2\pi$.
  • Trap:
  • Period of $\sin x, \cos x, \sec x, \operatorname{cosec} x$ is $2\pi$.
  • Period of $\tan x, \cot x$ is $\pi$.
  • For $f(x) = \tan(kx)$, the period is $\frac{\pi}{|k|}$, not $\frac{2\pi}{|k|}$.

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Fundamental Principle & Geometric Orientation

In early secondary education (Class 10), trigonometric ratios are defined strictly as ratios of sides of a right-angled triangle, restricting the domain of an angle $x$ to $0^\circ \le x \le 90^\circ$. In Class 11, we generalize trigonometric ratios to Trigonometric Functions of real numbers (or radian measure of angles) defined over the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.

A common misconception among students when introduced to compound angles is assuming linearity of trigonometric operators, i.e., treating $\cos(x+y)$ as $\cos x + \cos y$ or $\sin(x-y)$ as $\sin x - \sin y$. Trigonometric functions are non-linear transcendental functions. The projection of a vector sum on an axis does not decompose into independent single-angle projections without cross-coupling terms (sine-cosine products).

Theorem 1: The Master Identity — $\cos(x + y) = \cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y$

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Most frequently asked long-answer derivation in CBSE Class 11 examinations)

Given:

Two real numbers (or angles in radian measure) $x$ and $y$.

To Prove:

$$ \cos(x + y) = \cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y $$

Construction & Geometric Setup:

Consider a unit circle centered at the origin $O(0,0)$ of the Cartesian coordinate plane with radius $r = 1$. Let the standard initial line be the positive $x$-axis, cutting the unit circle at point $P_4(1, 0)$.

  1. Let an angle $x$ be generated in the standard counter-clockwise direction to locate point $P_1$ on the circle. The coordinates of $P_1$ are:

$$ P_1 = (\cos x, \sin x) $$

  1. Let an angle $y$ be further added in the counter-clockwise direction from ray $OP_1$ so that the total angle from the positive $x$-axis to ray $OP_2$ is $(x + y)$. The coordinates of $P_2$ are:

$$ P_2 = (\cos(x + y), \sin(x + y)) $$

  1. Now, from the positive $x$-axis, traverse an angle $y$ in the clockwise direction (representing the angle $-y$) to locate point $P_3$. The coordinates of $P_3$ are:

$$ P_3 = (\cos(-y), \sin(-y)) = (\cos y, -\sin y) $$

(Using parity properties $\cos(-y) = \cos y$ and $\sin(-y) = -\sin y$)

  1. Join the line segments $P_1P_3$ and $P_2P_4$.

$$ P_1P_3 = P_2P_4 \implies (P_1P_3)^2 = (P_2P_4)^2 $$

Step 2: Distance Formula Computation for $(P_1P_3)^2$ Using the Euclidean distance formula $d^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2$:

$$ P_1 = (\cos x, \sin x), \quad P_3 = (\cos y, -\sin y) $$

$$ (P_1P_3)^2 = [\cos x - \cos y]^2 + [\sin x - (-\sin y)]^2 $$

$$ (P_1P_3)^2 = [\cos x - \cos y]^2 + [\sin x + \sin y]^2 $$

Expanding each square:

$$ (P_1P_3)^2 = (\cos^2 x - 2\cos x \cos y + \cos^2 y) + (\sin^2 x + 2\sin x \sin y + \sin^2 y) $$

Regrouping Pythagorean identities $(\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1)$ and $(\cos^2 y + \sin^2 y = 1)$:

$$ (P_1P_3)^2 = (\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x) + (\cos^2 y + \sin^2 y) - 2\cos x \cos y + 2\sin x \sin y $$

$$ (P_1P_3)^2 = 1 + 1 - 2(\cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y) $$

$$ (P_1P_3)^2 = 2 - 2(\cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y) \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)} $$

Step 3: Distance Formula Computation for $(P_2P_4)^2$ Using points $P_2 = (\cos(x + y), \sin(x + y))$ and $P_4 = (1, 0)$:

$$ (P_2P_4)^2 = [\cos(x + y) - 1]^2 + [\sin(x + y) - 0]^2 $$

$$ (P_2P_4)^2 = [\cos^2(x + y) - 2\cos(x + y) + 1] + \sin^2(x + y) $$

Regrouping $\cos^2(x + y) + \sin^2(x + y) = 1$:

$$ (P_2P_4)^2 = [\cos^2(x + y) + \sin^2(x + y)] + 1 - 2\cos(x + y) $$

$$ (P_2P_4)^2 = 1 + 1 - 2\cos(x + y) $$

$$ (P_2P_4)^2 = 2 - 2\cos(x + y) \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)} $$

Step 4: Equating Equations (1) and (2) Since $(P_1P_3)^2 = (P_2P_4)^2$:

$$ 2 - 2\cos(x + y) = 2 - 2(\cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y) $$

Subtracting $2$ from both sides:

$$ -2\cos(x + y) = -2(\cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y) $$

Dividing both sides by $-2$:

$$ \cos(x + y) = \cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Deduction of $\cos(x - y) = \cos x \cos y + \sin x \sin y$

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Proof:

In the identity $\cos(x + y) = \cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y$, replace $y$ with $-y$:

$$ \cos(x + (-y)) = \cos x \cos(-y) - \sin x \sin(-y) $$

Using the even/odd symmetry relations $\cos(-y) = \cos y$ and $\sin(-y) = -\sin y$:

$$ \cos(x - y) = \cos x (\cos y) - \sin x (-\sin y) $$

$$ \cos(x - y) = \cos x \cos y + \sin x \sin y $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: Complementary Angle Formulations

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

  1. To Prove: $\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \sin x$ Substitute $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ and $y = x$ into $\cos(x - y)$:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos\frac{\pi}{2}\cos x + \sin\frac{\pi}{2}\sin x $$

Since $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 0$ and $\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 1$:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = (0)\cos x + (1)\sin x = \sin x $$

  1. To Prove: $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos x$ Let $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{2} - x$. Then $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta$.

$$ \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos\left[\frac{\pi}{2} - \left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)\right] = \cos(x) $$

Theorem 4: The Sine Addition and Subtraction Identities

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

1. To Prove: $\sin(x + y) = \sin x \cos y + \cos x \sin y$

Using the complementary relation $\sin \theta = \cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right)$:

$$ \sin(x + y) = \cos\left[\frac{\pi}{2} - (x + y)\right] = \cos\left[\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) - y\right] $$

Treat $\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right)$ as a single angle and apply $\cos(A - B) = \cos A \cos B + \sin A \sin B$:

$$ \sin(x + y) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)\cos y + \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)\sin y $$

Applying $\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \sin x$ and $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos x$:

$$ \sin(x + y) = \sin x \cos y + \cos x \sin y $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2. To Prove: $\sin(x - y) = \sin x \cos y - \cos x \sin y$

Replace $y$ with $-y$ in $\sin(x + y)$:

$$ \sin(x - y) = \sin(x + (-y)) = \sin x \cos(-y) + \cos x \sin(-y) $$

$$ \sin(x - y) = \sin x \cos y - \cos x \sin y $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 5: The Tangent Addition and Subtraction Identities

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Conditions:

None of the angles $x$, $y$, or $(x \pm y)$ is an odd multiple of $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ (so that tangents are well-defined: $\cos x \ne 0, \cos y \ne 0, \cos(x \pm y) \ne 0$).

1. To Prove: $\tan(x + y) = \dfrac{\tan x + \tan y}{1 - \tan x \tan y}$

By quotient identity:

$$ \tan(x + y) = \frac{\sin(x + y)}{\cos(x + y)} = \frac{\sin x \cos y + \cos x \sin y}{\cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y} $$

Divide numerator and denominator simultaneously by $\cos x \cos y$ (permissible since $\cos x \ne 0$ and $\cos y \ne 0$):

$$ \tan(x + y) = \frac{\dfrac{\sin x \cos y}{\cos x \cos y} + \dfrac{\cos x \sin y}{\cos x \cos y}}{\dfrac{\cos x \cos y}{\cos x \cos y} - \dfrac{\sin x \sin y}{\cos x \cos y}} $$

Simplifying each individual quotient term:

$$ \tan(x + y) = \frac{\tan x + \tan y}{1 - \tan x \tan y} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2. To Prove: $\tan(x - y) = \dfrac{\tan x - \tan y}{1 + \tan x \tan y}$

Replace $y$ with $-y$ in the addition identity:

$$ \tan(x - y) = \frac{\tan x + \tan(-y)}{1 - \tan x \tan(-y)} $$

Since $\tan(-y) = -\tan y$:

$$ \tan(x - y) = \frac{\tan x - \tan y}{1 + \tan x \tan y} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 6: The Cotangent Addition and Subtraction Identities

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐

Conditions:

None of the angles $x$, $y$, or $(x \pm y)$ is an integral multiple of $\pi$ ($\sin x \ne 0, \sin y \ne 0, \sin(x \pm y) \ne 0$).

To Prove:

$$ \cot(x + y) = \frac{\cot x \cot y - 1}{\cot y + \cot x} $$

Proof:

$$ \cot(x + y) = \frac{\cos(x + y)}{\sin(x + y)} = \frac{\cos x \cos y - \sin x \sin y}{\sin x \cos y + \cos x \sin y} $$

Divide numerator and denominator by $\sin x \sin y$:

$$ \cot(x + y) = \frac{\dfrac{\cos x \cos y}{\sin x \sin y} - \dfrac{\sin x \sin y}{\sin x \sin y}}{\dfrac{\sin x \cos y}{\sin x \sin y} + \dfrac{\cos x \sin y}{\sin x \sin y}} = \frac{\cot x \cot y - 1}{\cot y + \cot x} $$

Replacing $y$ with $-y$ yields:

$$ \cot(x - y) = \frac{\cot x \cot y + 1}{\cot y - \cot x} $$

(Note the critical order in the denominator: $\cot y - \cot x$).

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

3. High-Yield Examples & Exemplar Applications

Problem 1 (Direct Exact Value Evaluation)

Find the exact values of: 1. $\sin 75^\circ$ and $\cos 15^\circ$ 2. $\tan 105^\circ$

Solution:

(1) For $\sin 75^\circ$: Express $75^\circ$ as the sum of standard angles $45^\circ + 30^\circ$:

$$ \sin 75^\circ = \sin(45^\circ + 30^\circ) = \sin 45^\circ \cos 30^\circ + \cos 45^\circ \sin 30^\circ $$

Substitute standard values:

$$ \sin 75^\circ = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{3} + 1}{2\sqrt{2}} $$

Rationalizing the denominator:

$$ \sin 75^\circ = \frac{(\sqrt{3} + 1)\sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4} $$

Notice that by complementary angle relations:

$$ \cos 15^\circ = \cos(90^\circ - 75^\circ) = \sin 75^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{6} + \sqrt{2}}{4} $$

(2) For $\tan 105^\circ$: Express $105^\circ = 60^\circ + 45^\circ$:

$$ \tan 105^\circ = \tan(60^\circ + 45^\circ) = \frac{\tan 60^\circ + \tan 45^\circ}{1 - \tan 60^\circ \tan 45^\circ} $$

Substitute $\tan 60^\circ = \sqrt{3}$ and $\tan 45^\circ = 1$:

$$ \tan 105^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{3} + 1}{1 - \sqrt{3}(1)} = \frac{\sqrt{3} + 1}{1 - \sqrt{3}} $$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $(1 + \sqrt{3})$:

$$ \tan 105^\circ = \frac{(\sqrt{3} + 1)(\sqrt{3} + 1)}{(1 - \sqrt{3})(1 + \sqrt{3})} = \frac{3 + 2\sqrt{3} + 1}{1 - 3} = \frac{4 + 2\sqrt{3}}{-2} = -(2 + \sqrt{3}) $$

Problem 2 (NCERT Exemplar — Trigonometric Equation Identity)

Prove that:

$$ \frac{\cos 9^\circ + \sin 9^\circ}{\cos 9^\circ - \sin 9^\circ} = \tan 54^\circ $$

Step-by-Step Solution & Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Divide numerator and denominator of LHS by $\cos 9^\circ$
    (1 Mark)

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{\dfrac{\cos 9^\circ}{\cos 9^\circ} + \dfrac{\sin 9^\circ}{\cos 9^\circ}}{\dfrac{\cos 9^\circ}{\cos 9^\circ} - \dfrac{\sin 9^\circ}{\cos 9^\circ}} = \frac{1 + \tan 9^\circ}{1 - \tan 9^\circ} $$

  • Step 2: Recognize $\tan 45^\circ = 1$ to map onto the addition formula
    (1 Mark)

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{\tan 45^\circ + \tan 9^\circ}{1 - (\tan 45^\circ)(\tan 9^\circ)} $$

  • Step 3: Apply $\tan(A + B)$ formula
    (1 Mark)

$$ \text{LHS} = \tan(45^\circ + 9^\circ) = \tan 54^\circ = \text{RHS} $$

$$ \text{Hence Proved.} $$

Problem 3 (Classic 3-Angle Tangent Identity)

Prove that:

$$ \tan 3x \tan 2x \tan x = \tan 3x - \tan 2x - \tan x $$

Solution:

Decompose the largest compound angle:

$$ 3x = 2x + x $$

Take tangent on both sides:

$$ \tan 3x = \tan(2x + x) $$

Apply the sum formula:

$$ \tan 3x = \frac{\tan 2x + \tan x}{1 - \tan 2x \tan x} $$

Cross-multiply:

$$ \tan 3x (1 - \tan 2x \tan x) = \tan 2x + \tan x $$

$$ \tan 3x - \tan 3x \tan 2x \tan x = \tan 2x + \tan x $$

Rearranging terms:

$$ \tan 3x - \tan 2x - \tan x = \tan 3x \tan 2x \tan x $$

$$ \text{Hence Proved.} $$

Problem 4 (Conditional Identity in Triangles)

If $A + B + C = \pi$, prove that:

$$ \tan A + \tan B + \tan C = \tan A \tan B \tan C $$

Solution:

Given $A + B + C = \pi \implies A + B = \pi - C$.

Apply tangent on both sides:

$$ \tan(A + B) = \tan(\pi - C) $$

Using quadrant rule $\tan(\pi - \theta) = -\tan \theta$:

$$ \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A \tan B} = -\tan C $$

Cross-multiply:

$$ \tan A + \tan B = -\tan C (1 - \tan A \tan B) $$

$$ \tan A + \tan B = -\tan C + \tan A \tan B \tan C $$

Add $\tan C$ to both sides:

$$ \tan A + \tan B + \tan C = \tan A \tan B \tan C $$

$$ \text{Hence Proved.} $$

Problem 5 (Linear Combination: $A\cos x + B\sin x$)

Find the maximum and minimum values of the expression:

$$ f(x) = 3\sin x + 4\cos x + 7 $$

Solution:

For any expression of the form $A\cos x + B\sin x$: Let $A = R\cos\alpha$ and $B = R\sin\alpha$. Squaring and adding:

$$ R^2(\cos^2\alpha + \sin^2\alpha) = A^2 + B^2 \implies R = \sqrt{A^2 + B^2} $$

Then:

$$ A\cos x + B\sin x = R(\cos x \cos\alpha + \sin x \sin\alpha) = R\cos(x - \alpha) $$

Since $-1 \le \cos(x - \alpha) \le 1$:

$$ -\sqrt{A^2 + B^2} \le A\cos x + B\sin x \le \sqrt{A^2 + B^2} $$

For our function:

$$ A = 4, \quad B = 3 \implies R = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5 $$

$$ -5 \le 3\sin x + 4\cos x \le 5 $$

Adding 7 across the inequality:

$$ -5 + 7 \le 3\sin x + 4\cos x + 7 \le 5 + 7 $$

$$ 2 \le f(x) \le 12 $$

  • Maximum Value: $12$
  • Minimum Value: $2$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Trap 1: The "Algebraic Linear Distributive" Illusion

  • Incorrect Step: $\sin(A + B) = \sin A + \sin B$ or $\cos(A - B) = \cos A - \cos B$.
  • Pedagogical Correction: Emphasize that $\sin$ and $\cos$ are functions, not multiplicative factors. Show counter-examples:

$$ \sin(30^\circ + 60^\circ) = \sin 90^\circ = 1 $$

$$ \sin 30^\circ + \sin 60^\circ = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \frac{1 + \sqrt{3}}{2} \ne 1 $$

Trap 2: Signs in Cosine vs. Sine Formulae

  • Error: Writing $\cos(x + y) = \cos x \cos y + \sin x \sin y$ (swapping the negative sign).
  • The Rule: Cosine changes signs ($\pm \to \mp$); Sine retains signs ($\pm \to \pm$).
  • $\cos(\text{Sum}) \longrightarrow \text{Difference}$
  • $\sin(\text{Sum}) \longrightarrow \text{Sum}$

Trap 3: The Cotangent Denominator Inversion

  • Error: Writing $\cot(A - B) = \dfrac{\cot A \cot B + 1}{\cot A - \cot B}$.
  • The Correction: The denominator is strictly $\cot B - \cot A$. Because cotangent subtraction is anti-commutative ($\cot A - \cot B \ne \cot B - \cot A$), reversing the order leads to an overall sign flip.

Trap 4: Domain Violations in Tangent Identities

  • Error: Applying $\tan(x + y) = \dfrac{\tan x + \tan y}{1 - \tan x \tan y}$ when $x = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ or $y = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.
  • The Correction: $\tan\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$ is undefined. When simplifying expressions involving $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$, revert to $\dfrac{\sin}{\cos}$ forms directly rather than using $\tan$ compound expansion.

5. Speed Hacks, Mnemonics & Golden Points

5.1 The Master Mnemonics

1. Co-workers vs. Selfish functions (Sine vs. Cosine):

  • Sine is Friendly / Cooperative: It mixes functions and preserves family signs:

$$ \sin(x \pm y) = \sin x \cos y \pm \cos x \sin y $$

(Mixes Sine and Cosine together, keeps the exact same $+$ or $-$ sign). * Cosine is Selfish / Opposing: It clumps its own kind together and reverses the sign:

$$ \cos(x \pm y) = \cos x \cos y \mp \sin x \sin y $$

(Keeps Cosines together, Sines together, and flips the sign).

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Foundation & Progression

In the sequence of CBSE Class 11 Trigonometry, students transition from compound angle identities:

$$ \sin(A \pm B), \quad \cos(A \pm B), \quad \tan(A \pm B) $$

to Multiple Angles ($2x, 3x, \dots$) and Submultiple Angles ($x/2, x/3, \dots$).

  • Multiple Angles: Scaling the domain argument by an integer ($x \to n x$).

  • Transformation Identities: Bilateral conversion bridges between Products of Trigonometric Functions and Sums/Differences of Trigonometric Functions. They play a foundational role in calculus (enabling algebraic integration of products like $\int \sin mx \cos nx \, dx$).

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: Double-Angle Expansion of Sine

Statement: For any real number $x$,

$$ \sin 2x = 2\sin x \cos x = \frac{2\tan x}{1 + \tan^2 x} \quad \left(x \neq (2k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}, k \in \mathbb{Z}\right) $$

Proof (Part I: Product Form):

  • Given: Compound angle identity $\sin(A + B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B$.
  • To Prove: $\sin 2x = 2\sin x \cos x$.
  • Step 1: Substitute $A = x$ and $B = x$:

$$ \sin(x + x) = \sin x \cos x + \cos x \sin x $$

  • Step 2: Combine like terms:

$$ \sin 2x = 2\sin x \cos x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof (Part II: Tangent Form):

  • Given: $\sin 2x = 2\sin x \cos x$.
  • To Prove: $\sin 2x = \dfrac{2\tan x}{1 + \tan^2 x}$.
  • Step 1: Express $\sin 2x$ as a fraction over $1$:

$$ \sin 2x = \frac{2\sin x \cos x}{1} $$

  • Step 2: Apply the fundamental Pythagorean identity $1 = \cos^2 x + \sin^2 x$:

$$ \sin 2x = \frac{2\sin x \cos x}{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x} $$

  • Step 3: Divide both numerator and denominator by $\cos^2 x$ (for $\cos x \neq 0$):

$$ \sin 2x = \frac{\dfrac{2\sin x \cos x}{\cos^2 x}}{\dfrac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x}} = \frac{2\left(\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right)}{1 + \left(\dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right)^2} = \frac{2\tan x}{1 + \tan^2 x} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Double-Angle Expansions of Cosine

Statement: For any real number $x$,

$$ \cos 2x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = 2\cos^2 x - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \tan^2 x}{1 + \tan^2 x} $$

Proof:

  • Given: $\cos(A + B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B$.
  • Step 1: Substitute $A = x$ and $B = x$:

$$ \cos(x + x) = \cos x \cos x - \sin x \sin x \implies \cos 2x = \cos^2 x - \sin^2 x $$

  • Step 2: Express purely in terms of $\cos x$ using $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$:

$$ \cos 2x = \cos^2 x - (1 - \cos^2 x) = 2\cos^2 x - 1 $$

  • Step 3: Express purely in terms of $\sin x$ using $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$:

$$ \cos 2x = (1 - \sin^2 x) - \sin^2 x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x $$

  • Step 4: Express in terms of $\tan x$:

$$ \cos 2x = \frac{\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x}{1} = \frac{\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x} $$

Dividing numerator and denominator by $\cos^2 x$:

$$ \cos 2x = \frac{1 - \tan^2 x}{1 + \tan^2 x} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: Triple-Angle Identities

Statement: 1. $\sin 3x = 3\sin x - 4\sin^3 x$ 2. $\cos 3x = 4\cos^3 x - 3\cos x$ 3. $\tan 3x = \dfrac{3\tan x - \tan^3 x}{1 - 3\tan^2 x}$

Proof of $\sin 3x$:

  • LHS: $\sin(2x + x) = \sin 2x \cos x + \cos 2x \sin x$
  • Step 1: Substitute $\sin 2x = 2\sin x \cos x$ and $\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$:

$$ \sin 3x = (2\sin x \cos x)\cos x + (1 - 2\sin^2 x)\sin x $$

  • Step 2: Expand and simplify:

$$ \sin 3x = 2\sin x \cos^2 x + \sin x - 2\sin^3 x $$

  • Step 3: Replace $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$:

$$ \sin 3x = 2\sin x (1 - \sin^2 x) + \sin x - 2\sin^3 x = 2\sin x - 2\sin^3 x + \sin x - 2\sin^3 x $$

$$ \sin 3x = 3\sin x - 4\sin^3 x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof of $\cos 3x$:

  • LHS: $\cos(2x + x) = \cos 2x \cos x - \sin 2x \sin x$
  • Step 1: Substitute $\cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x - 1$ and $\sin 2x = 2\sin x \cos x$:

$$ \cos 3x = (2\cos^2 x - 1)\cos x - (2\sin x \cos x)\sin x $$

  • Step 2: Expand and simplify:

$$ \cos 3x = 2\cos^3 x - \cos x - 2\cos x \sin^2 x $$

  • Step 3: Replace $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$:

$$ \cos 3x = 2\cos^3 x - \cos x - 2\cos x(1 - \cos^2 x) = 2\cos^3 x - \cos x - 2\cos x + 2\cos^3 x $$

$$ \cos 3x = 4\cos^3 x - 3\cos x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof of $\tan 3x$:

  • LHS: $\tan(2x + x) = \dfrac{\tan 2x + \tan x}{1 - \tan 2x \tan x}$
  • Step 1: Substitute $\tan 2x = \dfrac{2\tan x}{1 - \tan^2 x}$:

$$ \tan 3x = \frac{\dfrac{2\tan x}{1 - \tan^2 x} + \tan x}{1 - \left(\dfrac{2\tan x}{1 - \tan^2 x}\right)\tan x} $$

  • Step 2: Clear denominators by multiplying numerator and denominator by $(1 - \tan^2 x)$:

$$ \tan 3x = \frac{2\tan x + \tan x(1 - \tan^2 x)}{(1 - \tan^2 x) - 2\tan^2 x} = \frac{2\tan x + \tan x - \tan^3 x}{1 - 3\tan^2 x} = \frac{3\tan x - \tan^3 x}{1 - 3\tan^2 x} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 4: Product-to-Sum/Difference Transformations

Statement: 1. $2\sin A \cos B = \sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)$ 2. $2\cos A \sin B = \sin(A+B) - \sin(A-B)$ 3. $2\cos A \cos B = \cos(A+B) + \cos(A-B)$ 4. $2\sin A \sin B = \cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)$

Derivation:

Recall the fundamental addition/subtraction formulas:

$$ \sin(A + B) = \sin A \cos B + \cos A \sin B \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

$$ \sin(A - B) = \sin A \cos B - \cos A \sin B \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

$$ \cos(A + B) = \cos A \cos B - \sin A \sin B \quad \text{--- (3)} $$

$$ \cos(A - B) = \cos A \cos B + \sin A \sin B \quad \text{--- (4)} $$

  • Adding $(1) + (2) \implies 2\sin A \cos B = \sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)$
  • Subtracting $(1) - (2) \implies 2\cos A \sin B = \sin(A+B) - \sin(A-B)$
  • Adding $(3) + (4) \implies 2\cos A \cos B = \cos(A+B) + \cos(A-B)$
  • Subtracting $(4) - (3) \implies 2\sin A \sin B = \cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 5: Sum/Difference-to-Product Formulas ($C$-$D$ Formulas)

Statement:

$$ \sin C + \sin D = 2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) $$

$$ \sin C - \sin D = 2\cos\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) $$

$$ \cos C + \cos D = 2\cos\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) $$

$$ \cos C - \cos D = -2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) = 2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{D-C}{2}\right) $$

Derivation:

Let $A + B = C$ and $A - B = D$.
Solving this linear system:

$$ A = \frac{C + D}{2}, \quad B = \frac{C - D}{2} $$

Substitute $A$ and $B$ into the Product-to-Sum/Difference identities: 1. $\sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B) = 2\sin A \cos B \implies \sin C + \sin D = 2\sin\left(\dfrac{C+D}{2}\right)\cos\left(\dfrac{C-D}{2}\right)$ 2. $\sin(A+B) - \sin(A-B) = 2\cos A \sin B \implies \sin C - \sin D = 2\cos\left(\dfrac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\dfrac{C-D}{2}\right)$ 3. $\cos(A+B) + \cos(A-B) = 2\cos A \cos B \implies \cos C + \cos D = 2\cos\left(\dfrac{C+D}{2}\right)\cos\left(\dfrac{C-D}{2}\right)$ 4. $\cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B) = 2\sin A \sin B \implies \cos D - \cos C = 2\sin\left(\dfrac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\dfrac{C-D}{2}\right)$

$$ \implies \cos C - \cos D = -2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) = 2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{D-C}{2}\right) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

3. High-Yield Solved Problems & Board Exemplars

Problem 1 (Standard Classic):

Prove that:

$$ \cos 20^\circ \cos 40^\circ \cos 60^\circ \cos 80^\circ = \frac{1}{16} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Extract Known Standard Values
We know $\cos 60^\circ = \dfrac{1}{2}$.

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \cos 20^\circ \cos 40^\circ \cos 80^\circ \right] $$

Step 2: Group Terms and Apply $2\cos A \cos B$
Multiply and divide by $2$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{1}{4} \left[ (2\cos 40^\circ \cos 20^\circ) \cos 80^\circ \right] $$

Using $2\cos A \cos B = \cos(A+B) + \cos(A-B)$:

$$ 2\cos 40^\circ \cos 20^\circ = \cos(40^\circ + 20^\circ) + \cos(40^\circ - 20^\circ) = \cos 60^\circ + \cos 20^\circ = \frac{1}{2} + \cos 20^\circ $$

Step 3: Distribute Outer Factor

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{1}{4} \left[ \left(\frac{1}{2} + \cos 20^\circ\right)\cos 80^\circ \right] = \frac{1}{8}\cos 80^\circ + \frac{1}{4}\cos 80^\circ \cos 20^\circ $$

Step 4: Transform Product Again
Multiply and divide the second term by $2$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{1}{8}\cos 80^\circ + \frac{1}{8}\left[ 2\cos 80^\circ \cos 20^\circ \right] $$

$$ 2\cos 80^\circ \cos 20^\circ = \cos(80^\circ + 20^\circ) + \cos(80^\circ - 20^\circ) = \cos 100^\circ + \cos 60^\circ = \cos 100^\circ + \frac{1}{2} $$

Step 5: Reduce via Allied Angles

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{1}{8}\cos 80^\circ + \frac{1}{8}\cos 100^\circ + \frac{1}{16} $$

Since $\cos 100^\circ = \cos(180^\circ - 80^\circ) = -\cos 80^\circ$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{1}{8}\cos 80^\circ - \frac{1}{8}\cos 80^\circ + \frac{1}{16} = \frac{1}{16} = \text{RHS} $$

CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown:

  • Substituting $\cos 60^\circ = 1/2$ & pairing terms: [1 Mark]
  • Applying $2\cos A \cos B$ correctly: [1.5 Marks]
  • Expanding and applying $\cos 100^\circ = -\cos 80^\circ$: [1 Mark]
  • Final algebraic cancellation & concluding statement: [0.5 Mark]

Problem 2 (Sum-to-Product Ratio / NCERT Core):

Prove that:

$$ \frac{\sin 5x - 2\sin 3x + \sin x}{\cos 5x - \cos x} = \tan x $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Regroup Numerator to Apply $C$-$D$ Formula

$$ \text{Numerator} = (\sin 5x + \sin x) - 2\sin 3x $$

Step 2: Transform Sum into Product
Using $\sin C + \sin D = 2\sin\left(\dfrac{C+D}{2}\right)\cos\left(\dfrac{C-D}{2}\right)$:

$$ \sin 5x + \sin x = 2\sin\left(\frac{5x+x}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{5x-x}{2}\right) = 2\sin 3x \cos 2x $$

Substitute back into the numerator:

$$ \text{Numerator} = 2\sin 3x \cos 2x - 2\sin 3x = 2\sin 3x (\cos 2x - 1) $$

Step 3: Transform Denominator
Using $\cos C - \cos D = -2\sin\left(\dfrac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\dfrac{C-D}{2}\right)$:

$$ \cos 5x - \cos x = -2\sin\left(\frac{5x+x}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{5x-x}{2}\right) = -2\sin 3x \sin 2x $$

Step 4: Simplify the Rational Expression

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{2\sin 3x (\cos 2x - 1)}{-2\sin 3x \sin 2x} $$

Canceling non-zero common factor $2\sin 3x$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{\cos 2x - 1}{-\sin 2x} = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{\sin 2x} $$

Step 5: Apply Double-Angle Power Reduction Formulas
Using $1 - \cos 2x = 2\sin^2 x$ and $\sin 2x = 2\sin x \cos x$:

$$ \text{LHS} = \frac{2\sin^2 x}{2\sin x \cos x} = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \tan x = \text{RHS} $$

CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown:

  • Grouping $(\sin 5x + \sin x)$ and applying formula: [1 Mark]
  • Factoring numerator and expanding denominator: [1 Mark]
  • Converting $1 - \cos 2x$ and $\sin 2x$: [1 Mark]
  • Final simplification to $\tan x$: [1 Mark]

Problem 3 (Submultiple Angle Evaluation — NCERT Exemplar):

Find the value of $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{8}\right)$, $\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{8}\right)$, and $\tan\left(\dfrac{\pi}{8}\right)$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Let $x = \dfrac{\pi}{8}$, then $2x = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

Step 1: Finding $\sin(\pi/8)$
Using the power reduction formula $\sin^2 x = \dfrac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}$:

$$ \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) = \frac{1 - \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}{2} = \frac{1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{2} - 1}{2\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2 - \sqrt{2}}{4} $$

Since $\dfrac{\pi}{8} \in \left(0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$ (Quadrant I), $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{8}\right) > 0$:

$$ \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}}}{2} $$

Step 2: Finding $\cos(\pi/8)$
Using $\cos^2 x = \dfrac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}$:

$$ \cos^2\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) = \frac{1 + \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)}{2} = \frac{1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}{2} = \frac{\sqrt{2} + 1}{2\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2 + \sqrt{2}}{4} $$

Since $\dfrac{\pi}{8}$ is in Quadrant I, $\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{8}\right) > 0$:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}}}{2} $$

Step 3: Finding $\tan(\pi/8)$

$$ \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) = \frac{\sin(\pi/8)}{\cos(\pi/8)} = \frac{\sqrt{2 - \sqrt{2}}}{\sqrt{2 + \sqrt{2}}} $$

Alternative cleaner method using half-angle tangent:

$$ \tan x = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{\sin 2x} \implies \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{8}\right) = \frac{1 - \cos(\pi/4)}{\sin(\pi/4)} = \frac{1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}} = \sqrt{2} - 1 $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap 1: The Negative Sign in $\cos C - \cos D$

  • The Error: Students frequently write:

$$ \cos C - \cos D = 2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) \quad \text{--- [FATAL ERROR]} $$

  • Correct Formulation:

$$ \cos C - \cos D = -2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) = 2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{D-C}{2}\right) $$

  • Examiner Tip: When calculating $\cos 20^\circ - \cos 80^\circ$, using the positive form requires flipping the inside argument: $2\sin 50^\circ \sin(80^\circ - 20^\circ)/2 = 2\sin 50^\circ \sin 30^\circ$.

Trap 2: Resolving the Sign for Half Angles $\sqrt{1 \pm \sin 2x}$

  • The Error: Assuming $\sqrt{\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x \pm 2\sin x \cos x} = \sin x \pm \cos x$.
  • Correct Principle: $\sqrt{z^2} = |z|$.

$$ \sqrt{1 + \sin 2x} = |\sin x + \cos x| $$

$$ \sqrt{1 - \sin 2x} = |\sin x - \cos x| $$

  • Case in Point: If $x \in \left(\dfrac{\pi}{4}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$, $\sin x > \cos x \implies |\sin x - \cos x| = \sin x - \cos x$.
    However, if $x \in \left(0, \dfrac{\pi}{4}\right)$, $\cos x > \sin x \implies |\sin x - \cos x| = \cos x - \sin x$. Dropping the modulus results in an immediate loss of marks.

Trap 3: The Tangent Double-Angle Denominator Sign

  • The Error: Conflating $\sin 2x$ and $\tan 2x$ tangent forms:

$$ \tan 2x \neq \frac{2\tan x}{1 + \tan^2 x} $$

  • Correction:
  • Plus sign in denominator $\to \sin 2x = \dfrac{2\tan x}{1 + \tan^2 x}$
  • Minus sign in denominator $\to \tan 2x = \dfrac{2\tan x}{1 - \tan^2 x}$

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Competitive & Board Exams

Golden Point 1: Symmetric Triple Products

The following three identities appear regularly in CBSE board exams, JEE Main, and NDA:

$$ \sin x \sin(60^\circ - x) \sin(60^\circ + x) = \frac{1}{4}\sin 3x $$

$$ \cos x \cos(60^\circ - x) \cos(60^\circ + x) = \frac{1}{4}\cos 3x $$

$$ \tan x \tan(60^\circ - x) \tan(60^\circ + x) = \tan 3x $$

Instant Verification (for $x = 20^\circ$):

$$ \cos 20^\circ \cos(60^\circ - 20^\circ) \cos(60^\circ + 20^\circ) = \cos 20^\circ \cos 40^\circ \cos 80^\circ = \frac{1}{4}\cos(3 \times 20^\circ) = \frac{1}{4}\cos 60^\circ = \frac{1}{8} $$

Directly solves Problem 1 in 5 seconds for multiple-choice questions!

Golden Point 2: Telescoping Cosine Product (Binary Argument Doubling)

For a product of cosines where each successive angle doubles:

$$ \prod_{k=0}^{n-1} \cos(2^k \theta) = \cos \theta \cos 2\theta \cos 4\theta \dots \cos(2^{n-1}\theta) = \frac{\sin(2^n \theta)}{2^n \sin \theta} $$

(Provided $\sin \theta \neq 0$).

Application:

Calculate $\cos\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{7}\right)\cos\left(\dfrac{4\pi}{7}\right)\cos\left(\dfrac{8\pi}{7}\right)$: - Here $\theta = \dfrac{2\pi}{7}$, $n = 3$:

$$ \text{Product} = \frac{\sin\left(2^3 \cdot \dfrac{2\pi}{7}\right)}{2^3 \sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{7}\right)} = \frac{\sin\left(\dfrac{16\pi}{7}\right)}{8\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{7}\right)} = \frac{\sin\left(2\pi + \dfrac{2\pi}{7}\right)}{8\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{7}\right)} = \frac{\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{7}\right)}{8\sin\left(\dfrac{2\pi}{7}\right)} = \frac{1}{8} $$

Golden Point 3: Half-Angle Tangent Substitution ("$t$-Substitution")

Set $t = \tan\left(\dfrac{x}{2}\right)$:

$$ \sin x = \frac{2t}{1 + t^2}, \quad \cos x = \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2}, \quad \tan x = \frac{2t}{1 - t^2} $$

Pedagogical Significance: This rationalizes any linear combination of trigonometric functions, serving as the foundation for the Weierstrass substitution in Class 12 integral calculus.

$$ y_2(t) = 4\sin\left(100\pi t - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) $$

$$ Y(t) = y_1(t) + y_2(t) $$

$$ \left[\text{Final Evaluation: } \frac{1}{2} \text{ Mark}\right] $$

Question A.3: Assertion-Reason Conceptual Trap (Sign of Trigonometric Radian Measures)

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, Assertion-Reason] [1 Mark]</span>
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Assertion (A): $\sin(1) > \cos(1)$, where the angle is measured in radians.
Reason (R): In the interval $\left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, $\sin\theta$ is a strictly increasing function and $\cos\theta$ is a strictly decreasing function, with $\sin\theta = \cos\theta$ occurring at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Wise Marking Scheme:

  • Analysis of Reason (R):
    In the first quadrant $\left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, as angle $\theta$ increases:

$$ \frac{d}{d\theta}(\sin\theta) = \cos\theta > 0 \implies \sin\theta \text{ is strictly increasing} $$

$$ \frac{d}{d\theta}(\cos\theta) = -\sin\theta < 0 \implies \cos\theta \text{ is strictly decreasing} $$

At $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$ radians, $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$. Hence, (R) is true. $\left[\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}\right]$

  • Analysis of Assertion (A):
    Recall that $\pi \approx 3.14159$, so:

$$ \frac{\pi}{4} \approx \frac{3.14159}{4} \approx 0.7854\text{ radians} $$

Since $1\text{ rad} > 0.7854\text{ rad} = \frac{\pi}{4}\text{ rad}$, and $1 < \frac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.5708\text{ rad}$, the angle $\theta = 1\text{ rad}$ lies in the interval $\left(\frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.
For all $\theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, $\sin\theta > \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) > \cos\theta$.
Therefore, $\sin(1) > \cos(1)$ holds true, and (R) provides the complete explanation.
Correct Option: (a)* $\left[\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}\right]$

Question A.5: HOTS Multi-Concept Problem (Telescoping Sum of Tangent Products)

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, HOTS] [3 Marks]</span>
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Problem Statement:

Prove analytically that for any positive integer $n$ and any real angle $x$ such that no denominator vanishes:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n} \tan(kx)\tan((k+1)x) = \frac{\tan((n+1)x) - \tan x}{\tan x} - n $$

Hence, evaluate the exact closed-form value of:

$$ \tan x \tan 2x + \tan 2x \tan 3x + \tan 3x \tan 4x $$

when $x = \frac{\pi}{10}$.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Wise Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Express the general product term using the tangent subtraction formula.
    Consider the identity for the tangent of a difference:

$$ \tan(A - B) = \frac{\tan A - \tan B}{1 + \tan A \tan B} $$

Rearranging for $1 + \tan A \tan B$:

$$ 1 + \tan A \tan B = \frac{\tan A - \tan B}{\tan(A - B)} $$

$$ \tan A \tan B = \frac{\tan A - \tan B}{\tan(A - B)} - 1 $$

$$ \left[\text{Identity Derivation: } 1\text{ Mark}\right] $$

  • Step 2: Apply to the general summand.
    Let $A = (k+1)x$ and $B = kx$. Then $A - B = x$.

$$ \tan((k+1)x)\tan(kx) = \frac{\tan((k+1)x) - \tan(kx)}{\tan x} - 1 $$

$$ \left[\text{Substitution: } \frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}\right] $$

  • Step 3: Sum the telescoping series from $k=1$ to $n$.

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n} \tan((k+1)x)\tan(kx) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left[ \frac{\tan((k+1)x) - \tan(kx)}{\tan x} - 1 \right] $$

$$ = \frac{1}{\tan x}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left[\tan((k+1)x) - \tan(kx)\right] - \sum_{k=1}^{n} 1 $$

The summation telescopes:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n} [\tan((k+1)x) - \tan(kx)] = [\tan 2x - \tan x] + [\tan 3x - \tan 2x] + \dots + [\tan((n+1)x) - \tan(nx)] $$

$$ = \tan((n+1)x) - \tan x $$

Therefore:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{n} \tan(kx)\tan((k+1)x) = \frac{\tan((n+1)x) - \tan x}{\tan x} - n $$

$$ \left[\text{Telescoping Evaluation and Proof: } 1\text{ Mark}\right] $$

  • Step 4: Evaluate for $n = 3$ and $x = \frac{\pi}{10}$.
    Using the derived formula with $n = 3$:

$$ \sum_{k=1}^{3} \tan(kx)\tan((k+1)x) = \frac{\tan(4x) - \tan x}{\tan x} - 3 = \frac{\tan(4x)}{\tan x} - 1 - 3 = \frac{\tan(4x)}{\tan x} - 4 $$

Substitute $x = \frac{\pi}{10}$:

$$ 4x = \frac{4\pi}{10} = \frac{2\pi}{5} $$

Notice that $x + 4x = \frac{\pi}{10} + \frac{4\pi}{10} = \frac{5\pi}{10} = \frac{\pi}{2}$.
Thus, $4x = \frac{\pi}{2} - x$, which implies:

$$ \tan(4x) = \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cot x = \frac{1}{\tan x} $$

Therefore:

$$ \frac{\tan(4x)}{\tan x} = \frac{\cot x}{\tan x} = \frac{1}{\tan^2 x} = \cot^2 x = \cot^2\left(\frac{\pi}{10}\right) $$

Since $\frac{\pi}{10} = 18^\circ$, and $\cot(18^\circ) = \sqrt{5 + 2\sqrt{5}}$:

$$ \cot^2\left(\frac{\pi}{10}\right) = 5 + 2\sqrt{5} $$

Subtracting 4:

$$ \text{Value} = (5 + 2\sqrt{5}) - 4 = 1 + 2\sqrt{5} $$

$$ \left[\text{Accurate Evaluation: } \frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}\right] $$

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Wise Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Write $3x$ as a compound angle of $2x$ and $x$.

$$ 3x = 2x + x $$

$$ \cot(3x) = \cot(2x + x) $$

$$ \cot(A + B) = \frac{\cot A \cot B - 1}{\cot B + \cot A} $$

$$ 1 = \cot 2x \cot x - \cot 3x \cot 2x - \cot 3x \cot x $$

Rearranging into symmetric order:

$$ \cot x \cot 2x - \cot 2x \cot 3x - \cot 3x \cot x = 1 $$

Hence Proved. $\left[\text{Final Transposition: } \frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}\right]$

Question C.1: Multi-Step Trigonometric Product Evaluation

Problem Statement:

Prove without using trigonometric tables that:

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Wise Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Set up the product and apply the sine double-angle identity.
    Let:

$$ P = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{8\pi}{5}\right) $$

Multiply and divide by $2\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right) \neq 0$:

$$ P = \frac{1}{2\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right)} \left[ 2\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right) \right] \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{8\pi}{5}\right) $$

$$ P = \frac{1}{4\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right)} \left[ 2\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right) \right] \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{8\pi}{5}\right) $$

$$ = \frac{1}{8\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right)} \sin\left(\frac{8\pi}{5}\right)\cos\left(\frac{8\pi}{5}\right) $$

$$ \left[\text{Iterative Doubling Steps: } 1.5\text{ Marks}\right] $$

Hence Proved. $\left[\text{Angle Reduction and Final Cancellation: } 1\text{ Mark}\right]$

Question C.2: Algebraic Elimination of Trigonometric Parameters

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar Analytical, 5 Marks]</span>
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem Statement:

If $\sin x + \sin y = a$ and $\cos x + \cos y = b$, where $a, b \neq 0$, prove that: 1. $\cos(x - y) = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - 2}{2}$ 2. $\sin(x + y) = \frac{2ab}{a^2 + b^2}$ 3. $\cos(x + y) = \frac{b^2 - a^2}{a^2 + b^2}$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Wise Marking Scheme:

  • Part 1: Derivation of $\cos(x - y)$
    Given:

$$ \sin x + \sin y = a \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

$$ \cos x + \cos y = b \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Squaring both equations:

$$ \sin^2 x + \sin^2 y + 2\sin x \sin y = a^2 $$

$$ \cos^2 x + \cos^2 y + 2\cos x \cos y = b^2 $$

Adding these equations:

$$ (\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x) + (\sin^2 y + \cos^2 y) + 2(\cos x \cos y + \sin x \sin y) = a^2 + b^2 $$

Using $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ and $\cos(x - y) = \cos x \cos y + \sin x \sin y$:

$$ 1 + 1 + 2\cos(x - y) = a^2 + b^2 $$

$$ 2 + 2\cos(x - y) = a^2 + b^2 \implies 2\cos(x - y) = a^2 + b^2 - 2 $$

$$ \cos(x - y) = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - 2}{2} $$

$$ \left[\text{Proof of Part 1: } 1.5\text{ Marks}\right] $$

  • Part 2: Alternative expansion using sum-to-product identities
    Apply sum-to-product formulas to equations (1) and (2):

$$ a = 2\sin\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) \quad \text{--- (3)} $$

$$ b = 2\cos\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) \quad \text{--- (4)} $$

Dividing (3) by (4) (since $b \neq 0$ and $\cos\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) \neq 0$):

$$ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{2\sin\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)}{2\cos\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)} = \tan\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right) $$

Let $T = \tan\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right) = \frac{a}{b}$.

$$ \left[\text{Derivation of } \tan\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right) = \frac{a}{b}: 1.5\text{ Marks}\right] $$

  • Part 3: Derivation of $\sin(x + y)$ and $\cos(x + y)$
    Using the half-angle identities for $\sin(2\theta)$ and $\cos(2\theta)$ where $\theta = \frac{x+y}{2}$:

For $\sin(x+y)$:

$$ \sin(x+y) = \frac{2\tan\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{1 + \tan^2\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)} = \frac{2(a/b)}{1 + (a/b)^2} = \frac{2a/b}{(b^2 + a^2)/b^2} = \frac{2ab}{a^2 + b^2} $$

$$ \left[\text{Proof of Part 2: } 1\text{ Mark}\right] $$

For $\cos(x+y)$:

$$ \cos(x+y) = \frac{1 - \tan^2\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)}{1 + \tan^2\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)} = \frac{1 - (a/b)^2}{1 + (a/b)^2} = \frac{(b^2 - a^2)/b^2}{(b^2 + a^2)/b^2} = \frac{b^2 - a^2}{a^2 + b^2} $$

Hence Proved. $\left[\text{Proof of Part 3: } 1\text{ Mark}\right]$

Question C.3: Constrained Triangle Trigonometry (Conditional Identity)

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar Subjective Analytical, 4 Marks]</span>
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Problem Statement:

If $A, B, C$ are the interior angles of a non-right-angled triangle $\Delta ABC$ (so $A + B + C = \pi$), prove that:

$$ \tan A + \tan B + \tan C = \tan A \tan B \tan C $$

Hence, determine the value of $\cot A \cot B + \cot B \cot C + \cot C \cot A$.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Wise Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Set up the angle relationship.
    Given $A + B + C = \pi$, we have:

$$ A + B = \pi - C $$

Taking the tangent on both sides:

$$ \tan(A + B) = \tan(\pi - C) = -\tan C $$

$$ \left[\text{Relation Setup: } 1\text{ Mark}\right] $$

  • Step 2: Expand using the compound angle identity.

$$ \frac{\tan A + \tan B}{1 - \tan A \tan B} = -\tan C $$

Cross-multiplying:

$$ \tan A + \tan B = -\tan C(1 - \tan A \tan B) $$

$$ \tan A + \tan B = -\tan C + \tan A \tan B \tan C $$

Rearranging:

$$ \tan A + \tan B + \tan C = \tan A \tan B \tan C $$

Hence Proved. $\left[\text{Expansion & First Identity: } 1.5\text{ Marks}\right]$

  • Step 3: Evaluate the sum of pairwise cotangent products.
    Divide both sides of $\tan A + \tan B + \tan C = \tan A \tan B \tan C$ by the non-zero product $(\tan A \tan B \tan C)$:

$$ \frac{\tan A}{\tan A \tan B \tan C} + \frac{\tan B}{\tan A \tan B \tan C} + \frac{\tan C}{\tan A \tan B \tan C} = 1 $$

$$ \frac{1}{\tan B \tan C} + \frac{1}{\tan A \tan C} + \frac{1}{\tan A \tan B} = 1 $$

Since $\cot\theta = \frac{1}{\tan\theta}$:

$$ \cot B \cot C + \cot A \cot C + \cot A \cot B = 1 $$

Rearranging:

$$ \cot A \cot B + \cot B \cot C + \cot C \cot A = 1 $$

$$ \left[\text{Algebraic Division and Evaluation: } 1.5\text{ Marks}\right] $$

Question C.4: Analytical Radical Identity Evaluation ($\sin 18^\circ$ and $\cos 36^\circ$)

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar HOTS, 4 Marks]</span>
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem Statement:

Derive the exact radical value of $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{10}\right)$ (i.e., $\sin 18^\circ$) by setting $\theta = 18^\circ$ and using the identity $2\theta = 90^\circ - 3\theta$.
Hence, show that:

$$ \cos 36^\circ - \sin 18^\circ = \frac{1}{2} $$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Wise Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Set up the angle equivalence.
    Let $\theta = 18^\circ$. Then $5\theta = 90^\circ$.
    Split into $2\theta + 3\theta = 90^\circ \implies 2\theta = 90^\circ - 3\theta$.
    Taking the sine on both sides:

$$ \sin 2\theta = \sin(90^\circ - 3\theta) = \cos 3\theta $$

$$ \left[\text{Angle Splitting: } 1\text{ Mark}\right] $$

  • Step 2: Expand using multiple-angle formulas.
    Substitute $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$:

$$ 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta $$

Since $\theta = 18^\circ$, $\cos\theta \neq 0$. Dividing both sides by $\cos\theta$:

$$ 2\sin\theta = 4\cos^2\theta - 3 $$

Using $\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta$:

$$ 2\sin\theta = 4(1 - \sin^2\theta) - 3 = 4 - 4\sin^2\theta - 3 = 1 - 4\sin^2\theta $$

Rearranging into standard quadratic form in $\sin\theta$:

$$ 4\sin^2\theta + 2\sin\theta - 1 = 0 $$

$$ \left[\text{Derivation of Quadratic Equation: } 1\text{ Mark}\right] $$

  • Step 3: Solve the quadratic equation.
    Using the quadratic formula:

$$ \sin\theta = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{2^2 - 4(4)(-1)}}{2(4)} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 16}}{8} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{20}}{8} = \frac{-2 \pm 2\sqrt{5}}{8} = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{4} $$

Since $\theta = 18^\circ$ lies in the first quadrant, $\sin 18^\circ > 0$.
Therefore, we discard the negative root:

$$ \sin 18^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{4} $$

$$ \left[\text{Root Selection and Value of } \sin 18^\circ: 1\text{ Mark}\right] $$

  • Step 4: Compute $\cos 36^\circ$ and their difference.
    Using the double-angle formula:

$$ \cos 36^\circ = \cos(2 \cdot 18^\circ) = 1 - 2\sin^2(18^\circ) $$

Substitute $\sin 18^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{4}$:

$$ \sin^2 18^\circ = \frac{(\sqrt{5} - 1)^2}{16} = \frac{5 - 2\sqrt{5} + 1}{16} = \frac{6 - 2\sqrt{5}}{16} = \frac{3 - \sqrt{5}}{8} $$

$$ \cos 36^\circ = 1 - 2\left(\frac{3 - \sqrt{5}}{8}\right) = 1 - \frac{3 - \sqrt{5}}{4} = \frac{4 - 3 + \sqrt{5}}{4} = \frac{\sqrt{5} + 1}{4} $$

Now calculate the difference:

$$ \cos 36^\circ - \sin 18^\circ = \frac{\sqrt{5} + 1}{4} - \frac{\sqrt{5} - 1}{4} = \frac{(\sqrt{5} + 1) - (\sqrt{5} - 1)}{4} = \frac{2}{4} = \frac{1}{2} $$

Hence Proved. $\left[\text{Evaluation of } \cos 36^\circ \text{ and Final Difference: } 1\text{ Mark}\right]$

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 The Auxiliary Argument Shortcut ($A\cos x + B\sin x$)

Whenever confronted with expressions of the linear combination form $f(x) = A\cos x + B\sin x$: * Extreme Value Direct Write:

$$ \text{Range} = \left[-\sqrt{A^2 + B^2}, \, \sqrt{A^2 + B^2}\right] $$

  • Phase Collapse:

$$ A\cos x + B\sin x = \sqrt{A^2+B^2} \sin(x + \phi) = \sqrt{A^2+B^2} \cos(x - \theta) $$

where $\tan\phi = \frac{A}{B}$ and $\tan\theta = \frac{B}{A}$.
Application:* Solving equations like $\sqrt{3}\sin x + \cos x = \sqrt{2}$. Immediately divide by $\sqrt{(\sqrt{3})^2 + 1^2} = 2$ to collapse to $\sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$. Saves at least 4 lines of algebraic manipulation.

1.2 The Geometric Progression (GP) Cosine Product Shortcut

For products of cosines with doubling angles:

$$ \prod_{r=0}^{n-1} \cos(2^r \theta) = \cos\theta \cdot \cos(2\theta) \cdot \cos(4\theta) \cdots \cos(2^{n-1}\theta) = \frac{\sin(2^n \theta)}{2^n \sin\theta} \quad (\sin\theta \neq 0) $$

  • Example: Find the value of $\cos 20^\circ \cos 40^\circ \cos 80^\circ$.

$$ \text{Here } \theta = 20^\circ, \, n=3 \implies \frac{\sin(2^3 \cdot 20^\circ)}{2^3 \sin 20^\circ} = \frac{\sin 160^\circ}{8 \sin 20^\circ} = \frac{\sin(180^\circ - 20^\circ)}{8 \sin 20^\circ} = \frac{\sin 20^\circ}{8 \sin 20^\circ} = \frac{1}{8} $$

(Eliminates the tedious step-by-step multiplication and division by $2\sin 20^\circ$).

1.3 The Symmetric Difference Product Identity (Triple-Angle Triplets)

Standard shortcuts for rapid evaluation of specific $60^\circ \pm \theta$ products:

$$ \sin\theta \cdot \sin(60^\circ - \theta) \cdot \sin(60^\circ + \theta) = \frac{1}{4}\sin(3\theta) $$

$$ \cos\theta \cdot \cos(60^\circ - \theta) \cdot \cos(60^\circ + \theta) = \frac{1}{4}\cos(3\theta) $$

$$ \tan\theta \cdot \tan(60^\circ - \theta) \cdot \tan(60^\circ + \theta) = \tan(3\theta) $$

  • Usage: For evaluating $\sin 20^\circ \sin 40^\circ \sin 60^\circ \sin 80^\circ$:

$$ \sin 60^\circ \cdot \left[\sin 20^\circ \sin(60^\circ - 20^\circ) \sin(60^\circ + 20^\circ)\right] = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot \left[\frac{1}{4}\sin(3 \times 20^\circ)\right] = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{8} = \frac{3}{16} $$

1.4 The Componendo & Dividendo (C&D) Trigger

Whenever an equation contains ratios of linear trigonometric terms, e.g., $\frac{\sin(A+B)}{\sin(A-B)} = \frac{p}{q}$ or $m\sin(\alpha + \beta) = n\sin(\alpha - \beta)$: * Rule: If $\frac{a}{b} = \frac{c}{d}$, then $\frac{a+b}{a-b} = \frac{c+d}{c-d}$. * Applying C&D directly converts sums and differences of trigonometric functions into products via factorisation formulas:

$$ \frac{\sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B)}{\sin(A+B) - \sin(A-B)} = \frac{2\sin A \cos B}{2\cos A \sin B} = \tan A \cot B = \frac{p+q}{p-q} $$

This avoids lengthy expansion and cross-multiplication.

1.5 Half-Angle Rational Parameterisation (Weierstrass Substitution)

To quickly express any trigonometric function in terms of $t = \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$:

$$ \sin x = \frac{2t}{1+t^2}, \quad \cos x = \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}, \quad \tan x = \frac{2t}{1-t^2} $$

  • Transforms trigonometric equations into single-variable polynomials in $t$.

1.6 Sum of Sines & Cosines with Angles in Arithmetic Progression (AP)

For $n$ terms with common difference $\beta$:

$$ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \sin(\alpha + k\beta) = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{n\beta}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)} \cdot \sin\left(\alpha + \frac{(n-1)\beta}{2}\right) $$

$$ \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \cos(\alpha + k\beta) = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{n\beta}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)} \cdot \cos\left(\alpha + \frac{(n-1)\beta}{2}\right) $$

  • Mental Rule: $\frac{\sin(\text{Number of terms} \times \frac{\text{diff}}{2})}{\sin(\frac{\text{diff}}{2})} \times \sin/\cos\left(\frac{\text{First angle} + \text{Last angle}}{2}\right)$.

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

2.1 The Square-Root Modulus Trap: $\sqrt{1 - \cos 2x}$ and $\sqrt{1 + \sin 2x}$

  • Examiner Penalty: $-1$ mark instantly.
  • The Error: Students write $\sqrt{1 - \cos 2x} = \sqrt{2\sin^2 x} = \sqrt{2}\sin x$.
  • Correct Strict Rule:

$$ \sqrt{x^2} = |x| \implies \sqrt{2\sin^2 x} = \sqrt{2}|\sin x| $$

  • If $x \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right)$ (Quadrant II), $\sin x > 0$, so $|\sin x| = \sin x$.
    However, if $x \in \left(\pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}\right)$ (Quadrant III), $\sin x < 0 \implies \sqrt{2\sin^2 x} = -\sqrt{2}\sin x$.
  • The Classic $\sqrt{1+\sin 2x}$ Trap:

$$ \sqrt{1 + \sin 2x} = \sqrt{(\cos x + \sin x)^2} = |\cos x + \sin x| $$

If $x \in \left(\frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, $\sin x > \cos x$, so $\sin x - \cos x > 0$. When working with $\sqrt{1-\sin 2x} = |\cos x - \sin x|$, writing $\cos x - \sin x$ instead of $\sin x - \cos x$ in that interval yields a negative value for an arithmetic square root, causing an automatic deduction of marks.

2.2 Quadrant Sign Loss during Half-Angle Extraction

  • Context: Given $\cos x = -\frac{1}{3}$, where $x \in \text{Quadrant III}$ ($\pi < x < \frac{3\pi}{2}$). Find $\sin\frac{x}{2}, \cos\frac{x}{2}, \tan\frac{x}{2}$.
  • The Error: Students use $\cos\frac{x}{2} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1+\cos x}{2}}$ and blindly pick $+$ or leave the $\pm$ unresolved.
  • Evaluation Scheme Requirement:
  • Explicit step defining the half-angle domain:

$$ \pi < x < \frac{3\pi}{2} \implies \frac{\pi}{2} < \frac{x}{2} < \frac{3\pi}{4} $$

  1. Conclusion of Quadrant: State clearly that $\frac{x}{2}$ lies in Quadrant II.
  2. Deduce signs before substitution:

$$ \sin\frac{x}{2} > 0, \quad \cos\frac{x}{2} < 0, \quad \tan\frac{x}{2} < 0 $$

Omitting the explicit quadrant check costs $\frac{1}{2}$ mark even if the final numerical values happen to be right.

2.3 Division by Zero & Cancelling Variable Factors

  • The Error: Solving $\sin 2x = \sin x \implies 2\sin x \cos x = \sin x$. Students cancel $\sin x$ from both sides, arriving at $2\cos x = 1 \implies \cos x = \frac{1}{2}$.
  • Deduction: $-1\frac{1}{2}$ marks (Loss of roots).
  • Mandatory Procedure:

$$ 2\sin x \cos x - \sin x = 0 \implies \sin x(2\cos x - 1) = 0 $$

$$ \implies \sin x = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \cos x = \frac{1}{2} $$

2.4 Extraneous Roots from Squaring Both Sides

  • When solving equations like $\sin x + \cos x = 1$, squaring both sides yields $1 + \sin 2x = 1 \implies \sin 2x = 0$.
  • This introduces spurious solutions (e.g., points where $\sin x + \cos x = -1$).
  • Examiner Instruction: If squaring is employed, the candidate must verify solutions in the original equation or write down the domain constraint $\sin x + \cos x \ge 0$. Failure to check and discard extraneous roots costs up to $1$ full mark.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

3.1 Strict Domains of Natural Trigonometric Functions

Every candidate must know the non-permissible values where functions are undefined: * $\tan x$ and $\sec x$ are undefined for $x = (2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{2}, \, n \in \mathbb{Z}$ (odd multiples of $\frac{\pi}{2}$). * $\cot x$ and $\csc x$ are undefined for $x = n\pi, \, n \in \mathbb{Z}$ (integral multiples of $\pi$). * Identity Trap: The identity $1 + \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x$ does not hold over all real numbers; it is valid strictly on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{(2n+1)\frac{\pi}{2} : n \in \mathbb{Z}\right\}$.

3.2 Fundamental Squeeze Principle / Limits Foundation (NCERT Appendix)

For $0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$ \sin x < x < \tan x $$

  • Inverting and scaling:

$$ \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1 $$

  • This geometric inequality (proved via sectors and triangles of the unit circle) is the cornerstone for all calculus involving circular functions.

3.3 Angle Measure Conversion Rigour

$$ 1\text{ radian} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57^\circ 16' 22'' $$

$$ 1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180}\text{ radians} \approx 0.01746\text{ radians} $$

  • NCERT Specification: If no unit is mentioned, the angle is always assumed to be in radians.
  • In the arc-length formula:

$$ l = r\theta $$

$\theta$ must strictly be in radians. Using $\theta$ in degrees without conversion leads to an immediate zero for that step.

3.4 Periodicity & Parity Matrix

  • Odd Functions: $f(-x) = -f(x)$

$$ \sin(-x) = -\sin x, \quad \csc(-x) = -\csc x, \quad \tan(-x) = -\tan x, \quad \cot(-x) = -\cot x $$

  • Even Functions: $f(-x) = f(x)$

$$ \cos(-x) = \cos x, \quad \sec(-x) = \sec x $$

  • Fundamental Periods:
  • $\sin x, \cos x, \sec x, \csc x \implies 2\pi$
  • $\tan x, \cot x \implies \pi$
  • $|\sin x|, |\cos x|, |\tan x|, |\cot x|, |\sec x|, |\csc x| \implies \pi$

Trigonometric Equations: Principal and General Solutions

Target Level: CBSE Class 11 Mathematics / JEE Main & Advanced Foundation
Reference Syllabus: NCERT Mathematics Class 11 — Chapter: Trigonometric Functions

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Definition of a Trigonometric Equation

An equation involving one or more trigonometric functions of an unknown angle $x$ (or $\theta$) is called a trigonometric equation.

$$ \text{Examples: } \sin x = \frac{1}{2}, \quad \cos 2x - 3\cos x + 2 = 0, \quad \tan^2 x + \sqrt{3}\tan x = 0 $$

Unlike trigonometric identities (which hold true for all valid values in the domain of the variable, e.g., $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$), a trigonometric equation is satisfied only for specific, discrete values of the unknown variable.

1.2 The Periodicity Principle & Multiplicity of Roots

Because trigonometric functions are periodic: - $\sin(x + 2k\pi) = \sin x, \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{Z}$ - $\cos(x + 2k\pi) = \cos x, \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{Z}$ - $\tan(x + k\pi) = \tan x, \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{Z}$

If a trigonometric equation has one solution, it inherently possesses infinitely many solutions generated by moving forward or backward in intervals of the function's fundamental period across the real number line.

1.3 Principal Solutions (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Definition (NCERT):
The solutions of a trigonometric equation for which the unknown variable $x$ lies in the interval $[0, 2\pi)$ (or $0^\circ \le x < 360^\circ$) are called its Principal Solutions.

Geometric & Quadrant Mechanics

Since each standard trigonometric ratio is positive in two quadrants and negative in two quadrants within $[0, 2\pi)$, a basic equation $\sin x = c$, $\cos x = c$, or $\tan x = c$ (where $|c| \le 1$ for sine/cosine, and $c \in \mathbb{R}$ for tangent, $c \neq 0$) yields exactly two principal solutions:

  1. Let $\alpha \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ be the acute reference angle such that $f(\alpha) = |c|$.
  2. Determine the two quadrants where $f(x)$ matches the sign of $c$:
  3. Quadrant I: $x = \alpha$
  4. Quadrant II: $x = \pi - \alpha$
  5. Quadrant III: $x = \pi + \alpha$
  6. Quadrant IV: $x = 2\pi - \alpha$

Crucial Note on Interval Boundaries:
The interval for principal solutions is strictly $[0, 2\pi)$. Hence: - $x = 0$ is included, but $x = 2\pi$ is excluded. - Negative angles (e.g., $-\frac{\pi}{6}$) are not principal solutions, even if they represent the same terminal ray.

1.4 General Solutions (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Definition (NCERT):
An expression involving an integer parameter $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ which gives all possible solutions of a trigonometric equation is called its General Solution.

The set of all integers is denoted by $\mathbb{Z} = \{ \dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots \}$.

2. Fundamental Theorems & Complete Step-by-Step Proofs

Theorem 1: Roots of $\sin x = 0$, $\cos x = 0$, and $\tan x = 0$ (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement 1.1: $\sin x = 0 \iff x = n\pi, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}$

  • Given: $\sin x = 0$.
  • To Prove: $x = n\pi$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • Proof:
    Consider a unit circle centered at the origin $(0,0)$. Any point $P$ on the circle corresponding to an angle $x$ has coordinates $(\cos x, \sin x)$.

$$ \sin x = 0 \iff \text{the } y\text{-coordinate of point } P \text{ is } 0 $$

The point $P$ lies on the $x$-axis. The terminal side intersects the $x$-axis at $(1, 0)$ when $x = 0, \pm 2\pi, \pm 4\pi, \dots$ and at $(-1, 0)$ when $x = \pm \pi, \pm 3\pi, \dots$.
Combining both sets of terminal rays:

$$ x \in \{0, \pm\pi, \pm 2\pi, \pm 3\pi, \dots\} $$

$$ x = n\pi, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \blacksquare $$

Statement 1.2: $\cos x = 0 \iff x = (2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{2}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}$

  • Given: $\cos x = 0$.
  • To Prove: $x = (2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{2}$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • Proof:
    On the unit circle, $\cos x = 0$ means the $x$-coordinate of $P(\cos x, \sin x)$ is zero.
    This occurs precisely when $P$ lies on the $y$-axis: at $(0, 1)$ for $x = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}{2}, \dots$ and at $(0, -1)$ for $x = \frac{3\pi}{2}, \frac{7\pi}{2}, \dots$.
    The angles are all odd multiples of $\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$ x = \dots, -\frac{3\pi}{2}, -\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \dots $$

$$ x = (2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{2}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \blacksquare $$

Statement 1.3: $\tan x = 0 \iff x = n\pi, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}$

  • Proof:

$$ \tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = 0 \iff \sin x = 0 \text{ and } \cos x \neq 0 $$

Since $\cos(n\pi) = (-1)^n \neq 0$, the condition reduces purely to $\sin x = 0$.
Therefore, $x = n\pi, \; n \in \mathbb{Z}$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2: General Solution of $\sin x = \sin y$ (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:
For any real numbers $x$ and $y$:

$$ \sin x = \sin y \implies x = n\pi + (-1)^n y, \quad \text{where } n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

  • Given: $\sin x = \sin y$.
  • To Prove: $x = n\pi + (-1)^n y$ for some $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • Proof:

$$ \sin x - \sin y = 0 $$

Applying the sum-to-product transformation identity $2\cos\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) = 0$:

$$ 2\cos\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) = 0 $$

This yields two mutually exclusive branches:

$$ Case 1: \cos\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right) = 0 $$

By Theorem 1.2:

$$ \frac{x+y}{2} = (2k + 1)\frac{\pi}{2}, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z} $$

Multiplying both sides by $2$:

$$ x + y = (2k + 1)\pi \implies x = (2k + 1)\pi - y $$

Notice that $-y = (-1)^{2k+1} y$ because $2k+1$ is odd. Hence:

$$ x = (2k + 1)\pi + (-1)^{2k + 1}y \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)} $$

$$ Case 2: \sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) = 0 $$

By Theorem 1.1:

$$ \frac{x-y}{2} = k\pi, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z} $$

Multiplying both sides by $2$:

$$ x - y = 2k\pi \implies x = 2k\pi + y $$

Notice that $+y = (-1)^{2k} y$ because $2k$ is even. Hence:

$$ x = 2k\pi + (-1)^{2k}y \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)} $$

Synthesizing Cases 1 and 2:
Every integer $n$ is either even ($n = 2k$) or odd ($n = 2k + 1$). Combining Equations (1) and (2):

$$ x = n\pi + (-1)^n y, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 3: General Solution of $\cos x = \cos y$ (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:
For any real numbers $x$ and $y$:

$$ \cos x = \cos y \implies x = 2n\pi \pm y, \quad \text{where } n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

  • Given: $\cos x = \cos y$.
  • To Prove: $x = 2n\pi \pm y$ for some $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • Proof:

$$ \cos x - \cos y = 0 $$

Applying the transformation identity $-2\sin\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) = 0$:

$$ \sin\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) = 0 $$

$$ Case 1: \sin\left(\frac{x+y}{2}\right) = 0 $$

$$ \frac{x+y}{2} = n\pi, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \implies x + y = 2n\pi \implies x = 2n\pi - y $$

$$ Case 2: \sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) = 0 $$

$$ \frac{x-y}{2} = n\pi, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \implies x - y = 2n\pi \implies x = 2n\pi + y $$

Combining both cases:

$$ x = 2n\pi \pm y, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 4: General Solution of $\tan x = \tan y$ (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:
If neither $x$ nor $y$ is an odd multiple of $\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$ \tan x = \tan y \implies x = n\pi + y, \quad \text{where } n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

  • Given: $\tan x = \tan y$, where $x, y \neq (2k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}$.
  • To Prove: $x = n\pi + y$ for some $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • Proof:

$$ \tan x - \tan y = 0 \implies \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} - \frac{\sin y}{\cos y} = 0 $$

Taking common denominator:

$$ \frac{\sin x \cos y - \cos x \sin y}{\cos x \cos y} = 0 $$

Since $\cos x \neq 0$ and $\cos y \neq 0$, the numerator must be zero:

$$ \sin(x - y) = 0 $$

By Theorem 1.1:

$$ x - y = n\pi, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \implies x = n\pi + y, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 5: Quadratic General Solutions (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement:
For equations of the form $\sin^2 x = \sin^2 y$, $\cos^2 x = \cos^2 y$, or $\tan^2 x = \tan^2 y$:

$$ x = n\pi \pm y, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

  • Proof (for $\cos^2 x = \cos^2 y$):
    Using the half-angle identity $\cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2 \theta - 1 \implies \cos^2 \theta = \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}$:

$$ \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2} = \frac{1 + \cos 2y}{2} \implies \cos 2x = \cos 2y $$

By Theorem 3:

$$ 2x = 2n\pi \pm 2y \implies x = n\pi \pm y, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} \quad \blacksquare $$

(Note: The exact same unified formula holds for $\sin^2 x = \sin^2 y$ and $\tan^2 x = \tan^2 y$ via double-angle transformations).

3. High-Yield Examples & Exemplar Application Problems

Example 1: Finding Principal Solutions

Problem: Find the principal solutions of the equation $\sin x = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Identify the reference angle:
    Find the acute angle $\alpha \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ such that:

$$ \sin \alpha = \left|-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right| = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \implies \alpha = \frac{\pi}{3} $$

  1. Locate quadrants:
    Sine is negative in Quadrant III and Quadrant IV.
  2. Compute the angles within $[0, 2\pi)$:
  3. In Quadrant III:

$$ x = \pi + \alpha = \pi + \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{4\pi}{3} $$

  • In Quadrant IV:

$$ x = 2\pi - \alpha = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{5\pi}{3} $$

Final Answer: The principal solutions are $x = \frac{4\pi}{3}$ and $x = \frac{5\pi}{3}$.

Example 2: Factoring via Trigonometric Transformations

Problem: Solve the general equation $\cos 3x + \cos x - \cos 2x = 0$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Group terms to apply sum-to-product formulas:

$$ (\cos 3x + \cos x) - \cos 2x = 0 $$

  1. Apply the identity $\cos A + \cos B = 2\cos\left(\frac{A+B}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{A-B}{2}\right)$:

$$ 2\cos\left(\frac{3x + x}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{3x - x}{2}\right) - \cos 2x = 0 $$

$$ 2\cos 2x \cos x - \cos 2x = 0 $$

  1. Factor out the common term:

$$ \cos 2x(2\cos x - 1) = 0 $$

  1. Solve each factor independently:
  2. Factor 1: $\cos 2x = 0$

$$ 2x = (2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{2} \implies x = (2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{4}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

  • Factor 2: $2\cos x - 1 = 0 \implies \cos x = \frac{1}{2}$
    Since $\cos \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$, write:

$$ \cos x = \cos \frac{\pi}{3} \implies x = 2k\pi \pm \frac{\pi}{3}, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z} $$

Final Answer:

$$ x = (2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{4} \quad \text{or} \quad x = 2k\pi \pm \frac{\pi}{3}, \quad \text{where } n, k \in \mathbb{Z} $$

Example 3: The Auxiliary Angle Method ($a\cos x + b\sin x = c$)

Problem: Find the general solution of $\sqrt{3}\cos x + \sin x = \sqrt{2}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Normalize the coefficients:
    Here, $a = \sqrt{3}, b = 1$. Compute $r = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{(\sqrt{3})^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{3 + 1} = 2$.
    Divide the entire equation by $r = 2$:

$$ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\cos x + \frac{1}{2}\sin x = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} $$

  1. Rewrite as a compound cosine formula:
    Note that $\cos \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sin \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$. Also, $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \cos \frac{\pi}{4}$.

$$ \cos x \cos \frac{\pi}{6} + \sin x \sin \frac{\pi}{6} = \cos \frac{\pi}{4} $$

$$ \cos\left(x - \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \cos \frac{\pi}{4} $$

  1. Apply the General Solution formula for cosine:

$$ x - \frac{\pi}{6} = 2n\pi \pm \frac{\pi}{4}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

$$ x = 2n\pi + \frac{\pi}{6} \pm \frac{\pi}{4} $$

  1. Split into distinct solution families:
  2. Positive sign (+):

$$ x = 2n\pi + \left(\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = 2n\pi + \frac{5\pi}{12} $$

  • Negative sign (-):

$$ x = 2n\pi + \left(\frac{\pi}{6} - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = 2n\pi - \frac{\pi}{12} $$

Final Answer:

$$ x = 2n\pi + \frac{5\pi}{12} \quad \text{or} \quad x = 2n\pi - \frac{\pi}{12}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

Example 4: NCERT Exemplar Challenge

Problem: Solve $\tan x + \tan 2x + \sqrt{3}\tan x \tan 2x = \sqrt{3}$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

  1. Rearrange to match the addition formula for tangent:

$$ \tan x + \tan 2x = \sqrt{3} - \sqrt{3}\tan x \tan 2x $$

$$ \tan x + \tan 2x = \sqrt{3}(1 - \tan x \tan 2x) $$

  1. Divide by $(1 - \tan x \tan 2x)$:

$$ \frac{\tan x + \tan 2x}{1 - \tan x \tan 2x} = \sqrt{3} $$

(Condition: $1 - \tan x \tan 2x \neq 0$) 3. Recognize the compound angle identity:

$$ \tan(x + 2x) = \sqrt{3} \implies \tan 3x = \sqrt{3} $$

  1. Express RHS as a standard tangent value:

$$ \tan 3x = \tan \frac{\pi}{3} $$

  1. Apply the General Solution for tangent:

$$ 3x = n\pi + \frac{\pi}{3} \implies x = \frac{n\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{9}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

  1. Domain verification:
    Ensure that for these roots, neither $\tan x$ nor $\tan 2x$ becomes undefined.
    If $x = \frac{n\pi}{3} + \frac{\pi}{9}$, then $x \neq (2k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $2x \neq (2m+1)\frac{\pi}{2}$ for any integers $k, m$. All roots are valid.

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Blunders

# Pitfall / Illegal Step Mathematical Error Explanation Correct Approach
1 Canceling variable terms across equality
(e.g., $\sin 2x = \sin x \implies 2\sin x \cos x = \sin x \implies 2\cos x = 1$) Canceling $\sin x$ assumes $\sin x \neq 0$. This destroys an entire family of roots ($\sin x = 0 \implies x = n\pi$). Bring all terms to one side and factorize: $\sin x(2\cos x - 1) = 0$.
2 Squaring both sides unnecessarily
(e.g., $\sin x + \cos x = 1 \implies 1 + \sin 2x = 1$) Squaring introduces extraneous roots (solutions where $\sin x + \cos x = -1$). Use the auxiliary angle form $r\cos(x - \alpha) = c$, or check all generated solutions in the original unsquared equation.
3 Ignoring Domain Restrictions in Tangent / Secant
(e.g., $\tan 2x = \cot x$) $\tan \theta$ is undefined whenever $\theta = (2k+1)\frac{\pi}{2}$. Check every candidate solution against the domains of the original functions.
4 Writing Negative Angles as Principal Solutions
(e.g., for $\tan x = -1$, writing $x = -\frac{\pi}{4}$) The NCERT definition explicitly mandates $x \in [0, 2\pi)$. Negative values receive zero credit. Shift to the proper quadrants: $x = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4}$ and $x = 2\pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{7\pi}{4}$.
5 Misplacing the $(-1)^n$ Factor in Sine General Solution Writing $x = n\pi \pm \alpha$ for sine instead of $x = n\pi + (-1)^n \alpha$. Reserve $\pm$ strictly for cosine and square forms. Sine requires the alternating sign $(-1)^n$.
