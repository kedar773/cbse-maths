1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 The Need for the Limit Concept: The Crisis of $\frac{0}{0}$

Consider the function:

$$ f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} $$

The domain of $f(x)$ is $\mathbb{R} \setminus \{1\}$. At $x = 1$, direct substitution yields:

$$ f(1) = \frac{1^2 - 1}{1 - 1} = \frac{0}{0} $$

The symbol $\frac{0}{0}$ is indeterminate—it represents an undefined mathematical operation. However, for any $x \neq 1$, we can simplify $f(x)$ through algebraic factorization:

$$ f(x) = \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1} = x + 1 \quad (\text{since } x - 1 \neq 0) $$

Let us examine the behavior of $f(x)$ as $x$ gets arbitrarily close to $1$ from both sides:

$x < 1$ (Left Approach) $f(x) = x + 1$ $x > 1$ (Right Approach) $f(x) = x + 1$
$0.9$ $1.9$ $1.1$ $2.1$
$0.99$ $1.99$ $1.01$ $2.01$
$0.999$ $1.999$ $1.001$ $2.001$
$0.9999$ $1.9999$ $1.0001$ $2.0001$

Intuition: As $x$ approaches $1$ (denoted $x \to 1$), the value of $f(x)$ gets arbitrarily close to $2$. The actual value $f(1)$ does not exist, yet the trend or tendency of the function near $x = 1$ is unambiguously $2$.

$$ \lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = 2 $$

Core Philosophy of Calculus: The value $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ describes the behavior of $f(x)$ in the punctured neighborhood of $a$ (i.e., $0 < |x - a| < \delta$). The limit depends strictly on what happens near $a$, never on what happens at $a$.

1.2 Formal Definition and One-Sided Limits (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Left-Hand Limit (LHL)

The Left-Hand Limit of $f(x)$ as $x \to a$ is the expected value of $f(x)$ when $x$ approaches $a$ through values strictly less than $a$ ($x < a$).

$$ \text{Notation: } \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) $$

Standard Substitution Form: Let $x = a - h$, where $h > 0$ and $h \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(a - h) $$

Right-Hand Limit (RHL)

The Right-Hand Limit of $f(x)$ as $x \to a$ is the expected value of $f(x)$ when $x$ approaches $a$ through values strictly greater than $a$ ($x > a$).

$$ \text{Notation: } \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) $$

Standard Substitution Form: Let $x = a + h$, where $h > 0$ and $h \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(a + h) $$

Fundamental Criterion for Existence of a Limit (NCERT Theorem)

$$ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L $$

where $L$ is a unique, finite real number.

If: 1. $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) \neq \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$, OR 2. Either one-sided limit does not exist or becomes $\pm\infty$,

then we say $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ does not exist (DNE).

1.3 Geometric Interpretation

Consider the coordinate plane with $x = a$: 1. Approaching along the curve from the left of the vertical line $x = a$, the $y$-coordinate settles toward $L_1 = \text{LHL}$. 2. Approaching along the curve from the right of the vertical line $x = a$, the $y$-coordinate settles toward $L_2 = \text{RHL}$. 3. If the graph has a "jump" or "break" at $x = a$, then $L_1 \neq L_2$, meaning the two-sided limit fails to exist. 4. If the curve heads toward a single open hole $(a, L)$ from both sides, $L_1 = L_2 = L$, and the limit exists regardless of whether the hole is plugged at $(a, L)$, plugged elsewhere at $(a, c)$, or left undefined.

1.4 The Algebra of Limits (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $f$ and $g$ be two functions such that $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = l$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = m$, where $l, m \in \mathbb{R}$.

  1. Sum Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) + g(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) + \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = l + m $$

  1. Difference Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) - g(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) - \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = l - m $$

  1. Scalar Multiple Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} [k \cdot f(x)] = k \cdot \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = k \cdot l \quad (\forall k \in \mathbb{R}) $$

  1. Product Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) \cdot g(x)] = \left(\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\right) \cdot \left(\lim_{x \to a} g(x)\right) = l \cdot m $$

  1. Quotient Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{\lim_{x \to a} f(x)}{\lim_{x \to a} g(x)} = \frac{l}{m}, \quad \text{provided } m \neq 0 $$

  1. Power / Root Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} [f(x)]^n = \left[\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\right]^n = l^n \quad (n \in \mathbb{Q}, \text{ provided } l^n \in \mathbb{R}) $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Derivation 2.1: The Standard Algebraic Limit Formula (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1} \quad (\forall n \in \mathbb{Q}, a > 0) $$

Case 1: When $n$ is a positive integer ($n \in \mathbb{N}$)

Given: The expression $\frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a}$ with $x \neq a$.

To Prove:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1} $$

Proof: Using the standard polynomial identity for the difference of $n$-th powers:

$$ x^n - a^n = (x - a)(x^{n-1} + x^{n-2}a + x^{n-3}a^2 + \dots + x a^{n-2} + a^{n-1}) $$

Since $x \to a$, $x \neq a \implies x - a \neq 0$. Dividing the numerator and denominator by $(x - a)$:

$$ \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = x^{n-1} + x^{n-2}a + x^{n-3}a^2 + \dots + x a^{n-2} + a^{n-1} $$

Taking the limit as $x \to a$ on both sides:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = \lim_{x \to a} \left[ x^{n-1} + x^{n-2}a + x^{n-3}a^2 + \dots + x a^{n-2} + a^{n-1} \right] $$

Applying the Algebra of Limits (Sum Rule):

$$ = \lim_{x \to a} x^{n-1} + a \lim_{x \to a} x^{n-2} + a^2 \lim_{x \to a} x^{n-3} + \dots + a^{n-1} $$

$$ = a^{n-1} + a \cdot a^{n-2} + a^2 \cdot a^{n-3} + \dots + a^{n-1} $$

$$ = \underbrace{a^{n-1} + a^{n-1} + a^{n-1} + \dots + a^{n-1}}_{n \text{ terms}} $$

$$ = n a^{n-1} $$

Q.E.D.

Case 2: When $n$ is a negative integer ($n = -m$, where $m \in \mathbb{N}$)

Given:

$$ n = -m, \quad m \in \mathbb{N} $$

To Prove:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1} $$

Proof: Express the function in terms of $m$:

$$ \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = \frac{x^{-m} - a^{-m}}{x - a} = \frac{\frac{1}{x^m} - \frac{1}{a^m}}{x - a} $$

$$ = \frac{\frac{a^m - x^m}{x^m a^m}}{x - a} = -\frac{1}{x^m a^m} \cdot \left(\frac{x^m - a^m}{x - a}\right) $$

Apply the Quotient and Product Rules of Limits:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = \lim_{x \to a} \left( -\frac{1}{x^m a^m} \right) \cdot \lim_{x \to a} \left(\frac{x^m - a^m}{x - a}\right) $$

Since $m \in \mathbb{N}$, from Case 1 we know $\lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^m - a^m}{x - a} = m a^{m-1}$:

$$ = \left( -\frac{1}{a^m \cdot a^m} \right) \cdot \left( m a^{m-1} \right) $$

$$ = -\frac{1}{a^{2m}} \cdot m a^{m-1} = -m a^{m - 1 - 2m} = -m a^{-m - 1} $$

Substituting back $-m = n$:

$$ = n a^{n - 1} $$

Q.E.D.

Case 3: When $n$ is a rational fraction ($n = \frac{p}{q}$, where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}, q \neq 0$)

Given:

$$ n = \frac{p}{q} $$

To Prove:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^{p/q} - a^{p/q}}{x - a} = \frac{p}{q} a^{\frac{p}{q} - 1} $$

Proof: Let $x^{1/q} = y$ and $a^{1/q} = b$. Then $x = y^q$ and $a = b^q$. As $x \to a$, we have $y \to b$.

Transforming the original expression:

$$ \frac{x^{p/q} - a^{p/q}}{x - a} = \frac{(x^{1/q})^p - (a^{1/q})^p}{y^q - b^q} = \frac{y^p - b^p}{y^q - b^q} $$

Divide numerator and denominator by $(y - b)$ ($y \neq b$ since $x \neq a$):

$$ \frac{x^{p/q} - a^{p/q}}{x - a} = \frac{\frac{y^p - b^p}{y - b}}{\frac{y^q - b^q}{y - b}} $$

Taking limits as $y \to b$ using the Quotient Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^{p/q} - a^{p/q}}{x - a} = \frac{\lim_{y \to b} \frac{y^p - b^p}{y - b}}{\lim_{y \to b} \frac{y^q - b^q}{y - b}} $$

Since $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$, applying Cases 1 & 2 to numerator and denominator gives:

$$ = \frac{p b^{p-1}}{q b^{q-1}} = \frac{p}{q} b^{(p-1) - (q-1)} = \frac{p}{q} b^{p - q} $$

Substitute back $b = a^{1/q}$:

$$ = \frac{p}{q} (a^{1/q})^{p - q} = \frac{p}{q} a^{\frac{p - q}{q}} = \frac{p}{q} a^{\frac{p}{q} - 1} = n a^{n - 1} $$

Q.E.D.

3. High-Yield Examples, NCERT Exemplar & Application Problems

Problem 3.1: Piecewise Continuity and Limit Existence (NCERT Core)

Problem Statement: Find whether $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ exists, where

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{|x|}{x}, & x \neq 0 \\ 0, & x = 0 \end{cases} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Compute Left-Hand Limit (LHL)

$$ \text{LHL} = \lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(0 - h) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(-h) $$

For $x < 0$, $|x| = -x \implies |-h| = h$.

$$ \text{LHL} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|-h|}{-h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h}{-h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (-1) = -1 $$

Step 2: Compute Right-Hand Limit (RHL)

$$ \text{RHL} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(0 + h) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(h) $$

For $x > 0$, $|x| = x \implies |h| = h$.

$$ \text{RHL} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|h|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (1) = 1 $$

Step 3: Comparison and Conclusion

$$ \text{LHL} = -1 \quad \text{and} \quad \text{RHL} = 1 $$

$$ \text{Since } \text{LHL} \neq \text{RHL}, \lim_{x \to 0} f(x) \text{ does not exist.} $$

CBSE Board Marking Breakdown: - Evaluation of LHL with $x = 0 - h$: [1 Mark] - Evaluation of RHL with $x = 0 + h$: [1 Mark] - Stating $\text{LHL} \neq \text{RHL}$ and concluding DNE: [1 Mark]

Problem 3.2: Greatest Integer Function (NCERT Exemplar)

Problem Statement: Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 3} [x]$, where $[ \cdot ]$ denotes the greatest integer function (floor function).

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Left-Hand Limit (LHL)

$$ \text{LHL} = \lim_{x \to 3^-} [x] = \lim_{h \to 0} [3 - h], \quad (h > 0) $$

For any positive $h$ such that $0 < h < 1$, we have:

$$ 2 < 3 - h < 3 \implies [3 - h] = 2 $$

$$ \therefore \text{LHL} = \lim_{h \to 0} (2) = 2 $$

Step 2: Right-Hand Limit (RHL)

$$ \text{RHL} = \lim_{x \to 3^+} [x] = \lim_{h \to 0} [3 + h], \quad (h > 0) $$

For $0 < h < 1$, we have:

$$ 3 < 3 + h < 4 \implies [3 + h] = 3 $$

$$ \therefore \text{RHL} = \lim_{h \to 0} (3) = 3 $$

Step 3: Conclusion

$$ \text{LHL} = 2 \neq 3 = \text{RHL} \implies \lim_{x \to 3} [x] \text{ does not exist.} $$

Problem 3.3: Unknown Parameters System (CBSE Class 11 High-Yield)

Problem Statement: Let

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases} a + bx, & x < 1 \\ 4, & x = 1 \\ b - ax, & x > 1 \end{cases} $$

If $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = f(1)$, find the values of $a$ and $b$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Compute LHL at $x = 1$

$$ \text{LHL} = \lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(1 - h) $$

Since $1 - h < 1$:

$$ f(1 - h) = a + b(1 - h) $$

$$ \text{LHL} = \lim_{h \to 0} [a + b(1 - h)] = a + b $$

Step 2: Compute RHL at $x = 1$

$$ \text{RHL} = \lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} f(1 + h) $$

Since $1 + h > 1$:

$$ f(1 + h) = b - a(1 + h) $$

$$ \text{RHL} = \lim_{h \to 0} [b - a(1 + h)] = b - a $$

Step 3: Formulate System of Equations Given $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = f(1) = 4$. Existence of the limit requires $\text{LHL} = \text{RHL} = f(1)$:

$$ \begin{cases} a + b = 4 & \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)} \\ b - a = 4 & \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)} \end{cases} $$

Step 4: Solve the Equations Adding (1) and (2):

$$ (a + b) + (b - a) = 4 + 4 \implies 2b = 8 \implies b = 4 $$

Substitute $b = 4$ into (Equation 1):

$$ a + 4 = 4 \implies a = 0 $$

Final Answer:

$$ a = 0, \quad b = 4 $$

Problem 3.4: Application of the Standard Exponential Theorem

Problem Statement: Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(x + 1)^5 - 1}{x} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Method 1: Direct Substitution using Formula $\lim_{y \to a}\frac{y^n - a^n}{y - a} = na^{n-1}$ Let $y = x + 1$. As $x \to 0$, $y \to 1$. Also, $x = y - 1$. The limit transforms into:

$$ \lim_{y \to 1} \frac{y^5 - 1^5}{y - 1} $$

Comparing with $\lim_{y \to a} \frac{y^n - a^n}{y - a} = n a^{n-1}$, where $n = 5$ and $a = 1$:

$$ = 5 \cdot (1)^{5 - 1} = 5 \cdot 1 = 5 $$

Method 2: Binomial Expansion Verification

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\left( \binom{5}{0} + \binom{5}{1}x + \binom{5}{2}x^2 + \dots + \binom{5}{5}x^5 \right) - 1}{x} $$

$$ = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(1 + 5x + 10x^2 + 10x^3 + 5x^4 + x^5) - 1}{x} $$

$$ = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x(5 + 10x + 10x^2 + 5x^3 + x^4)}{x} $$

Since $x \to 0 \implies x \neq 0$, cancel $x$:

$$ = \lim_{x \to 0} (5 + 10x + 10x^2 + 5x^3 + x^4) = 5 + 0 = 5 $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Trap 4.1: Direct Substitution without Checking Indeterminacy

  • Incorrect Student Practice: Writing $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \frac{4 - 4}{2 - 2} = \text{Undefined or } 0$.
  • Correct Approach: Recognize $\frac{0}{0}$ as an indicator to factor out the common vanishing term $(x - 2)$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{(x - 2)(x + 2)}{x - 2} = \lim_{x \to 2} (x + 2) = 4 $$

Trap 4.2: Confusing $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ with the Function Value $f(a)$

  • The Error: Assuming that if $f(a)$ is undefined, the limit cannot exist.
  • The Counterexample:

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2, & x \neq 2 \\ 99, & x = 2 \end{cases} $$

Here, $f(2) = 99$, but $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) = 2^2 = 4$. Limits inspect only the punctured neighborhood $0 < |x - a| < \delta$.

Trap 4.3: Absolute Value Stripping Without One-Sided Analysis

  • The Error: Canceling $\frac{|x - 3|}{x - 3} = 1$ blindly.
  • The Reality:

$$ |x - 3| = \begin{cases} x - 3, & x > 3 \\ -(x - 3), & x < 3 \end{cases} $$

Therefore:

$$ \lim_{x \to 3^+} \frac{x - 3}{x - 3} = 1, \quad \text{while} \quad \lim_{x \to 3^-} \frac{-(x - 3)}{x - 3} = -1 $$

The two-sided limit does not exist. Whenever an absolute value, signum, or step function appears at an evaluation point, evaluating both LHL and RHL is mandatory.

Trap 4.4: Misapplying the Algebra of Limits

  • The rule $\lim [f(x) \cdot g(x)] = \lim f(x) \cdot \lim g(x)$ holds only if both individual limits exist independently.
  • Consider $f(x) = x$ and $g(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ as $x \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} [f(x) \cdot g(x)] = \lim_{x \to 0} \left( x \cdot \frac{1}{x} \right) = \lim_{x \to 0} 1 = 1 $$

Writing $\left(\lim_{x \to 0} x\right) \cdot \left(\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x}\right) = 0 \cdot \infty = 0$ is completely invalid.

5. Speed Hacks, Mnemonics & Golden Points

5.1 The General Power-Matching Shortcut

For rational-power algebraic fractions of the form:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^p - a^p}{x^q - a^q} = \frac{p}{q} a^{p - q} $$

Derivation:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{\frac{x^p - a^p}{x - a}}{\frac{x^q - a^q}{x - a}} = \frac{p a^{p-1}}{q a^{q-1}} = \frac{p}{q} a^{p - q} $$

Example (Solve in 5 Seconds):

$$ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^7 - 128}{x^3 - 8} = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^7 - 2^7}{x^3 - 2^3} = \frac{7}{3} \cdot 2^{7 - 3} = \frac{7}{3} \cdot 16 = \frac{112}{3} $$

5.2 Golden Rules for Greatest Integer Function Limits

For any integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$ \lim_{x \to k^-} [x] = k - 1 $$

$$ \lim_{x \to k^+} [x] = k $$

$$ \implies \lim_{x \to k} [x] \text{ NEVER exists for } k \in \mathbb{Z}. $$

For non-integer points $c \notin \mathbb{Z}$:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} [x] = [c] \quad (\text{Limit always exists!}) $$

Quick Check:

$$ \lim_{x \to 2.7} [x] = 2, \quad \lim_{x \to -1.4} [x] = -2 $$

Section 1: Pedagogical Foundations & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 The Concept of a Limit (NCERT Paradigm) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In elementary algebra, evaluating a function $f(x)$ at $x = c$ means computing the exact value $f(c)$. However, differential calculus originates from situations where $f(c)$ cannot be evaluated directly because it results in an indeterminate expression—most notably $\frac{0}{0}$.

Formal Intuitive Definition:
Let $f(x)$ be defined on an open interval containing $c$, except possibly at $c$ itself. We say that the limit of $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $c$ is $L$, denoted by:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L $$

if the value of $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $c$, from both sides of $c$, without ever setting $x = c$.

Left-Hand Limit (LHL) and Right-Hand Limit (RHL)
  • Left-Hand Limit (LHL): The value approached by $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $c$ through values strictly less than $c$ ($x < c$):

$$ \lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} f(c - h), \quad \text{where } h > 0 $$

  • Right-Hand Limit (RHL): The value approached by $f(x)$ as $x$ approaches $c$ through values strictly greater than $c$ ($x > c$):

$$ \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} f(c + h), \quad \text{where } h > 0 $$

Existence Criterion for a Limit:
A limit $\lim_{x \to c} f(x)$ exists if and only if both the LHL and RHL exist finitely and are identical:

$$ \lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L $$

Fundamental Distinction: $x \to c$ versus $x = c$

A common conceptual hurdle in Class 11 is confusing the dynamic condition $x \to c$ with the static equality $x = c$. - $x \to c \implies x \neq c \implies x - c \neq 0$. - Because $x - c \neq 0$, we are mathematically permitted to cancel the factor $(x - c)$ from both numerator and denominator without dividing by zero.

1.2 Fundamental Algebra of Limits (Limit Theorems) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = l$ and $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = m$, where $l, m \in \mathbb{R}$.

  1. Sum Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} [f(x) + g(x)] = \lim_{x \to c} f(x) + \lim_{x \to c} g(x) = l + m $$

  1. Difference Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} [f(x) - g(x)] = \lim_{x \to c} f(x) - \lim_{x \to c} g(x) = l - m $$

  1. Scalar Multiple Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} [k \cdot f(x)] = k \cdot \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = k \cdot l, \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{R} $$

  1. Product Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} [f(x) \cdot g(x)] = \left[\lim_{x \to c} f(x)\right] \cdot \left[\lim_{x \to c} g(x)\right] = l \cdot m $$

  1. Quotient Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} \left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{\lim_{x \to c} f(x)}{\lim_{x \to c} g(x)} = \frac{l}{m}, \quad \text{provided } m \neq 0 $$

  1. Power Rule:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} [f(x)]^k = \left[\lim_{x \to c} f(x)\right]^k = l^k, \quad \forall k \in \mathbb{Q} \text{ (subject to well-defined real outputs)} $$

1.3 Limits of Polynomial Functions ⭐⭐⭐

A polynomial function of degree $n$ is defined as:

$$ P(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0, \quad a_n \neq 0, \quad a_i \in \mathbb{R} $$

By applying the Sum, Scalar Multiple, and Power rules iteratively:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} P(x) = P(c) = a_n c^n + a_{n-1} c^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 c + a_0 $$

Direct Substitution Rule for Polynomials:
The limit of any polynomial function as $x \to c$ equals its functional value at $x = c$. It never yields an indeterminate form.

1.4 Limits of Rational Functions & The $\frac{0}{0}$ Indeterminacy ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

A rational function $R(x)$ is defined as the quotient of two polynomials:

$$ R(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}, \quad Q(x) \not\equiv 0 $$

When evaluating $\lim_{x \to c} \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$, three distinct cases arise upon direct substitution:

Case Direct Substitution Result Nature of Limit Pedagogical Treatment
Case I $P(c) \in \mathbb{R}, Q(c) \neq 0$ Determinate Immediate evaluation: $\lim_{x \to c} R(x) = \frac{P(c)}{Q(c)}$
Case II $P(c) \neq 0, Q(c) = 0$ Non-existent / Infinite Limit does not exist finitely ($\to \pm \infty$). Check one-sided limits for vertical asymptote behavior.
Case III $P(c) = 0, Q(c) = 0$ Indeterminate ($\frac{0}{0}$) Factor Theorem guarantees $(x - c)$ is a common factor. Must eliminate the vanishing factor before evaluating.
Factor Theorem Justification for Method of Factorization

By the Polynomial Factor Theorem:

$$ P(c) = 0 \iff (x - c) \text{ divides } P(x) \implies P(x) = (x - c) \cdot P_1(x) $$

$$ Q(c) = 0 \iff (x - c) \text{ divides } Q(x) \implies Q(x) = (x - c) \cdot Q_1(x) $$

Because the limit considers $x \to c$, we have $x \neq c \implies x - c \neq 0$. Thus, division by $(x - c)$ is valid:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{(x - c) P_1(x)}{(x - c) Q_1(x)} = \lim_{x \to c} \frac{P_1(x)}{Q_1(x)} = \frac{P_1(c)}{Q_1(c)} \quad (\text{if } Q_1(c) \neq 0) $$

Section 2: Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem (Standard Formula) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

For any positive integer $n$, or more generally for any real/rational number $n$ such that $a > 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1} $$

Proof Part A: For $n \in \mathbb{N}$ (Positive Integers)

Case 1: Algebraic Factorization Method
  • Given: Let $n$ be a positive integer ($n \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$), $a \in \mathbb{R}$, and $x \neq a$.
  • To Prove:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1} $$

Proof:
Recall the classical polynomial identity for the difference of two $n$-th powers:

$$ x^n - a^n = (x - a)(x^{n-1} + x^{n-2}a + x^{n-3}a^2 + \dots + x a^{n-2} + a^{n-1}) $$

We verify this identity by expanding the right-hand side:

$$ (x - a) \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} x^{n-1-k}a^k = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} x^{n-k}a^k - \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} x^{n-1-k}a^{k+1} $$

Expanding terms:

$$ = \left(x^n + x^{n-1}a + x^{n-2}a^2 + \dots + x a^{n-1}\right) - \left(x^{n-1}a + x^{n-2}a^2 + \dots + x a^{n-1} + a^n\right) $$

All intermediate terms cancel telescopically, leaving:

$$ = x^n - a^n $$

Now, consider the expression inside the limit:

$$ \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} $$

Since $x \to a$, we have $x \neq a$, which implies $x - a \neq 0$. Dividing numerator and denominator by $(x - a)$:

$$ \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = x^{n-1} + x^{n-2}a + x^{n-3}a^2 + \dots + x a^{n-2} + a^{n-1} $$

Taking limits on both sides as $x \to a$:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = \lim_{x \to a} \left( x^{n-1} + x^{n-2}a + x^{n-3}a^2 + \dots + x a^{n-2} + a^{n-1} \right) $$

Applying the Sum Rule of Limits across the finite sum of $n$ individual terms:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = \lim_{x \to a}(x^{n-1}) + a \lim_{x \to a}(x^{n-2}) + a^2 \lim_{x \to a}(x^{n-3}) + \dots + a^{n-2}\lim_{x \to a}(x) + a^{n-1} $$

Evaluating each limit by direct substitution:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = a^{n-1} + a \cdot a^{n-2} + a^2 \cdot a^{n-3} + \dots + a^{n-2} \cdot a + a^{n-1} $$

$$ = \underbrace{a^{n-1} + a^{n-1} + a^{n-1} + \dots + a^{n-1}}_{n \text{ terms}} $$

$$ = n a^{n-1} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Case 2: Substitution Method ($x = a + h$) via Binomial Expansion
  • Given: $x \to a$, $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
  • Construction / Transformation: Let $x = a + h$. As $x \to a$, it follows that $h = x - a \to 0$ with $h \neq 0$.

Proof:
Substitute $x = a + h$ into the standard rational expression:

$$ \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = \frac{(a + h)^n - a^n}{(a + h) - a} = \frac{(a + h)^n - a^n}{h} $$

Applying the Binomial Theorem for Positive Integral Index:

$$ (a + h)^n = \binom{n}{0} a^n + \binom{n}{1} a^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2} a^{n-2}h^2 + \dots + \binom{n}{n} h^n $$

$$ = a^n + n a^{n-1}h + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!} a^{n-2}h^2 + \dots + h^n $$

Subtracting $a^n$:

$$ (a + h)^n - a^n = n a^{n-1}h + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!} a^{n-2}h^2 + \dots + h^n $$

Factoring out $h$ from the numerator:

$$ (a + h)^n - a^n = h \left[ n a^{n-1} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!} a^{n-2}h + \dots + h^{n-1} \right] $$

Since $h \to 0$, $h \neq 0$. We divide numerator and denominator by $h$:

$$ \frac{(a + h)^n - a^n}{h} = n a^{n-1} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!} a^{n-2}h + \dots + h^{n-1} $$

Taking the limit as $h \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a + h)^n - a^n}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ n a^{n-1} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2!} a^{n-2}h + \dots + h^{n-1} \right] $$

Every term following the first term contains at least one power of $h$, causing those terms to evaluate to zero:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(a + h)^n - a^n}{h} = n a^{n-1} + 0 + 0 + \dots + 0 = n a^{n-1} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof Part B: Extension to Negative Integers ($n = -m$, where $m \in \mathbb{N}$)

  • Given: Let $n = -m$, where $m$ is a positive integer ($m \in \mathbb{N}$) and $a \neq 0$.
  • To Prove:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1} = -m a^{-m-1} $$

Proof:
Express the numerator in terms of $m$:

$$ x^n - a^n = x^{-m} - a^{-m} = \frac{1}{x^m} - \frac{1}{a^m} = \frac{a^m - x^m}{x^m a^m} = -\frac{x^m - a^m}{x^m a^m} $$

Dividing by $(x - a)$:

$$ \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = \frac{-\frac{x^m - a^m}{x^m a^m}}{x - a} = -\frac{1}{x^m a^m} \cdot \left(\frac{x^m - a^m}{x - a}\right) $$

Taking the limit as $x \to a$:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = \lim_{x \to a} \left[ -\frac{1}{x^m a^m} \cdot \frac{x^m - a^m}{x - a} \right] $$

Applying the Product Rule of Limits:

$$ = \left( \lim_{x \to a} \left[-\frac{1}{x^m a^m}\right] \right) \cdot \left( \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^m - a^m}{x - a} \right) $$

Evaluating the first factor by direct substitution (since $a \neq 0$):

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \left[-\frac{1}{x^m a^m}\right] = -\frac{1}{a^m \cdot a^m} = -\frac{1}{a^{2m}} $$

From Part A, because $m \in \mathbb{N}$:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^m - a^m}{x - a} = m a^{m-1} $$

Multiplying the results:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = -\frac{1}{a^{2m}} \cdot m a^{m-1} = -m \cdot \frac{a^{m-1}}{a^{2m}} = -m a^{(m-1) - 2m} = -m a^{-m-1} $$

Since $n = -m$:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof Part C: Extension to Rational Numbers ($n = \frac{p}{q}$, where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}, q \neq 0, a > 0$)

  • Given: Let $n = \frac{p}{q}$, where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$, $q > 0$, and $a > 0$.
  • To Prove:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^{p/q} - a^{p/q}}{x - a} = \frac{p}{q} a^{(p/q) - 1} $$

Proof:
Substitute variables: Let $x^{1/q} = y$ and $a^{1/q} = b$.
Then:

$$ x = y^q \quad \text{and} \quad a = b^q $$

Moreover, as $x \to a$, $x^{1/q} \to a^{1/q}$, which means $y \to b$.

Express the terms of the quotient in terms of $y$ and $b$:

$$ x^{p/q} = (x^{1/q})^p = y^p $$

$$ a^{p/q} = (a^{1/q})^p = b^p $$

$$ x = y^q, \quad a = b^q $$

The limit becomes:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^{p/q} - a^{p/q}}{x - a} = \lim_{y \to b} \frac{y^p - b^p}{y^q - b^q} $$

Divide both the numerator and denominator by $(y - b)$ (valid because $y \to b \implies y \neq b \implies y - b \neq 0$):

$$ \lim_{y \to b} \frac{y^p - b^p}{y^q - b^q} = \lim_{y \to b} \frac{\frac{y^p - b^p}{y - b}}{\frac{y^q - b^q}{y - b}} $$

Applying the Quotient Rule of Limits:

$$ = \frac{\lim_{y \to b} \frac{y^p - b^p}{y - b}}{\lim_{y \to b} \frac{y^q - b^q}{y - b}} $$

Since $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$, we apply the results from Part A and Part B to both the numerator and denominator:

$$ \lim_{y \to b} \frac{y^p - b^p}{y - b} = p b^{p-1} $$

$$ \lim_{y \to b} \frac{y^q - b^q}{y - b} = q b^{q-1} \quad (\neq 0 \text{ since } a > 0 \implies b > 0) $$

Therefore:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^{p/q} - a^{p/q}}{x - a} = \frac{p b^{p-1}}{q b^{q-1}} = \frac{p}{q} b^{(p-1) - (q-1)} = \frac{p}{q} b^{p - q} $$

Substitute back $b = a^{1/q}$:

$$ b^{p - q} = \left(a^{1/q}\right)^{p - q} = a^{\frac{p-q}{q}} = a^{\frac{p}{q} - 1} $$

Substituting this back gives:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^{p/q} - a^{p/q}}{x - a} = \frac{p}{q} a^{\frac{p}{q} - 1} = n a^{n-1} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Problem 1: Evaluation by Polynomial Factorization (Quadratic over Cubic) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem Statement:
Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{3x^2 - x - 10}{x^3 - 4x} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Test for Indeterminacy.
Substitute $x = 2$:

$$ \text{Numerator} = 3(2)^2 - (2) - 10 = 12 - 2 - 10 = 0 $$

$$ \text{Denominator} = (2)^3 - 4(2) = 8 - 8 = 0 $$

The expression takes the indeterminate form $\frac{0}{0}$.

Step 2: Factorize Numerator and Denominator.
- Factorizing $3x^2 - x - 10$ using middle-term splitting (product $= -30$, sum $= -1$):

$$ 3x^2 - 6x + 5x - 10 = 3x(x - 2) + 5(x - 2) = (x - 2)(3x + 5) $$

  • Factorizing $x^3 - 4x$:

$$ x(x^2 - 4) = x(x - 2)(x + 2) $$

Step 3: Cancel Common Vanishing Factor.
Substitute the factored forms back into the limit:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{(x - 2)(3x + 5)}{x(x - 2)(x + 2)} $$

Since $x \to 2$, $x \neq 2 \implies x - 2 \neq 0$. Cancelling $(x - 2)$:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{3x + 5}{x(x + 2)} $$

Step 4: Direct Substitution.

$$ L = \frac{3(2) + 5}{2(2 + 2)} = \frac{6 + 5}{2(4)} = \frac{11}{8} $$

Final Answer: $\frac{11}{8}$

Scoring Breakdown (CBSE Board Standard - 3 Marks):
  • Identifying form $\frac{0}{0}$ and stating the factorization plan: 0.5 Mark
  • Correct factorization of both numerator and denominator: 1.0 Mark
  • Justifying $x - 2 \neq 0$ and cancelling the common factor: 0.5 Mark
  • Correct substitution and final fraction $\frac{11}{8}$: 1.0 Mark

Problem 2: Rationalization Combined with Algebraic Identity ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem Statement:
Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} - 1}{x} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Test for Indeterminacy.
Direct substitution of $x = 0$:

$$ \frac{\sqrt{1 + 0 + 0} - 1}{0} = \frac{1 - 1}{0} = \frac{0}{0} \quad (\text{Indeterminate Form}) $$

Step 2: Rationalize the Numerator.
Multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate $\left(\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} + 1\right)$:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\left(\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} - 1\right)\left(\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} + 1\right)}{x \left(\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} + 1\right)} $$

Applying the difference of squares identity $(A - B)(A + B) = A^2 - B^2$:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(1 + x + x^2) - 1}{x \left(\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} + 1\right)} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x + x^2}{x \left(\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} + 1\right)} $$

Step 3: Extract and Cancel the Vanishing Factor.
Factor out $x$ from the numerator:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x(1 + x)}{x \left(\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} + 1\right)} $$

Since $x \to 0$, $x \neq 0$. Cancelling $x$:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 + x}{\sqrt{1 + x + x^2} + 1} $$

Step 4: Direct Substitution.

$$ L = \frac{1 + 0}{\sqrt{1 + 0 + 0} + 1} = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \frac{1}{2} $$

Final Answer: $\frac{1}{2}$

Problem 3: NCERT Exemplar — Direct Application of Standard Formula ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem Statement:
Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{15} - 1}{x^{10} - 1} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Test for Indeterminacy.
At $x = 1$, the form is $\frac{1^{15} - 1}{1^{10} - 1} = \frac{0}{0}$.

Step 2: Algebraic Restructuring to Match Standard Form.
Recall the standard theorem: $\lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1}$.
Divide both the numerator and denominator by $(x - 1)$ (since $x \to 1 \implies x - 1 \neq 0$):

$$ \frac{x^{15} - 1}{x^{10} - 1} = \frac{\frac{x^{15} - 1^{15}}{x - 1}}{\frac{x^{10} - 1^{10}}{x - 1}} $$

Step 3: Apply Limit Theorems Separately.

$$ L = \frac{\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{15} - 1^{15}}{x - 1}}{\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{10} - 1^{10}}{x - 1}} $$

Applying the standard formula $\lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1}$: - For the numerator ($n = 15, a = 1$):

$$ \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{15} - 1^{15}}{x - 1} = 15(1)^{15 - 1} = 15(1)^{14} = 15 $$

  • For the denominator ($n = 10, a = 1$):

$$ \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^{10} - 1^{10}}{x - 1} = 10(1)^{10 - 1} = 10(1)^9 = 10 $$

Step 4: Compute the Final Ratio.

$$ L = \frac{15}{10} = \frac{3}{2} $$

Final Answer: $\frac{3}{2}$

Problem 4: Composite Variable Transformation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem Statement:
Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(1 + x)^6 - 1}{(1 + x)^2 - 1} $$

Step-by-Step Solution:

$$ \text{As } x \to 0, \quad y \to 1 + 0 \implies y \to 1 $$

Substitute $1 + x = y$ and note that $1 = 1^6 = 1^2$:

Divide numerator and denominator by $(y - 1)$:

Numerator: $6 \cdot (1)^{6-1} = 6$

Problem 5: Parameter Determination (Inverse Problem — High Board Frequency) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Problem Statement:
If $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^n - 2^n}{x - 2} = 80$, and $n \in \mathbb{N}$, find the value of $n$.

Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Apply the Standard Limit Theorem.
Using $\lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1}$ with $a = 2$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^n - 2^n}{x - 2} = n \cdot 2^{n-1} $$

Step 2: Formulate the Algebraic Equation.
We are given:

$$ n \cdot 2^{n-1} = 80 $$

Step 3: Prime Factorization and Term Matching.
Express $80$ as a product of an integer and a power of $2$:

$$ 80 = 5 \times 16 = 5 \times 2^4 $$

Equating the two forms:

$$ n \cdot 2^{n-1} = 5 \cdot 2^4 $$

By inspection: - $n = 5$ - Check exponent: $n - 1 = 5 - 1 = 4$, which matches $2^4$.

Since $f(n) = n 2^{n-1}$ is strictly monotonically increasing for $n \ge 1$, $n = 5$ is the unique positive integer solution.

Section 4: Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Blind Cancellation without Stating $x \neq a$

  • The Mistake: Writing $\frac{(x-2)(x+3)}{x-2} = x+3$ without qualifying that $x \neq 2$.
  • Examiner's Perspective: In board examinations, cancelling an expression that equals zero violates algebraic laws ($0/0$ is undefined).
  • Remedy: Always write:

$$ \text{"Since } x \to a, \; x \neq a \implies x - a \neq 0\text{"} $$

before crossing out factors.

Pitfall 2: Premature Limit Dropping or Persistent Limit Notation

  • The Mistake A: Writing $\lim_{x \to 2} (x + 3) = \lim_{x \to 2} (2 + 3) = 5$. (Once the limit is evaluated, the $\lim$ operator must be dropped).
  • The Mistake B: Omitting $\lim_{x \to a}$ in intermediate simplification steps:

$$ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^2 - 4}{x - 2} = \frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2} = x + 2 = 4 \quad \text{[Incorrect Notation]} $$

  • Remedy: Keep writing $\lim_{x \to a}$ until the substitution step, and drop it the moment you substitute $x = a$.

Pitfall 3: Applying Standard Formula with Non-Zero Base Values in $h$ Form

  • The Mistake: Confusing forms like:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(x+a)^n - a^n}{x} \quad \text{versus} \quad \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^n - a^n}{x} $$

Students sometimes misidentify $x \to 0$ and mistakenly write the answer as $n x^{n-1}$ instead of $n a^{n-1}$. - Remedy: Check the denominator. If the denominator is $x$ and $x \to 0$, identify $h = x$, write it as $\lim_{h \to 0}\frac{(a+h)^n - a^n}{h}$, which produces $n a^{n-1}$.

Pitfall 4: Neglecting Conjugate Signs in Rationalization

  • The Mistake:

$$ \sqrt{x^2 + 5} - 3 \implies \text{multiplying by } \sqrt{x^2 - 5} + 3 $$

  • Remedy: The sign inside the radical must never change during conjugation; change only the sign between the radical and the adjacent term.

$$ \text{Conjugate of } (\sqrt{x^2 + 5} - 3) \text{ is } (\sqrt{x^2 + 5} + 3) $$

Section 5: Speed Hacks, Golden Points & Mnemonics

1. The Generalized Dual-Power Quotient Shortcut ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Whenever a problem features differences of powers in both numerator and denominator:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^m - a^m}{x^n - a^n} = \frac{m}{n} a^{m-n} $$

Derivation:

$$ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{\frac{x^m - a^m}{x - a}}{\frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a}} = \frac{m a^{m-1}}{n a^{n-1}} = \frac{m}{n} a^{(m-1) - (n-1)} = \frac{m}{n} a^{m-n} $$

Mental Math Verification Example:
Find $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x^7 - 128}{x^3 - 8}$: - Here $a = 2$, $m = 7$, $n = 3$. - Instant Answer: $\frac{7}{3} \cdot 2^{7 - 3} = \frac{7}{3} \cdot 2^4 = \frac{7}{3} \cdot 16 = \frac{112}{3}$.

2. The Shifted Variable Shortcut

For limits centered around zero involving shifted powers:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(a + x)^n - a^n}{x} = n a^{n-1} $$

Specifically, when $a = 1$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(1 + x)^n - 1}{x} = n $$

Mental Math Verification Example:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt[5]{1 + x} - 1}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{(1 + x)^{1/5} - 1}{x} = \frac{1}{5} $$

3. Binomial Approximation for Rapid Checking (Calculus Speed Hack)

When $x \to 0$ and $|x| \ll 1$, use the first-order Taylor/Binomial approximation:

$$ (1 + x)^n \approx 1 + nx $$

  • Example:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{1 + 3x} - \sqrt[3]{1 - 2x}}{x} $$

Approximate the terms:

$$ \sqrt{1 + 3x} = (1 + 3x)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \frac{3}{2}x $$

$$ \sqrt[3]{1 - 2x} = (1 - 2x)^{1/3} \approx 1 - \frac{2}{3}x $$

Subtract:

$$ \left(1 + \frac{3}{2}x\right) - \left(1 - \frac{2}{3}x\right) = \left(\frac{3}{2} + \frac{2}{3}\right)x = \frac{13}{6}x $$

Divide by $x$:

$$ L = \frac{13}{6} $$

(Note: In board exams, use this method to check your work; write out full rationalization or standard substitutions for full credit).

$$ \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{T} \implies Test \quad &&\text{Direct substitute to verify if it is truly } \frac{0}{0}. \\ &\mathbf{F} \implies Factor \quad &&\text{Identify } (x - c) \text{ via identities or factor theorem.} \\ &\mathbf{C} \implies Cancel \quad &&\text{Cancel } (x - c) \text{ while stating } x - c \neq 0. \\ &\mathbf{S} \implies Substitute \quad &&\text{Plug in } x = c \text{ to evaluate the remaining determinate expression.} \end{aligned} $$

Limits of Trigonometric Functions and the Sandwich Theorem

Class 11 Mathematics — Complete Teaching & Lecture Notes

Chapter: Limits and Derivatives

Subtopic: Limits of Trigonometric Functions & The Sandwich Theorem

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

Concept 1.1: Motivation & The Indeterminate Form $\left[\frac{0}{0}\right]$

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐
In algebraic limits, indeterminate forms such as $\left[\frac{0}{0}\right]$ are eliminated via factorization, rationalization, or algebraic identities. However, when evaluating trigonometric limits such as:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} $$

direct substitution gives $\frac{\sin 0}{0} = \left[\frac{0}{0}\right]$. Trigonometric functions are non-algebraic (transcendental), meaning direct algebraic factorization fails. To resolve this boundary behavior without differential calculus (such as L'Hôpital's Rule, which is logically circular at this stage because the derivative of $\sin x$ depends on this very limit), a geometric inequality bounding technique is required.

Concept 1.2: The Sandwich Theorem (Squeeze Principle)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Board & Competitive Exam Yield)

Formal Statement:

Let $f(x)$, $g(x)$, and $h(x)$ be three real-valued functions defined on a common interval $I$ containing $c$, except possibly at the point $x = c$ itself. If: 1. $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x \in I \setminus \{c\}$ 2. $\lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L$

Then:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L $$

Intuition & Geometric Picture:

The function $f(x)$ is strictly trapped (sandwiched/squeezed) between the lower bounding curve $g(x)$ and the upper bounding curve $h(x)$. As the independent variable $x$ approaches $c$, both outer boundaries collapse into the single real value $L$. Because $f(x)$ has no freedom to escape the envelope defined by $g(x)$ and $h(x)$, its limit must also be forced to $L$.

Concept 1.3: Fundamental Fundamental Inequality for Trigonometric Functions

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐
For any real angle $x$ measured in radians such that $0 < |x| < \frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$ \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1 $$

and equivalently:

$$ \sin x < x < \tan x \quad \text{for } x \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) $$

CRITICAL NCERT NOTE: The angle $x$ MUST be measured in radians. The geometric length of a circular arc is given by $s = r\theta$ if and only if $\theta$ is in radians. If $x$ is given in degrees, the relation fails unless converted using $1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180}\text{ rad}$.

Concept 1.4: Standard Fundamental Limit Theorems

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  1. Sine Fundamental Limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1 \quad \iff \quad \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{\sin x} = 1 $$

  1. Cosine Identity Limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0 $$

  1. Cosine Quadratic Limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2} $$

  1. Tangent Fundamental Limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\tan x}{x} = 1 \quad \iff \quad \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x}{\tan x} = 1 $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: The Inequality $\cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1$ for $0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$

1. Statement:

Prove geometrically that for an acute angle $x$ measured in radians ($0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$):

$$ \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1 $$

2. Construction:

Consider a unit circle (radius $r = 1$) centered at the origin $O(0,0)$. - Let $A$ be the point $(1,0)$ on the circumference. - Let $B$ be a point on the circle such that $\angle AOB = x$ radians, where $0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$. - Drop a perpendicular from $B$ to the line segment $OA$, meeting $OA$ at point $D$. Therefore, $BD \perp OA$. - Draw a tangent to the circle at point $A$. Extend the ray $OB$ to intersect this tangent line at point $C$. Thus, $\angle OAC = 90^\circ$ and $CA \perp OA$.

         C
        /|
       / |
      B  |
     /|  |
    / |  |
   /  |  |
  O---D--A

In the right-angled triangle $\triangle ODB$:

$$ OD = OB \cos x = 1 \cdot \cos x = \cos x $$

$$ BD = OB \sin x = 1 \cdot \sin x = \sin x $$

In the right-angled triangle $\triangle OAC$:

$$ OA = 1 $$

$$ AC = OA \tan x = 1 \cdot \tan x = \tan x $$

3. Step-by-Step Proof:

Step 1: Establish geometric containment of regions

From the construction, we observe the nesting of geometric figures:

$$ \text{Area}(\triangle OAB) < \text{Area}(\text{Sector } OAB) < \text{Area}(\triangle OAC) $$

Step 2: Compute individual areas

  1. Area of $\triangle OAB$:

$$ \text{Area}(\triangle OAB) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} \times OA \times BD = \frac{1}{2} \times 1 \times \sin x = \frac{1}{2}\sin x $$

  1. Area of circular sector $OAB$: For a circle of radius $r$ and sector angle $x$ radians:

$$ \text{Area}(\text{Sector } OAB) = \frac{1}{2} r^2 x = \frac{1}{2} (1)^2 x = \frac{1}{2}x $$

  1. Area of $\triangle OAC$:

$$ \text{Area}(\triangle OAC) = \frac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \frac{1}{2} \times OA \times AC = \frac{1}{2} \times 1 \times \tan x = \frac{1}{2}\tan x $$

Step 3: Insert areas into the inequality

$$ \frac{1}{2}\sin x < \frac{1}{2}x < \frac{1}{2}\tan x $$

Multiplying through by $2$ (since $2 > 0$, inequality direction is preserved):

$$ \sin x < x < \tan x $$

Step 4: Divide by $\sin x$

Since $0 < x < \frac{\pi}{2}$, $\sin x > 0$. Dividing the entire compound inequality by $\sin x$:

$$ \frac{\sin x}{\sin x} < \frac{x}{\sin x} < \frac{\tan x}{\sin x} $$

Recalling $\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$:

$$ 1 < \frac{x}{\sin x} < \frac{1}{\cos x} $$

Step 5: Take reciprocals

Taking reciprocals of all terms reverses the order of the inequalities:

$$ \frac{1}{1} > \frac{\sin x}{x} > \cos x $$

$$ \iff \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Standard Limit $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$

1. Statement:

Prove that:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1 $$

2. Step-by-Step Proof:

Case I: Right-Hand Limit ($x \to 0^+$)

Let $x > 0$. As $x \to 0^+$, we may assume $x \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.
From Proof 2.1:

$$ \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1 $$

Evaluate the boundary limits:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0^+} \cos x = \cos(0) = 1 $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 0^+} 1 = 1 $$

Applying the Sandwich Theorem:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1 $$

Case II: Left-Hand Limit ($x \to 0^-$)

Let $x < 0$. Put $x = -y$, where $y > 0$.
As $x \to 0^-$, $y \to 0^+$.
Substitute $x = -y$ into the expression:

$$ \frac{\sin x}{x} = \frac{\sin(-y)}{-y} = \frac{-\sin y}{-y} = \frac{\sin y}{y} $$

Taking the limit as $y \to 0^+$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{y \to 0^+} \frac{\sin y}{y} = 1 \quad (\text{from Case I}) $$

Conclusion:

Since:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1 $$

Therefore:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: Standard Limit $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0$

1. Statement:

Prove that:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0 $$

2. Proof (Algebraic & Half-Angle Method):

Using the half-angle trigonometric identity:

$$ 1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

Substitute this into the expression:

$$ \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = \frac{2\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{x} = \frac{\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{\frac{x}{2}} $$

Decompose the expression:

$$ \frac{\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{\frac{x}{2}} = \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{\frac{x}{2}}\right] \cdot \sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

Now take the limit as $x \to 0$. As $x \to 0$, $\theta = \frac{x}{2} \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{\frac{x}{2}}\right] \cdot \lim_{x \to 0} \sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

$$ = 1 \cdot \sin(0) = 1 \cdot 0 = 0 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.4: Standard Limit $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2}$

1. Statement:

Prove that:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2} $$

2. Proof:

Using $1 - \cos x = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$:

$$ \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{2\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{x^2} $$

Rewrite the denominator to match the argument of the sine function, which is $\frac{x}{2}$:

$$ x^2 = 4 \cdot \left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^2 $$

Therefore:

$$ \frac{2\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{x^2} = \frac{2\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{4\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)^2} = \frac{1}{2} \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{\frac{x}{2}}\right]^2 $$

Taking limits as $x \to 0$ (letting $u = \frac{x}{2} \to 0$):

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2} \left[\lim_{u \to 0}\frac{\sin u}{u}\right]^2 = \frac{1}{2} (1)^2 = \frac{1}{2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.5: Standard Limit $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\tan x}{x} = 1$

1. Statement:

Prove that:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\tan x}{x} = 1 $$

2. Proof:

Rewrite $\tan x$ as $\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$:

$$ \frac{\tan x}{x} = \frac{\sin x}{x \cos x} = \left(\frac{\sin x}{x}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{\cos x}\right) $$

Taking limits as $x \to 0$ using limit algebra:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\tan x}{x} = \left(\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x}\right) \cdot \left(\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1}{\cos x}\right) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{\cos 0} = 1 \cdot 1 = 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Concept of Derivative, Geometric Interpretation, and Differentiation from First Principles

Class 11 Mathematics: Limits and Derivatives

Topic: Concept of Derivative, Geometric Interpretation, and Differentiation from First Principles

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

Concept 1.1: Physical Motivation — Average Rate of Change vs. Instantaneous Rate of Change

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Prerequisites: Intuitive concept of limits, functional values.

1. Intuition

Consider a particle moving along a straight line whose distance $s$ from a fixed origin at time $t$ is given by $s = f(t)$. - If we observe the particle over a time interval $[t_0, t_0 + \Delta t]$, the displacement is:

$$ \Delta s = f(t_0 + \Delta t) - f(t_0) $$

  • The average velocity over this interval is the ratio of displacement to elapsed time:

$$ v_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} = \frac{f(t_0 + \Delta t) - f(t_0)}{\Delta t} $$

  • If we shrink $\Delta t$ infinitely close to zero ($\Delta t \to 0$), the average velocity approaches a limiting value known as the instantaneous velocity at time $t = t_0$:

$$ v(t_0) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{f(t_0 + \Delta t) - f(t_0)}{\Delta t} $$

This ratio $\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$ as $\Delta x \to 0$ represents the instantaneous rate of change of any dependent variable $y = f(x)$ with respect to the independent variable $x$.

Concept 1.2: Geometric Interpretation — The Secant Line Becoming the Tangent Line

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Core Conceptual & Board Viva Question)

1. The Geometry

Let $y = f(x)$ be a continuous real-valued function whose graph is a smooth curve in the Cartesian plane.

       y ^
         |                                 Q (x + h, f(x + h))
         |                                /
         |                               /   Secant Line PQ
         |                   P          /
         |                  /•---------/
         |                 / |        /|
         |                /  |       / |
         |          _____•   |      /  |
         |         /     (x, f(x)) /   |
         |        /          |    /    |
         |       /           |   /     |
         |      /            |  /      |
         +-----+-------------+--+------+----------> x
               O             x         x + h
                             <--- h --->
  1. Select a fixed point $P(x, f(x))$ on the curve.
  2. Choose a neighboring point $Q(x + h, f(x + h))$ on the curve, where $h \neq 0$ is a small increment in $x$ (positive or negative).
  3. Draw the chord (secant line) passing through $P$ and $Q$.
  4. The slope of the secant line $PQ$, denoted by $m_{\text{sec}}$, is:

$$ m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{(x + h) - x} = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

2. The Limiting Process

Now, let $Q$ move along the curve towards $P$. This occurs if and only if $h \to 0$. - As $Q \to P$, the secant line $PQ$ rotates about the pivot point $P$. - In the limit as $h \to 0$, the secant line $PQ$ tends to a unique limiting line $PT$. - This limiting straight line $PT$ touches the curve at $P$ without crossing it locally and is defined as the tangent line to the curve at point $P$. - Therefore, the slope of the tangent line at $P$, denoted by $m_{\text{tan}}$, is:

$$ m_{\text{tan}} = \lim_{Q \to P} (\text{Slope of chord } PQ) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

Core Geometric Theorem: The derivative of a function $f(x)$ at a point $x = a$, denoted by $f'(a)$, is geometrically equal to the slope of the tangent line to the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $(a, f(a))$.

$$ \text{Slope of tangent at } x = a \implies m = \tan \theta = f'(a) = \left. \frac{df}{dx} \right|_{x=a} $$

where $\theta$ is the inclination angle of the tangent with the positive direction of the $x$-axis ($0 \le \theta < \pi, \theta \neq \frac{\pi}{2}$).

Concept 1.3: Rigorous Definition of Derivative & First Principle

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Standard 4-Mark / 5-Mark Question)

1. Formal Definition

Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval $(a, b)$, and let $x \in (a, b)$. The derivative of $f$ at $x$ is defined by:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

provided this limit exists finitely.

  • This process of computing the derivative directly using the limit definition is called Differentiation from First Principles (or Ab Initio method).

2. Notations for Derivatives

If $y = f(x)$, the derivative is symbolized by:

$$ f'(x) \equiv \frac{dy}{dx} \equiv \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)] \equiv y' \equiv y_1 \equiv Df(x) $$

3. Left-Hand and Right-Hand Derivatives (Differentiability at a Point)

For $f'(a)$ to exist, the two one-sided limits must both exist and be equal: - Left-Hand Derivative (LHD):

$$ f'(a^-) = L f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} = \lim_{k \to 0^+} \frac{f(a - k) - f(a)}{-k} $$

  • Right-Hand Derivative (RHD):

$$ f'(a^+) = R f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} $$

A function $f(x)$ is differentiable at $x = a$ if and only if:

$$ L f'(a) = R f'(a) = l \quad (\text{where } l \text{ is a finite real number}) $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

All proofs below are derived using the First Principle of Differentiation:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

Derivation 2.1: Power Function $f(x) = x^n$ ($n \in \mathbb{Q}$)

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Prove from first principles that $\frac{d}{dx}(x^n) = n x^{n-1}$ for all real $x$ where the function is defined.

Proof

Step 1: Set up the definition
Let $f(x) = x^n$. Then $f(x + h) = (x + h)^n$.
By definition of the derivative:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x + h)^n - x^n}{h} $$

Step 2: Change of variable
Let $t = x + h$.
As $h \to 0$, we have $t \to x$.
Substitute $h = t - x$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{t \to x} \frac{t^n - x^n}{t - x} $$

Step 3: Evaluate standard algebraic limit
Recall the standard limit: $\lim_{t \to a} \frac{t^n - a^n}{t - a} = n a^{n-1}$.
Applying this with $a = x$:

$$ f'(x) = n x^{n-1} $$

(Alternative Method via Binomial Expansion for $n \in \mathbb{N}$):

$$ (x + h)^n = x^n + \binom{n}{1} x^{n-1} h + \binom{n}{2} x^{n-2} h^2 + \dots + h^n $$

$$ (x + h)^n - x^n = h \left[ n x^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2} x^{n-2} h + \dots + h^{n-1} \right] $$

Dividing by $h$ ($h \neq 0$):

$$ \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = n x^{n-1} + \binom{n}{2} x^{n-2} h + \dots + h^{n-1} $$

Taking the limit as $h \to 0$:

$$ f'(x) = n x^{n-1} + 0 + \dots + 0 = n x^{n-1} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2.2: Trigonometric Function $f(x) = \sin x$

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Find the derivative of $f(x) = \sin x$ with respect to $x$ from first principles.

Proof

Step 1: Set up the definition

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} $$

Step 2: Apply the trigonometric transformation formula
Recall: $\sin C - \sin D = 2 \cos\left(\frac{C + D}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{C - D}{2}\right)$.
Setting $C = x + h$ and $D = x$:

$$ \sin(x + h) - \sin x = 2 \cos\left(\frac{x + h + x}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{x + h - x}{2}\right) = 2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) $$

Step 3: Substitute and restructure the limit

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \right] $$

Step 4: Distribute limit across products

$$ f'(x) = \left( \lim_{h \to 0} \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \right) \cdot \left( \lim_{\frac{h}{2} \to 0} \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \right) $$

Using standard limit $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$:

$$ f'(x) = \cos(x + 0) \cdot 1 = \cos x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2.3: Trigonometric Function $f(x) = \cos x$

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Find the derivative of $f(x) = \cos x$ from first principles.

Proof

Step 1: Set up the definition

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\cos(x + h) - \cos x}{h} $$

Step 2: Apply transformation formula
Recall: $\cos C - \cos D = -2 \sin\left(\frac{C + D}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{C - D}{2}\right)$.
Setting $C = x + h$ and $D = x$:

$$ \cos(x + h) - \cos x = -2 \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) $$

Step 3: Restructure and evaluate limit

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-2 \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h} = -\lim_{h \to 0} \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \cdot \lim_{\frac{h}{2} \to 0} \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} $$

$$ f'(x) = -\sin(x + 0) \cdot 1 = -\sin x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2.4: Trigonometric Function $f(x) = \tan x$

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Compute the derivative of $f(x) = \tan x$ from first principles ($x \neq (2n+1)\frac{\pi}{2}$).

Proof

Step 1: Set up the definition

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\tan(x + h) - \tan x}{h} $$

Step 2: Convert to sine and cosine

$$ \tan(x + h) - \tan x = \frac{\sin(x + h)}{\cos(x + h)} - \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{\sin(x + h)\cos x - \cos(x + h)\sin x}{\cos(x + h)\cos x} $$

Step 3: Apply compound angle identity in numerator
Using $\sin A \cos B - \cos A \sin B = \sin(A - B)$:

$$ \sin(x + h - x) = \sin h $$

Hence:

$$ \frac{\tan(x + h) - \tan x}{h} = \frac{\sin h}{h} \cdot \frac{1}{\cos(x + h)\cos x} $$

Step 4: Evaluate the limit

$$ f'(x) = \left( \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h}{h} \right) \cdot \left( \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\cos(x + h)\cos x} \right) $$

$$ f'(x) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{\cos(x + 0)\cos x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2.5: Trigonometric Functions $\cot x$, $\sec x$, and $\csc x$

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

(a) Derivative of $\cot x$:

$$ f(x + h) - f(x) = \frac{\cos(x + h)}{\sin(x + h)} - \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{\cos(x + h)\sin x - \sin(x + h)\cos x}{\sin(x + h)\sin x} = \frac{-\sin h}{\sin(x + h)\sin x} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left( -\frac{\sin h}{h} \right) \cdot \frac{1}{\sin(x + h)\sin x} = -1 \cdot \frac{1}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc^2 x $$

(b) Derivative of $\sec x$:

$$ f(x + h) - f(x) = \frac{1}{\cos(x + h)} - \frac{1}{\cos x} = \frac{\cos x - \cos(x + h)}{\cos(x + h)\cos x} = \frac{2 \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{\cos(x + h)\cos x} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \cdot \frac{\sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)}{\cos(x + h)\cos x} \right] = 1 \cdot \frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x} = \sec x \tan x $$

(c) Derivative of $\csc x$:

$$ f(x + h) - f(x) = \frac{1}{\sin(x + h)} - \frac{1}{\sin x} = \frac{\sin x - \sin(x + h)}{\sin(x + h)\sin x} = \frac{-2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{\sin(x + h)\sin x} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ -\frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \cdot \frac{\cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)}{\sin(x + h)\sin x} \right] = -1 \cdot \frac{\cos x}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc x \cot x $$

Derivation 2.6: Exponential and Logarithmic Functions ($e^x$ and $\ln x$)

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

(a) Derivative of $f(x) = e^x$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^{x + h} - e^x}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^x(e^h - 1)}{h} = e^x \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^h - 1}{h} $$

Using the standard exponential limit $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{e^h - 1}{h} = 1$:

$$ f'(x) = e^x \cdot 1 = e^x $$

(b) Derivative of $f(x) = \ln x$ ($x > 0$):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\ln(x + h) - \ln x}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{h} \ln\left(\frac{x + h}{x}\right) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{h} \ln\left(1 + \frac{h}{x}\right) $$

Multiply and divide by $x$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{x} \cdot \frac{\ln\left(1 + \frac{h}{x}\right)}{\frac{h}{x}} = \frac{1}{x} \cdot 1 = \frac{1}{x} $$

(Using standard limit $\lim_{u \to 0} \frac{\ln(1 + u)}{u} = 1$, where $u = \frac{h}{x}$).

3. High-Yield Examples & Application Problems

Problem 3.1: Fundamental Algebraic Fraction

Type: CBSE Board Standard (4 Marks)
Problem: Find the derivative of $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2x + 3}}$ from first principles with respect to $x$.

Detailed Solution:

  1. Definition of derivative:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(x + h) + 3}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2x + 3}}}{h} \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

  1. Combine fractions:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{2x + 3} - \sqrt{2x + 2h + 3}}{h \sqrt{2x + 2h + 3} \sqrt{2x + 3}} $$

  1. Rationalize the numerator:
    Multiply numerator and denominator by $\left(\sqrt{2x + 3} + \sqrt{2x + 2h + 3}\right)$:

$$ = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(2x + 3) - (2x + 2h + 3)}{h \sqrt{2x + 2h + 3}\sqrt{2x + 3}\left(\sqrt{2x + 3} + \sqrt{2x + 2h + 3}\right)} \quad \text{[1.5 Marks]} $$

$$ = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-2h}{h \sqrt{2x + 2h + 3}\sqrt{2x + 3}\left(\sqrt{2x + 3} + \sqrt{2x + 2h + 3}\right)} $$

  1. Cancel $h$ ($h \neq 0$) and evaluate limit:

$$ = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-2}{\sqrt{2x + 2h + 3}\sqrt{2x + 3}\left(\sqrt{2x + 3} + \sqrt{2x + 2h + 3}\right)} \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

$$ = \frac{-2}{\sqrt{2x + 3}\sqrt{2x + 3}\left(\sqrt{2x + 3} + \sqrt{2x + 3}\right)} = \frac{-2}{(2x + 3) \cdot 2\sqrt{2x + 3}} = -\frac{1}{(2x + 3)^{3/2}} \quad \text{[0.5 Mark]} $$

Problem 3.2: Product of Linear and Trigonometric Function

Type: NCERT Exemplar Problem (4 Marks)
Problem: Differentiate $f(x) = x \sin x$ with respect to $x$ from first principles.

Detailed Solution:

  1. Set up the limit:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x + h)\sin(x + h) - x \sin x}{h} $$

  1. Decompose into two workable terms:
    Expand the numerator:

$$ (x + h)\sin(x + h) - x \sin x = x \sin(x + h) + h \sin(x + h) - x \sin x $$

Regroup:

$$ = x [\sin(x + h) - \sin x] + h \sin(x + h) \quad \text{[1.5 Marks]} $$

  1. Substitute into limit quotient:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ x \cdot \frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} + \frac{h \sin(x + h)}{h} \right] \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

  1. Evaluate each limit separately:
  2. First term:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(x + h) - \sin x}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h} = \cos x \cdot 1 = \cos x $$

  • Second term:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} \sin(x + h) = \sin(x + 0) = \sin x $$

  1. Combine results:

$$ f'(x) = x \cos x + \sin x \quad \text{[1.5 Marks]} $$

Problem 3.3: Differentiability at a Corner Point (Absolute Value Function)

Type: NCERT Exemplar / Concept Check (3 Marks)
Problem: Show that the function $f(x) = |x - 2|$ is continuous at $x = 2$, but not differentiable at $x = 2$.

Detailed Solution:

Part A: Continuity at $x = 2$ - $f(2) = |2 - 2| = 0$ - $\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} |(2 + h) - 2| = \lim_{h \to 0^+} h = 0$ - $\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} |(2 - h) - 2| = \lim_{h \to 0^+} |-h| = \lim_{h \to 0^+} h = 0$ Since $\lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = f(2) = 0$, $f(x)$ is continuous at $x = 2$. $\quad \text{[1 Mark]}$

Part B: Differentiability at $x = 2$ 1. Left-Hand Derivative (LHD) at $x = 2$:

$$ L f'(2) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(2 + h) - f(2)}{h} = \lim_{k \to 0^+} \frac{f(2 - k) - f(2)}{-k} $$

$$ = \lim_{k \to 0^+} \frac{|(2 - k) - 2| - 0}{-k} = \lim_{k \to 0^+} \frac{|-k|}{-k} = \lim_{k \to 0^+} \frac{k}{-k} = -1 \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

  1. Right-Hand Derivative (RHD) at $x = 2$:

$$ R f'(2) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(2 + h) - f(2)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|(2 + h) - 2| - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = +1 \quad \text{[1 Mark]} $$

Conclusion:
Since $L f'(2) = -1 \neq +1 = R f'(2)$, the tangent has two conflicting slopes at $x = 2$ (a sharp corner). Hence, $f(x)$ is not differentiable at $x = 2$.

Use this to confirm the result of your multi-step algebraic first-principles derivation.

Algebra of Derivatives: Sum, Difference, Product Rule (Leibnitz Rule) and Quotient Rule

Target Audience: CBSE Class 11 / JEE Foundation
Chapter: Limits and Derivatives (Unit: Calculus)
Topic: Algebra of Derivatives — Sum Rule, Difference Rule, Product Rule (Leibnitz Rule), and Quotient Rule

1. Pedagogical Foundation & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Fundamental Definition of the Derivative at a Point

Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval $(a, b)$, and let $x \in (a, b)$. The derivative of $f$ at the point $x$, denoted by $f'(x)$ or $\dfrac{d}{dx}[f(x)]$, is defined as the limiting value:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

provided this limit exists finitely. This formulation is termed the First Principle of Mathematical Differentiation (or definition from ab initio).

1.2 The Linearity of Differentiation

Calculus operations are rooted in real analysis. The derivative operator $D = \dfrac{d}{dx}$ is a linear operator, meaning it preserves vector-space-like properties of scalar multiplication and vector addition:

  1. Additivity: $D(u + v) = D(u) + D(v)$
  2. Homogeneity: $D(c \cdot u) = c \cdot D(u)$ for any scalar constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$

However, the operator does NOT distribute over multiplication or division:

$$ D(u \cdot v) \neq D(u) \cdot D(v) $$

$$ \left(\frac{u}{v}\right) \neq \frac{D(u)}{D(v)} $$

The breakdown of distribution over products and quotients necessitates the Leibnitz Product Rule and the Quotient Rule.

2. Exhaustive Theorems, Derivations & Proofs

Theorem 1: Derivative of a Constant Multiple of a Function

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐
NCERT Reference: Class 11 Chapter 13 / 21

Statement:

Let $f$ be a differentiable function and let $c \in \mathbb{R}$ be an arbitrary real constant. Then the function $g(x) = c \cdot f(x)$ is differentiable, and:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[c \cdot f(x)] = c \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)] $$

Proof:

Given: A differentiable function $f(x)$, a constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$, and $g(x) = c \cdot f(x)$.
To Prove: $g'(x) = c \cdot f'(x)$.

By the definition of derivative from first principles:

$$ g'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{g(x + h) - g(x)}{h} $$

Substitute $g(x) = c \cdot f(x)$:

$$ g'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{c \cdot f(x + h) - c \cdot f(x)}{h} $$

Factor out the scalar $c$ from the numerator:

$$ g'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} c \left[ \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \right] $$

Applying the limit property $\lim_{x \to a} [c \cdot \phi(x)] = c \cdot \lim_{x \to a} \phi(x)$:

$$ g'(x) = c \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

Since $f$ is differentiable, $\lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = f'(x)$. Hence:

$$ g'(x) = c \cdot f'(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Derivative of Sum of Two Functions (Sum Rule)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Board Frequency: High (Appears frequently in 2-mark conceptual derivation sections)

Statement:

Let $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ be two real-valued functions differentiable on an open interval $I$. Then their sum $f(x) = u(x) + v(x)$ is also differentiable on $I$, and:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[u(x) + v(x)] = \frac{d}{dx}[u(x)] + \frac{d}{dx}[v(x)] $$

Proof:

Given: Differentiable functions $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ such that:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} = u'(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} = v'(x) $$

To Prove: $f'(x) = u'(x) + v'(x)$, where $f(x) = u(x) + v(x)$.

By definition of the first principle:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

Substitute $f(x) = u(x) + v(x)$ and $f(x + h) = u(x + h) + v(x + h)$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{[u(x + h) + v(x + h)] - [u(x) + v(x)]}{h} $$

Regroup the terms in the numerator to pair $u$ and $v$ separately:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{[u(x + h) - u(x)] + [v(x + h) - v(x)]}{h} $$

Distribute the denominator across the grouped terms:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} + \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} \right] $$

By the algebra of limits ($\lim_{x \to a} [A(x) + B(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} A(x) + \lim_{x \to a} B(x)$ provided both limits exist):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} + \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} $$

Substituting the known derivatives of $u(x)$ and $v(x)$:

$$ f'(x) = u'(x) + v'(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: Derivative of Difference of Two Functions (Difference Rule)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

Let $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ be two differentiable functions. Then:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[u(x) - v(x)] = \frac{d}{dx}[u(x)] - \frac{d}{dx}[v(x)] $$

Proof:

Let $f(x) = u(x) - v(x) = u(x) + (-1) \cdot v(x)$.
Applying Theorem 2 (Sum Rule) and Theorem 1 (Constant Multiple Rule with $c = -1$):

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)] = \frac{d}{dx}[u(x)] + \frac{d}{dx}[(-1) \cdot v(x)] = u'(x) + (-1) \cdot v'(x) = u'(x) - v'(x) $$

Alternatively, via First Principles:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{[u(x + h) - v(x + h)] - [u(x) - v(x)]}{h} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} - \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} \right] = u'(x) - v'(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 4: Derivative of Product of Two Functions (Leibnitz Product Rule)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Board Frequency: Extremely High (Crucial for 3-mark & 5-mark theoretical derivations)

Geometric & Physical Intuition:

Consider a rectangle whose adjacent sides have lengths changing with respect to a parameter $x$, say $u(x)$ and $v(x)$. The area of the rectangle is $A(x) = u(x) \cdot v(x)$.
When $x$ increases by an infinitesimal increment $\Delta x$: - Side $u$ expands to $u + \Delta u$ - Side $v$ expands to $v + \Delta v$

The new area is:

$$ A + \Delta A = (u + \Delta u)(v + \Delta v) = u \cdot v + u \cdot \Delta v + v \cdot \Delta u + \Delta u \cdot \Delta v $$

Subtracting $A = u \cdot v$:

$$ \Delta A = u \cdot \Delta v + v \cdot \Delta u + \Delta u \cdot \Delta v $$

Dividing across by $\Delta x$ and taking the limit $\Delta x \to 0$ (noting that $\Delta u \to 0$ as $\Delta x \to 0$):

$$ \frac{dA}{dx} = u \frac{dv}{dx} + v \frac{du}{dx} + 0 $$

Formal Analytical Proof:

Given: Two functions $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ differentiable on an interval $I$.
To Prove:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[u(x) v(x)] = u(x) \frac{d}{dx}[v(x)] + v(x) \frac{d}{dx}[u(x)] $$

Let $f(x) = u(x) \cdot v(x)$.
By definition of derivative:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x + h) v(x + h) - u(x) v(x)}{h} $$

Key Algebraic Maneuver (Add and Subtract $u(x + h) v(x)$ in the numerator):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x + h) v(x + h) - u(x + h) v(x) + u(x + h) v(x) - u(x) v(x)}{h} $$

Group terms into two separate fractions:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \frac{u(x + h)[v(x + h) - v(x)]}{h} + \frac{v(x)[u(x + h) - u(x)]}{h} \right] $$

Apply the sum law of limits:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ u(x + h) \cdot \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} \right] + \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ v(x) \cdot \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} \right] $$

Apply the product law of limits to each term:

$$ f'(x) = \left[ \lim_{h \to 0} u(x + h) \right] \cdot \left[ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} \right] + v(x) \cdot \left[ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} \right] $$

Evaluation of Limits: 1. Since $u$ is differentiable at $x$, it is inherently continuous at $x$. Therefore:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} u(x + h) = u(x) $$

  1. By the first principle:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} = v'(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} = u'(x) $$

Substituting these limit evaluations back:

$$ f'(x) = u(x) \cdot v'(x) + v(x) \cdot u'(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 5: Derivative of Quotient of Two Functions (Quotient Rule)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐
Board Frequency: Extremely High

Statement:

Let $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ be differentiable functions on an open interval $I$, and let $v(x) \neq 0$ for all $x \in I$. Then the quotient $f(x) = \dfrac{u(x)}{v(x)}$ is differentiable, and:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[ \frac{u(x)}{v(x)} \right] = \frac{v(x) \frac{d}{dx}[u(x)] - u(x) \frac{d}{dx}[v(x)]}{[v(x)]^2} = \frac{v(x) u'(x) - u(x) v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2} $$

Proof:

Given: Functions $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ differentiable at $x$, with $v(x) \neq 0$.
To Prove:

$$ \left( \frac{u}{v} \right)' = \frac{v \cdot u' - u \cdot v'}{v^2} $$

Let $f(x) = \dfrac{u(x)}{v(x)}$.
By First Principles:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{u(x + h)}{v(x + h)} - \frac{u(x)}{v(x)}}{h} $$

Find a common denominator in the numerator:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x + h) v(x) - u(x) v(x + h)}{h \cdot v(x + h) v(x)} $$

Key Algebraic Maneuver (Add and Subtract $u(x) v(x)$ in the numerator):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{[u(x + h) v(x) - u(x) v(x)] - [u(x) v(x + h) - u(x) v(x)]}{h \cdot v(x + h) v(x)} $$

Factor $v(x)$ from the first group and $u(x)$ from the second group:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x)[u(x + h) - u(x)] - u(x)[v(x + h) - v(x)]}{h \cdot v(x + h) v(x)} $$

Split into two distinct fractions divided by the denominator:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{v(x + h) v(x)} \cdot \left[ v(x) \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} - u(x) \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} \right] $$

Applying the properties of limits:

$$ f'(x) = \left( \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{v(x + h) v(x)} \right) \cdot \left[ v(x) \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} - u(x) \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} \right] $$

Evaluation of Individual Limits: 1. Since $v$ is differentiable at $x$, it is continuous at $x \implies \lim_{h \to 0} v(x + h) = v(x)$.
Hence:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{v(x + h) v(x)} = \frac{1}{v(x) \cdot v(x)} = \frac{1}{[v(x)]^2} $$

  1. $\lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{u(x + h) - u(x)}{h} = u'(x)$
  2. $\lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{v(x + h) - v(x)}{h} = v'(x)$

Assemble the evaluated limits:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{1}{[v(x)]^2} \left[ v(x) u'(x) - u(x) v'(x) \right] $$

$$ f'(x) = \frac{v(x) u'(x) - u(x) v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.1 Generalization: Product Rule for Three Functions

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐
Let $f(x) = u(x) \cdot v(x) \cdot w(x)$. Treating $u(x) \cdot v(x)$ as a single composite entity $[u \cdot v]$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[(uv) \cdot w] = (uv) \frac{dw}{dx} + w \frac{d}{dx}(uv) $$

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[(uv) \cdot w] = uv \frac{dw}{dx} + w \left( u \frac{dv}{dx} + v \frac{du}{dx} \right) $$

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[u \cdot v \cdot w] = u'vw + uv'w + uvw' $$

By mathematical induction, for $n$ functions:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[u_1 u_2 \dots u_n] = \sum_{i=1}^n u_1 u_2 \dots u_{i-1} u_i' u_{i+1} \dots u_n $$

SECTION 1: IN-DEPTH PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & GEOMETRIC INTUITION

1.1 The Concept of Derivative and Physical/Geometric Meaning

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

Definition (Derivative at a Point):
Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval $(a, b)$ and let $c \in (a, b)$. The derivative of $f$ at $x = c$, denoted by $f'(c)$ or $\left.\frac{df}{dx}\right|_{x=c}$, is defined by:

$$ f'(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h} $$

provided this limit exists finitely.

Geometric Interpretation:
Let $P(c, f(c))$ and $Q(c+h, f(c+h))$ be two neighboring points on the curve $y = f(x)$.
The slope of the secant line passing through $P$ and $Q$ is given by:

$$ m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{(c+h) - c} = \frac{f(c+h) - f(c)}{h} $$

As $h \to 0$, the point $Q$ slides along the curve towards $P$. The limiting position of the secant line $PQ$ becomes the tangent line to the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $P$.
Therefore, the derivative $f'(c)$ represents the exact slope of the tangent to the curve $y = f(x)$ at $(c, f(c))$.

Physical Interpretation:
If $s = f(t)$ represents the position of a particle at time $t$, then $\frac{f(t+h) - f(t)}{h}$ represents the average velocity over the time interval $[t, t+h]$. The limit as $h \to 0$ represents the instantaneous rate of change of displacement with respect to time, which is instantaneous velocity $v(t) = f'(t)$.

1.2 The First Principle of Derivative (ab initio)

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

To compute the derivative of a function $f(x)$ at an arbitrary point $x$ in its domain:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)] = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} $$

This fundamental method of evaluating the derivative via the limit of a difference quotient is known as differentiation from First Principles or ab initio.

1.3 Algebra of Derivatives (Standard Theorems)

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

Let $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ be two differentiable functions.

  1. Linearity Property:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[\alpha u(x) \pm \beta v(x)] = \alpha \frac{du}{dx} \pm \beta \frac{dv}{dx} \quad (\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}) $$

  1. Product Rule (Leibniz Rule):

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[u(x) \cdot v(x)] = u'(x)v(x) + u(x)v'(x) $$

  1. Quotient Rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}\right] = \frac{u'(x)v(x) - u(x)v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2}, \quad \text{provided } v(x) \neq 0 $$

SECTION 2: COMPLETE STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL PROOFS & DERIVATIONS

2.1 Derivative of Constant Function: $f(x) = c$

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $f(x) = c$, where $c$ is an arbitrary constant.
  • To Prove: $f'(x) = 0$.
  • Proof: By the First Principle of Differentiation:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} $$

Since $f(x) = c$ for all $x$, we have $f(x+h) = c$.

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{c - c}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{0}{h} $$

Note that the numerator is identically zero before the limit is taken, whereas $h \to 0$ implies $h \neq 0$.

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} 0 = 0 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.2 Derivative of Power Function: $f(x) = x^n$ ($n \in \mathbb{N}$)

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $f(x) = x^n$, where $n$ is a positive integer.
  • To Prove: $f'(x) = n x^{n-1}$.
  • Proof (Method 1: Using Binomial Theorem): By the definition of derivative:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h} $$

Applying the Binomial Theorem to expand $(x+h)^n$:

$$ (x+h)^n = \binom{n}{0}x^n + \binom{n}{1}x^{n-1}h + \binom{n}{2}x^{n-2}h^2 + \dots + \binom{n}{n}h^n $$

$$ = x^n + n x^{n-1}h + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}x^{n-2}h^2 + \dots + h^n $$

Substitute this expansion into the difference quotient:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\left[x^n + n x^{n-1}h + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}x^{n-2}h^2 + \dots + h^n\right] - x^n}{h} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h \left[n x^{n-1} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}x^{n-2}h + \dots + h^{n-1}\right]}{h} $$

Since $h \to 0$, $h \neq 0$, we cancel $h$ from the numerator and denominator:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[n x^{n-1} + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}x^{n-2}h + \dots + h^{n-1}\right] $$

Evaluating the limit by setting $h = 0$:

$$ f'(x) = n x^{n-1} + 0 + 0 + \dots + 0 = n x^{n-1} $$

  • Alternative Proof (Method 2: Using Standard Algebraic Limit Identity): We know the standard limit formula:

$$ \lim_{z \to a} \frac{z^n - a^n}{z - a} = n a^{n-1} $$

Let $z = x+h$. As $h \to 0$, $z \to x$.

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h} = \lim_{z \to x} \frac{z^n - x^n}{z - x} = n x^{n-1} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.3 Derivative of General Polynomial: $P(x) = \sum_{k=0}^m a_k x^k$

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $P(x) = a_m x^m + a_{m-1}x^{m-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$, with $a_k \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • To Prove: $P'(x) = \sum_{k=1}^m k a_k x^{k-1}$.
  • Proof: Applying the linearity of the differentiation operator $\frac{d}{dx}$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[P(x)] = \frac{d}{dx}\left( \sum_{k=0}^m a_k x^k \right) = \sum_{k=0}^m a_k \frac{d}{dx}[x^k] $$

For $k = 0$: $\frac{d}{dx}[a_0 x^0] = \frac{d}{dx}[a_0] = 0$.
For $k \ge 1$: By the power rule, $\frac{d}{dx}[x^k] = k x^{k-1}$.

$$ \therefore P'(x) = \sum_{k=1}^m k a_k x^{k-1} = m a_m x^{m-1} + (m-1)a_{m-1}x^{m-2} + \dots + 2a_2 x + a_1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.4 Fundamental Trigonometric Limits (Prerequisites for Trigonometric Proofs)

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

Before deriving derivatives of trigonometric functions, the following standard limits are established: 1. $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$ ($\theta$ in radians) 2. $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos \theta}{\theta} = \lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{2\sin^2(\theta/2)}{\theta} = \lim_{\theta \to 0} \left[ \frac{\sin(\theta/2)}{\theta/2} \right] \cdot \sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = 1 \cdot 0 = 0$

2.5 Derivative of $\sin x$

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $f(x) = \sin x$.
  • To Prove: $f'(x) = \cos x$.
  • Proof: By the First Principle:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(x+h) - \sin x}{h} $$

Using the trigonometric transformation formula:

$$ \sin C - \sin D = 2 \cos\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) $$

Setting $C = x+h$ and $D = x$:

$$ \sin(x+h) - \sin x = 2 \cos\left(\frac{2x+h}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) $$

Substituting this into the difference quotient:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h} $$

Rewrite the expression to isolate the standard limit:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \right] $$

Using the product law of limits $\lim(u \cdot v) = \lim u \cdot \lim v$:

$$ f'(x) = \left[ \lim_{h \to 0} \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \right] \cdot \left[ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \right] $$

Since $\cos x$ is continuous, $\lim_{h \to 0} \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) = \cos x$.
Since $h \to 0 \implies \frac{h}{2} \to 0$, we have $\lim_{h/2 \to 0} \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} = 1$.

$$ f'(x) = \cos x \cdot 1 = \cos x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.6 Derivative of $\cos x$

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $f(x) = \cos x$.
  • To Prove: $f'(x) = -\sin x$.
  • Proof: By the First Principle:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\cos(x+h) - \cos x}{h} $$

Using the trigonometric transformation formula:

$$ \cos C - \cos D = -2 \sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) $$

Setting $C = x+h$ and $D = x$:

$$ \cos(x+h) - \cos x = -2 \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) $$

Substituting into the difference quotient:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-2 \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h} $$

$$ f'(x) = -\lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \right] $$

$$ f'(x) = -\left[ \lim_{h \to 0} \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \right] \cdot \left[ \lim_{h/2 \to 0} \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \right] $$

$$ f'(x) = -[\sin x] \cdot [1] = -\sin x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.7 Derivative of $\tan x$

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $f(x) = \tan x$, where $x \neq (2n+1)\frac{\pi}{2}, n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • To Prove: $f'(x) = \sec^2 x$.
  • Proof (Method 1: First Principle):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\tan(x+h) - \tan x}{h} $$

Convert into sine and cosine:

$$ \tan(x+h) - \tan x = \frac{\sin(x+h)}{\cos(x+h)} - \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{\sin(x+h)\cos x - \cos(x+h)\sin x}{\cos(x+h)\cos x} $$

Using the addition formula $\sin(A - B) = \sin A \cos B - \cos A \sin B$:

$$ \sin((x+h) - x) = \sin h $$

Therefore:

$$ \tan(x+h) - \tan x = \frac{\sin h}{\cos(x+h)\cos x} $$

Substitute into the limit definition:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h}{h \cdot \cos(x+h)\cos x} = \lim_{h \to 0} \left( \frac{\sin h}{h} \right) \cdot \lim_{h \to 0} \left( \frac{1}{\cos(x+h)\cos x} \right) $$

$$ f'(x) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{\cos(x+0)\cos x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x $$

  • Proof (Method 2: Using Quotient Rule):

$$ f(x) = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} $$

Let $u(x) = \sin x \implies u'(x) = \cos x$, and $v(x) = \cos x \implies v'(x) = -\sin x$.

$$ f'(x) = \frac{u'(x)v(x) - u(x)v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2} = \frac{(\cos x)(\cos x) - (\sin x)(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} $$

$$ f'(x) = \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.8 Derivative of $\cot x$

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $f(x) = \cot x = \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}$, where $x \neq n\pi, n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • To Prove: $f'(x) = -\csc^2 x$.
  • Proof: Applying the Quotient Rule with $u = \cos x$ and $v = \sin x$:

$$ u'(x) = -\sin x, \quad v'(x) = \cos x $$

$$ f'(x) = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} = \frac{(-\sin x)(\sin x) - (\cos x)(\cos x)}{\sin^2 x} $$

$$ f'(x) = \frac{-\sin^2 x - \cos^2 x}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{-(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x)}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{-1}{\sin^2 x} = -\csc^2 x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.9 Derivative of $\sec x$

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $f(x) = \sec x = \frac{1}{\cos x}$, where $x \neq (2n+1)\frac{\pi}{2}, n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • To Prove: $f'(x) = \sec x \tan x$.
  • Proof (By First Principle):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sec(x+h) - \sec x}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{\cos(x+h)} - \frac{1}{\cos x}}{h} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\cos x - \cos(x+h)}{h \cos(x+h)\cos x} $$

Using $\cos C - \cos D = 2 \sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{D-C}{2}\right)$:

$$ \cos x - \cos(x+h) = 2 \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) $$

Substitute back:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \cdot \frac{\sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)}{\cos(x+h)\cos x} \right] $$

Evaluating the limits:

$$ f'(x) = 1 \cdot \frac{\sin x}{\cos x \cdot \cos x} = \left(\frac{1}{\cos x}\right)\left(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right) = \sec x \tan x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.10 Derivative of $\csc x$

$$ \text{Star Priority: } \bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar $$

  • Given: $f(x) = \csc x = \frac{1}{\sin x}$, where $x \neq n\pi, n \in \mathbb{Z}$.
  • To Prove: $f'(x) = -\csc x \cot x$.
  • Proof (By First Principle):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\csc(x+h) - \csc x}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{\sin(x+h)} - \frac{1}{\sin x}}{h} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin x - \sin(x+h)}{h \sin(x+h)\sin x} $$

Using $\sin C - \sin D = 2 \cos\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right)$:

$$ \sin x - \sin(x+h) = -2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) $$

$$ f'(x) = -\lim_{h \to 0} \left[ \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} \cdot \frac{\cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)}{\sin(x+h)\sin x} \right] $$

Evaluating limits as $h \to 0$:

$$ f'(x) = - (1) \cdot \frac{\cos x}{\sin x \cdot \sin x} = -\left(\frac{1}{\sin x}\right)\left(\frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\right) = -\csc x \cot x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Problem 1 (Direct First Principle Evaluation - NCERT Standard)

Question: Differentiate $f(x) = \sqrt{\sin x}$ from the first principle at all points where $\sin x > 0$.
Marks Breakdown (CBSE 4 Marks):
- Setup difference quotient: 1 Mark
- Rationalization of radical terms: 1 Mark
- Trigonometric identity + standard limit application: 1.5 Marks
- Final correct expression: 0.5 Mark

Solution:
By definition of First Principle:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{\sin(x+h)} - \sqrt{\sin x}}{h} $$

Multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugate $(\sqrt{\sin(x+h)} + \sqrt{\sin x})$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{[\sin(x+h) - \sin x]}{h \cdot [\sqrt{\sin(x+h)} + \sqrt{\sin x}]} $$

Applying $\sin(x+h) - \sin x = 2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h \cdot [\sqrt{\sin(x+h)} + \sqrt{\sin x}]} $$

Regrouping terms:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[\frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2}\right] \cdot \lim_{h \to 0}\left[\frac{\cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\sin(x+h)} + \sqrt{\sin x}}\right] $$

Evaluating the limits:

$$ f'(x) = 1 \cdot \frac{\cos x}{\sqrt{\sin x} + \sqrt{\sin x}} = \frac{\cos x}{2\sqrt{\sin x}} $$

Problem 2 (NCERT Exemplar - Product and Quotient Rules Combination)

Question: Differentiate $y = \frac{x^5 - \cos x}{\sin x}$ with respect to $x$.
Marks Breakdown (CBSE 3 Marks):
- Applying quotient rule formula correctly: 1 Mark
- Differentiating numerator and denominator terms: 1 Mark
- Final algebraic simplification: 1 Mark

Solution:
Let $u(x) = x^5 - \cos x$ and $v(x) = \sin x$.

$$ u'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}(x^5) - \frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = 5x^4 - (-\sin x) = 5x^4 + \sin x $$

$$ v'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x $$

By the Quotient Rule:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{u'(x)v(x) - u(x)v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2} $$

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{(5x^4 + \sin x)(\sin x) - (x^5 - \cos x)(\cos x)}{\sin^2 x} $$

Expanding the terms:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{5x^4 \sin x + \sin^2 x - x^5 \cos x + \cos^2 x}{\sin^2 x} $$

Using $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{5x^4 \sin x - x^5 \cos x + 1}{\sin^2 x} $$

Problem 3 (Rational Power Function by First Principle)

Question: Find the derivative of $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^3}$ ($x \neq 0$) using First Principles.
Marks Breakdown (CBSE 3 Marks):
- Setup difference quotient: 1 Mark
- Common denominator and factorization/simplification: 1 Mark
- Limit evaluation and final answer: 1 Mark

Solution:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{(x+h)^3} - \frac{1}{x^3}}{h} $$

$$ = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^3 - (x+h)^3}{h \cdot x^3 (x+h)^3} $$

Expanding $(x+h)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3$:

$$ x^3 - (x+h)^3 = -(3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3) = -h(3x^2 + 3xh + h^2) $$

Substitute back:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-h(3x^2 + 3xh + h^2)}{h \cdot x^3 (x+h)^3} $$

Canceling $h$ ($h \neq 0$):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-(3x^2 + 3xh + h^2)}{x^3(x+h)^3} $$

Taking the limit as $h \to 0$:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{-(3x^2 + 0 + 0)}{x^3(x+0)^3} = \frac{-3x^2}{x^6} = -\frac{3}{x^4} = -3x^{-4} $$

(Matches the power formula $\frac{d}{dx}[x^{-3}] = -3x^{-3-1} = -3x^{-4}$)

Problem 4 (First Principle for Composite Trigonometric Form)

Question: Differentiate $f(x) = x \sin x$ with respect to $x$ using First Principles.
Marks Breakdown (CBSE 4 Marks):
- Difference quotient setup: 1 Mark
- Strategic addition/subtraction to separate terms: 1.5 Marks
- Limit evaluation of both components: 1.5 Marks

Solution:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h)\sin(x+h) - x\sin x}{h} $$

Add and subtract $x\sin(x+h)$ in the numerator:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x\sin(x+h) - x\sin x + h\sin(x+h)}{h} $$

Split into two distinct limits:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x[\sin(x+h) - \sin x]}{h} + \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h\sin(x+h)}{h} $$

For the first term, factor out $x$ and apply the sine transformation:

$$ I_1 = x \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2 \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h} = x \lim_{h \to 0} \cos\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \cdot \lim_{h/2 \to 0} \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2} = x \cos x \cdot 1 = x \cos x $$

For the second term, cancel $h$:

$$ I_2 = \lim_{h \to 0} \sin(x+h) = \sin x $$

Combining $I_1$ and $I_2$:

$$ f'(x) = x \cos x + \sin x $$

💡 Example 3.1: Fundamental Angle Transformation (Degrees vs Radians)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x^\circ}{x} $$

Solution: - Step 1: Note that the limit formula $\lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$ applies only when $\theta$ is in radians. - Step 2: Convert $x^\circ$ into radians:

$$ 180^\circ = \pi \text{ radians} \implies 1^\circ = \frac{\pi}{180} \text{ radians} \implies x^\circ = \frac{\pi x}{180} \text{ radians} $$

  • Step 3: Substitute back into the limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{180}\right)}{x} $$

  • Step 4: Balance the denominator to match the angle:

$$ = \lim_{x \to 0} \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{180}\right)}{\frac{\pi x}{180}} \times \frac{\pi}{180}\right] $$

$$ = \frac{\pi}{180} \cdot \lim_{\frac{\pi x}{180} \to 0} \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{180}\right)}{\frac{\pi x}{180}}\right] = \frac{\pi}{180} \times 1 = \frac{\pi}{180} $$

💡 Example 3.2: Direct Application of Sandwich Theorem (NCERT Exemplar)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Show that:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0 $$

Solution: - Step 1: Observe that direct substitution is undefined because $\frac{1}{0}$ is undefined and $\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ oscillates violently between $-1$ and $1$. - Step 2: Formulate the bounding inequality for the sine function:

$$ -1 \le \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \le 1 \quad \forall x \neq 0 $$

  • Step 3: Multiply throughout by $x^2$. Since $x^2 > 0$ for all real $x \neq 0$, the inequality directions do not change:

$$ -x^2 \le x^2 \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \le x^2 $$

  • Step 4: Evaluate the limits of the bounding functions as $x \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} (-x^2) = -(0)^2 = 0 $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} (x^2) = (0)^2 = 0 $$

  • Step 5: Conclude using the Sandwich Theorem: Since $\lim_{x \to 0} (-x^2) = \lim_{x \to 0} (x^2) = 0$, it follows that:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0 $$

Board Marking Scheme Breakdown: - Stating the bound $-1 \le \sin(1/x) \le 1$: [1 Mark] - Multiplying by $x^2$ and stating $x^2 > 0$: [1 Mark] - Explicit invocation of the Sandwich Theorem and computing outer limits: [1 Mark]

💡 Example 3.3: Multiple Angle Matching & Difference of Cosines
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos ax - \cos bx}{x^2} $$

Solution: - Method A (Using Standard Identity): Add and subtract $1$ in the numerator:

$$ \frac{\cos ax - \cos bx}{x^2} = \frac{(1 - \cos bx) - (1 - \cos ax)}{x^2} = \frac{1 - \cos bx}{x^2} - \frac{1 - \cos ax}{x^2} $$

Now manipulate each term to invoke $\lim_{u \to 0}\frac{1 - \cos u}{u^2} = \frac{1}{2}$:

$$ = b^2 \left[\frac{1 - \cos bx}{(bx)^2}\right] - a^2 \left[\frac{1 - \cos ax}{(ax)^2}\right] $$

Taking limits as $x \to 0$:

$$ = b^2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) - a^2\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{b^2 - a^2}{2} $$

  • Method B (Using $C-D$ Formula): Recall: $\cos C - \cos D = -2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) = 2\sin\left(\frac{a+b}{2}x\right)\sin\left(\frac{b-a}{2}x\right)$

$$ \frac{\cos ax - \cos bx}{x^2} = \frac{2\sin\left(\frac{a+b}{2}x\right)\sin\left(\frac{b-a}{2}x\right)}{x^2} $$

$$ = 2 \cdot \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{a+b}{2}x\right)}{x}\right] \cdot \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{b-a}{2}x\right)}{x}\right] $$

$$ = 2 \cdot \left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right) \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{a+b}{2}x\right)}{\frac{a+b}{2}x}\right] \cdot \left(\frac{b-a}{2}\right) \left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{b-a}{2}x\right)}{\frac{b-a}{2}x}\right] $$

Taking limits:

$$ = 2 \cdot \left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)(1) \cdot \left(\frac{b-a}{2}\right)(1) = \frac{(b+a)(b-a)}{2} = \frac{b^2 - a^2}{2} $$

💡 Example 3.4: Limits at Non-Zero Points via Substitution
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to \frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{1 - \sin x}{\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)^2} $$

💡 Example 3.5: Rationalization Combined with Trigonometric Limits
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Evaluate:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{1 + \sin x} - \sqrt{1 - \sin x}}{x} $$

Solution: - Step 1: Multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the numerator:

$$ \frac{\sqrt{1 + \sin x} - \sqrt{1 - \sin x}}{x} \times \frac{\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x}}{\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x}} $$

  • Step 2: Simplify the numerator using $(A - B)(A + B) = A^2 - B^2$:

$$ = \frac{(1 + \sin x) - (1 - \sin x)}{x\left(\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x}\right)} = \frac{2\sin x}{x\left(\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x}\right)} $$

$$ = 2 \cdot (1) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+0} + \sqrt{1-0}}\right) = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1 $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

⚠️ Trap 1: Assuming the Limit Identity Holds Away from Zero

  • The Error: A student writes:

$$ \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1 \quad \mathbf{[FATAL ERROR]} $$

  • The Truth: The identity $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$ is valid only when the argument approaches zero!
  • Correct Evaluation for $x \to \infty$: By the Sandwich Theorem, since $-1 \le \sin x \le 1$:

$$ -\frac{1}{x} \le \frac{\sin x}{x} \le \frac{1}{x} $$

As $x \to \infty$, $-\frac{1}{x} \to 0$ and $\frac{1}{x} \to 0$. Therefore:

$$ \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 0 $$

⚠️ Trap 2: Mismatched Powers in Cosine Limits

  • The Error: Writing $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = \frac{1}{2}$.
  • The Truth: Check the denominator power carefully:
  • Linear denominator: $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0$
  • Quadratic denominator: $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2}$

⚠️ Trap 3: Argument Mismatch in Denominators

  • The Error: Writing $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 5x}{x} = 1$.
  • The Truth: The denominator must match the argument of the trigonometric function identically:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 5x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} 5 \cdot \left[\frac{\sin 5x}{5x}\right] = 5 \cdot 1 = 5 $$

⚠️ Trap 4: Forgetting the Modulus When Resolving $\sqrt{1 - \cos 2x}$

  • The Error:

$$ \sqrt{1 - \cos 2x} = \sqrt{2\sin^2 x} = \sqrt{2}\sin x \quad \text{[INCORRECT FOR NEGATIVE x\mathbf{]}} $$

  • The Truth: By definition, for any real $u$, $\sqrt{u^2} = |u|$.

$$ \sqrt{1 - \cos 2x} = \sqrt{2}|\sin x| $$

  • Consequence on Left/Right Limits: Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{1 - \cos 2x}}{x}$:
  • RHL ($x \to 0^+$): $\sin x > 0 \implies |\sin x| = \sin x \implies \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\sqrt{2}\sin x}{x} = \sqrt{2}$
  • LHL ($x \to 0^-$): $\sin x < 0 \implies |\sin x| = -\sin x \implies \lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{-\sqrt{2}\sin x}{x} = -\sqrt{2}$
  • Since $\text{LHL} \neq \text{RHL}$, the limit does not exist (DNE)!

💡 Example 2: Non-Trivial Quotient Rule (NCERT Exemplar)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Differentiate $f(x) = \dfrac{x + \cos x}{\tan x - 2x^3}$ with respect to $x$ for all $x$ where the denominator is non-zero.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:

Let:

$$ u(x) = x + \cos x \implies u'(x) = 1 - \sin x $$

$$ v(x) = \tan x - 2x^3 \implies v'(x) = \sec^2 x - 6x^2 $$

Apply the Quotient Rule formula:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{v(x) u'(x) - u(x) v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2} $$

Substitute $u(x), v(x), u'(x)$, and $v'(x)$:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{(\tan x - 2x^3)(1 - \sin x) - (x + \cos x)(\sec^2 x - 6x^2)}{(\tan x - 2x^3)^2} $$

Expand the numerator methodically: Part A:

$$ (\tan x - 2x^3)(1 - \sin x) = \tan x - \tan x \sin x - 2x^3 + 2x^3 \sin x $$

Part B:

$$ (x + \cos x)(\sec^2 x - 6x^2) = x \sec^2 x - 6x^3 + \cos x \sec^2 x - 6x^2 \cos x $$

Note that $\cos x \sec^2 x = \cos x \cdot \dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec x$. Therefore:

$$ = x \sec^2 x - 6x^3 + \sec x - 6x^2 \cos x $$

Assemble Numerator ($\text{Part A} - \text{Part B}$):

$$ \text{Num} = \tan x - \tan x \sin x - 2x^3 + 2x^3 \sin x - x \sec^2 x + 6x^3 - \sec x + 6x^2 \cos x $$

Combine the $x^3$ terms: $-2x^3 + 6x^3 = 4x^3$.

$$ \text{Num} = 4x^3 + \tan x - \sec x - \tan x \sin x + 2x^3 \sin x - x \sec^2 x + 6x^2 \cos x $$

Thus:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{4x^3 + \tan x - \sec x - \tan x \sin x + 2x^3 \sin x - x \sec^2 x + 6x^2 \cos x}{(\tan x - 2x^3)^2} $$

💡 Example 4: Rigorous First Principle Evaluation of a Quotient
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Differentiate $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x^2 + 1}$ strictly from First Principles.

Detailed Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Set up the definition from first principles:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

Step 2: Substitute $f(x)$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{(x + h)^2 + 1} - \frac{1}{x^2 + 1}}{h} $$

Step 3: Combine numerators over a common denominator:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x^2 + 1) - [(x + h)^2 + 1]}{h \cdot [(x + h)^2 + 1](x^2 + 1)} $$

Step 4: Expand and simplify the numerator:

$$ (x^2 + 1) - (x^2 + 2xh + h^2 + 1) = x^2 + 1 - x^2 - 2xh - h^2 - 1 = -2xh - h^2 = -h(2x + h) $$

Step 5: Substitute back and cancel the singular factor $h$ ($h \neq 0$):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-h(2x + h)}{h \cdot [(x + h)^2 + 1](x^2 + 1)} $$

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-(2x + h)}{[(x + h)^2 + 1](x^2 + 1)} $$

Step 6: Evaluate the limit as $h \to 0$:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{-(2x + 0)}{[(x + 0)^2 + 1](x^2 + 1)} = \frac{-2x}{(x^2 + 1)(x^2 + 1)} = \frac{-2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2} $$

Verification via Quotient Rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[ \frac{1}{x^2 + 1} \right] = \frac{(x^2 + 1)\cdot 0 - 1 \cdot (2x)}{(x^2 + 1)^2} = \frac{-2x}{(x^2 + 1)^2} \quad \checkmark $$

Pitfall 1: Sign Reversal in Quotient Rule Numerator

  • Fatal Error: Writing $\dfrac{u v' - v u'}{v^2}$ instead of $\dfrac{v u' - u v'}{v^2}$.
  • Consequence: Yields an answer identical in magnitude but exactly inverted in sign ($-f'(x)$ instead of $f'(x)$), resulting in a loss of 2 out of 3 marks in board evaluations.
  • Remedy: Anchor your execution on the phrase: "The denominator starts the numerator!" $\implies v \cdot u'$.

Pitfall 2: Denominator Not Squared or Erroneously Differentiated

  • Error: Writing $\dfrac{v u' - u v'}{v'^2}$ or forgetting the square: $\dfrac{v u' - u v'}{v}$.
  • Remedy: The denominator is strictly $[v(x)]^2$. The denominator function $v(x)$ is never differentiated in the denominator of the quotient rule.

Pitfall 3: Distributing Derivative to Product Terms

  • Error: $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2 \sin x) = (2x)(\cos x)$.
  • Analysis: Students inadvertently treat differentiation like algebraic brackets. Remind students that rates of change compound across interactions: $(u \cdot v)' = u'v + uv'$.

Pitfall 4: Missing the Chain Interaction on Constants

  • Error: Applying the product rule on $c \cdot f(x)$ as:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[5 x^3] = \frac{d}{dx}(5) \cdot x^3 + 5 \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x^3) = 0 \cdot x^3 + 5(3x^2) = 15x^2 $$

While mathematically valid, it wastes time. When one term is a pure constant, invoke the Constant Multiple Rule directly:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[c \cdot f(x)] = c \cdot f'(x) $$

5. Speed Hacks, Shortcuts & Memory Mnemonics

5.1 The "Low-D-High" Mnemonic for Quotient Rule

To never invert the numerator order, commit this classic rhythm to memory:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[ \frac{\text{High}}{\text{Low}} \right] = \frac{\text{Low } d(\text{High}) - \text{High } d(\text{Low})}{\text{Low } \cdot \text{Low}} $$

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{\text{Hi}}{\text{Lo}}\right] = \frac{\text{Lo} \cdot d\text{Hi} - \text{Hi} \cdot d\text{Lo}}{\text{Lo}^2} $$

5.2 The Logarithmic Derivative Trick (JEE Speed Hack)

When differentiating products of multiple terms, normal product rule expansions become bulky. For non-zero functions:

If $y = f_1 \cdot f_2 \cdot f_3 \dots f_n$, take the natural logarithm:

$$ \ln |y| = \sum_{k=1}^n \ln |f_k| $$

Differentiating implicitly with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{y'}{y} = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{f_k'}{f_k} \implies y' = \left(\prod_{k=1}^n f_k \right) \left[ \frac{f_1'}{f_1} + \frac{f_2'}{f_2} + \dots + \frac{f_n'}{f_n} \right] $$

Instant Mental Execution:

Find $\dfrac{d}{dx}[x(x + 1)(x + 2)]$:

$$ y' = x(x+1)(x+2) \left[ \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{x+1} + \frac{1}{x+2} \right] $$

$$ y' = (x+1)(x+2) + x(x+2) + x(x+1) = 3x^2 + 6x + 2 $$

5.3 Determinant Shortcut for Bilinear Quotients

For functions of the linear rational form:

$$ f(x) = \frac{a x + b}{c x + d} $$

The derivative can be written down directly using a $2 \times 2$ determinant:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix}}{(cx + d)^2} = \frac{ad - bc}{(cx + d)^2} $$

Instant Verification:

Let $f(x) = \dfrac{3x + 4}{5x - 2}$.

$$ f'(x) = \frac{(3)(-2) - (4)(5)}{(5x - 2)^2} = \frac{-6 - 20}{(5x - 2)^2} = \frac{-26}{(5x - 2)^2} $$

Computation time: 3 seconds.

Question A.1: Case-Based / Passage-Integrated Question

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, 4 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Context / Passage:

An aerospace startup is designing an experimental sounding rocket launched vertically upward from the ground. During the burn phase ($0 \le t \le 10$ seconds), telemetry systems track the vertical displacement $s(t)$ (in meters) and fuel consumption dynamics. The displacement is modeled by:

$$ s(t) = \begin{cases} \alpha t^2 + \beta t, & 0 \le t \le 4 \\ \gamma t^3 + 12t + \delta, & 4 < t \le 10 \end{cases} $$

For smooth flight dynamics and structural integrity, the velocity of the rocket must exist and be continuous across the transition boundary at $t = 4\text{ s}$. Furthermore, at $t = 0$, the rocket starts from rest ($s(0) = 0$ and initial velocity $v(0) = 0$). Ground radar data confirms that at the handover instant $t = 4\text{ s}$, the rocket reaches an instantaneous altitude of $s(4) = 64\text{ m}$.

Sub-Questions:

  1. Using the initial conditions at $t = 0$, evaluate the parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$. (1 Mark)
  2. Utilizing the physical requirement of altitude continuity at $t = 4\text{ s}$, establish an equation relating $\gamma$ and $\delta$. (1 Mark)
  3. Determine the numerical values of $\gamma$ and $\delta$ so that the rocket's velocity $v(t) = s'(t)$ exists at $t = 4\text{ s}$. (1 Mark)
  4. Calculate the instantaneous acceleration of the rocket at $t = 5\text{ s}$. (1 Mark)
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme:

Sub-question 1:
  • At $t = 0$, $s(0) = \alpha(0)^2 + \beta(0) = 0$ (identically satisfied).
  • The velocity for $t \in [0, 4)$ is:

$$ v(t) = \frac{d}{dt}(\alpha t^2 + \beta t) = 2\alpha t + \beta $$

  • Given initial velocity $v(0) = 0$:

$$ 2\alpha(0) + \beta = 0 \implies \beta = 0 $$

  • We are given $s(4) = 64\text{ m}$:

$$ \alpha(4)^2 + 0(4) = 64 \implies 16\alpha = 64 \implies \alpha = 4 $$

  • Marking Scheme:
  • Finding $\beta = 0$ using $v(0)=0$: [½ Mark]
  • Substituting $t=4$ into $s(t)$ to obtain $\alpha = 4$: [½ Mark]
Sub-question 2:
  • For the displacement function to be continuous at $t = 4$:

$$ \lim_{t \to 4^-} s(t) = \lim_{t \to 4^+} s(t) = s(4) $$

  • Left-Hand Limit: $\lim_{t \to 4^-} s(t) = 4(4)^2 = 64$
  • Right-Hand Limit: $\lim_{t \to 4^+} s(t) = \gamma(4)^3 + 12(4) + \delta = 64\gamma + 48 + \delta$
  • Equating limits:

$$ 64\gamma + 48 + \delta = 64 \implies 64\gamma + \delta = 16 $$

  • Marking Scheme:
  • Stating continuity condition $\lim_{t\to 4^-} s(t) = \lim_{t\to 4^+} s(t)$: [½ Mark]
  • Setting up correct linear relation $64\gamma + \delta = 16$: [½ Mark]
Sub-question 3:
  • For velocity to exist at $t = 4$, $s'(4)$ must exist, meaning $L[s'(4)] = R[s'(4)]$:

$$ L[s'(4)] = \left.\frac{d}{dt}(4t^2)\right|_{t=4} = 8(4) = 32\text{ m/s} $$

$$ R[s'(4)] = \left.\frac{d}{dt}(\gamma t^3 + 12t + \delta)\right|_{t=4} = 3\gamma(4)^2 + 12 = 48\gamma + 12 $$

  • Equating the one-sided derivatives:

$$ 48\gamma + 12 = 32 \implies 48\gamma = 20 \implies \gamma = \frac{20}{48} = \frac{5}{12} $$

  • Substitute $\gamma = \frac{5}{12}$ into $64\gamma + \delta = 16$:

$$ 64\left(\frac{5}{12}\right) + \delta = 16 \implies \frac{80}{3} + \delta = 16 \implies \delta = 16 - \frac{80}{3} = -\frac{32}{3} $$

  • Marking Scheme:
  • Formulating differentiability condition $L[s'(4)] = R[s'(4)]$ and finding $\gamma = \frac{5}{12}$: [½ Mark]
  • Evaluating $\delta = -\frac{32}{3}$: [½ Mark]
Sub-question 4:
  • For $t \in (4, 10]$, $s(t) = \frac{5}{12}t^3 + 12t - \frac{32}{3}$.
  • The velocity is:

$$ v(t) = s'(t) = 3\left(\frac{5}{12}\right)t^2 + 12 = \frac{5}{4}t^2 + 12 $$

  • The instantaneous acceleration $a(t)$ is:

$$ a(t) = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{5}{4}t^2 + 12\right) = \frac{5}{2}t $$

  • At $t = 5\text{ s}$:

$$ a(5) = \frac{5}{2}(5) = \frac{25}{2} = 12.5\text{ m/s}^2 $$

  • Marking Scheme:
  • Finding the derivative of velocity $a(t) = \frac{5}{2}t$: [½ Mark]
  • Correct final evaluation $12.5\text{ m/s}^2$ with proper units: [½ Mark]

Question A.2: Case-Based / Passage-Integrated Question

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE Competency Pattern, 4 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Context / Passage:

In optical telecommunications, an ultra-short optical pulse traverses a dispersive nonlinear optical fiber. The phase delay function $\Phi(\omega)$ across angular frequency $\omega$ in the neighborhood of the carrier frequency $\omega_0 = 0$ is governed by:

$$ f(x) = \frac{\sqrt{1 + \sin x} - \sqrt{1 - \sin x}}{x} $$

Engineers must characterize the behavior of this transmission signal at baseline $x \to 0$ and examine its operational derivative to calibrate chromatic dispersion compensators.

Sub-Questions:

  1. Show that $f(x)$ represents an indeterminate form as $x \to 0$, and identify the form. (1 Mark)
  2. Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ analytically using algebraic rationalization. (1 Mark)
  3. Alternatively, evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ by expressing $1 \pm \sin x$ as perfect trigonometric squares for $x \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, showing left-hand and right-hand limits. (1 Mark)
  4. If $g(x) = x \cdot f(x) = \sqrt{1+\sin x} - \sqrt{1-\sin x}$, evaluate $g'(0)$ using the First Principle of Derivatives. (1 Mark)
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme:

Sub-question 1:
  • As $x \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} (\sqrt{1 + \sin x} - \sqrt{1 - \sin x}) = \sqrt{1+0} - \sqrt{1-0} = 1 - 1 = 0 $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} x = 0 $$

  • Thus, $\lim_{x \to 0} f(x)$ takes the indeterminate form $\left[\frac{0}{0}\right]$.
  • Marking Scheme:
  • Substitution showing numerator $\to 0$ and denominator $\to 0$: [½ Mark]
  • Explicit identification of $\left[\frac{0}{0}\right]$ form: [½ Mark]
Sub-question 2:
  • Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate $(\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x})$:

$$ f(x) = \frac{(\sqrt{1 + \sin x} - \sqrt{1 - \sin x})(\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x})}{x(\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x})} $$

$$ = \frac{(1 + \sin x) - (1 - \sin x)}{x(\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x})} = \frac{2\sin x}{x(\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x})} $$

  • Taking the limit as $x \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = 2 \cdot \left(\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x}\right) \cdot \left(\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \sin x} + \sqrt{1 - \sin x}}\right) $$

$$ = 2 \cdot (1) \cdot \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+0} + \sqrt{1-0}}\right) = 2 \cdot 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = 1 $$

  • Marking Scheme:
  • Rationalization step and simplification to $\frac{2\sin x}{x(\dots)}$: [½ Mark]
  • Application of standard limit $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ and final result $1$: [½ Mark]
Sub-question 3:
  • Using the identities $1 \pm \sin x = \cos^2 \frac{x}{2} + \sin^2 \frac{x}{2} \pm 2\sin \frac{x}{2}\cos \frac{x}{2} = \left(\cos \frac{x}{2} \pm \sin \frac{x}{2}\right)^2$:

$$ \sqrt{1 \pm \sin x} = \left|\cos \frac{x}{2} \pm \sin \frac{x}{2}\right| $$

  • For $x \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, $\frac{x}{2} \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$, so $\cos \frac{x}{2} > 0$ and $\cos \frac{x}{2} > |\sin \frac{x}{2}|$.
  • Hence:

$$ \sqrt{1+\sin x} = \cos \frac{x}{2} + \sin \frac{x}{2}, \quad \sqrt{1-\sin x} = \cos \frac{x}{2} - \sin \frac{x}{2} $$

  • Numerator:

$$ (\cos \frac{x}{2} + \sin \frac{x}{2}) - (\cos \frac{x}{2} - \sin \frac{x}{2}) = 2\sin \frac{x}{2} $$

  • Limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{2\sin \frac{x}{2}}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin \frac{x}{2}}{\frac{x}{2}} = 1 $$

  • Marking Scheme:
  • Correct conversion to perfect square with absolute value domain justification: [½ Mark]
  • Evaluation of $\lim_{x\to 0} \frac{\sin(x/2)}{x/2} = 1$: [½ Mark]
Sub-question 4:
  • $g(x) = \sqrt{1+\sin x} - \sqrt{1-\sin x}$, so $g(0) = 1 - 1 = 0$.
  • By definition of derivative at $x = 0$:

$$ g'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{g(0+h) - g(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{1+\sin h} - \sqrt{1-\sin h} - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} f(h) $$

  • From Sub-question 2, $\lim_{h \to 0} f(h) = 1$. Therefore, $g'(0) = 1$.
  • Marking Scheme:
  • Stating the first principle definition $g'(0) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{g(h)-g(0)}{h}$: [½ Mark]
  • Identifying the equivalence to Sub-question 2 and concluding $g'(0) = 1$: [½ Mark]

Question A.3: Assertion-Reason Set

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE New Pattern, 1 Mark Each]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Directions:

In each of the following questions, a statement of Assertion (A) is followed by a statement of Reason (R). Choose the correct option: - (a) Both (A) and (R) are true, and (R) is the correct explanation of (A). - (b) Both (A) and (R) are true, but (R) is NOT the correct explanation of (A). - (c) (A) is true, but (R) is false. - (d) (A) is false, but (R) is true.

Item 1:

  • Assertion (A): The limit $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{|x|}{x}$ does not exist.
  • Reason (R): For any real-valued function $f(x)$, $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists if and only if both left-hand limit $\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x)$ and right-hand limit $\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x)$ exist and are equal to a finite real number.

Answer: (a)
CBSE Marking & Justification: - Step 1: For $x > 0$, $|x| = x \implies \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{x}{x} = 1$. [½ Mark] - Step 2: For $x < 0$, $|x| = -x \implies \lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{-x}{x} = -1$. [½ Mark] - Since $\lim_{x \to 0^+} f(x) \ne \lim_{x \to 0^-} f(x)$, the limit does not exist. Reason (R) is the precise theorem that explains Assertion (A).

Item 2:

  • Assertion (A): If $f(x) = x \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ for $x \ne 0$ and $f(0) = 0$, then $f'(0)$ does not exist.
  • Reason (R): The function $\sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ oscillates infinitely between $-1$ and $1$ as $x \to 0$.

Answer: (a)
CBSE Marking & Justification: - Step 1: By definition of derivative at origin:

$$ f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(0+h) - f(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h \sin(1/h) - 0}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \sin\left(\frac{1}{h}\right) $$

[½ Mark] - Step 2: As $h \to 0$, $\frac{1}{h} \to \pm\infty$. The term $\sin(1/h)$ oscillates endlessly within $[-1, 1]$ and does not approach any single unique real value. Hence, the limit does not exist, proving $f'(0)$ does not exist. Reason (R) correctly explains this non-existence. [½ Mark]

Item 3:

  • Assertion (A): The derivative of $\tan x$ with respect to $x$ is $\sec^2 x$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
  • Reason (R): By the quotient rule of differentiation, $\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{u}{v}\right) = \frac{v \frac{du}{dx} - u \frac{dv}{dx}}{v^2}$ provided $v \ne 0$.

Answer: (d)
CBSE Marking & Justification: - Step 1: The function $\tan x$ is defined only on $\mathbb{R} \setminus \left\{(2k+1)\frac{\pi}{2} : k \in \mathbb{Z}\right\}$. Thus, its derivative is $\sec^2 x$ only on this restricted domain, NOT for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Therefore, Assertion (A) is false. [½ Mark] - Step 2: The quotient rule statement in Reason (R) is standard and mathematically correct on valid domains where $v \ne 0$. Hence, Reason (R) is true. [½ Mark]

Item 4:

  • Assertion (A): $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos 4x}{x^2} = 8$.
  • Reason (R): $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$, where $\theta$ is measured in radians.

Answer: (a)
CBSE Marking & Justification: - Step 1: Use identity $1 - \cos 4x = 2\sin^2(2x)$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{2\sin^2(2x)}{x^2} = 2 \lim_{x \to 0} \left(\frac{\sin 2x}{2x}\right)^2 \cdot 4 = 2 \cdot (1)^2 \cdot 4 = 8 $$

Thus Assertion (A) is true. [½ Mark] - Step 2: The calculation relies directly on $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin \theta}{\theta} = 1$ with $\theta = 2x \to 0$. Hence Reason (R) is true and is the correct explanation of (A). [½ Mark]

Question A.4: HOTS / Multi-Concept Analytical Problem

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE HOTS Multi-Concept, 4 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem Statement:

Determine the real constants $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{a \cos x + b x \sin x - 5}{x^4} $$

exists as a finite non-zero real number. Hence, find the value of the limit.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Analyzing the Denominator & Indeterminacy Constraints
    As $x \to 0$, the denominator $x^4 \to 0$.
    For the limit to exist finitely, the numerator must also approach $0$ as $x \to 0$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} (a \cos x + b x \sin x - 5) = a(1) + b(0)(0) - 5 = a - 5 $$

For finite existence:

$$ a - 5 = 0 \implies a = 5 $$

(Marking: Identifying $\frac{0}{0}$ requirement and finding $a = 5$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 2: Taylor Series Expansion of Numerator Functions
    Substitute $a = 5$ into the expression:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{5 \cos x + b x \sin x - 5}{x^4} $$

Recall standard expansions about $x = 0$:

$$ \cos x = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - \mathcal{O}(x^6) = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^4}{24} - \dots $$

$$ \sin x = x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \mathcal{O}(x^5) = x - \frac{x^3}{6} + \dots $$

(Marking: Writing Taylor series expansions up to $x^4$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 3: Substituting and Collecting Powers of $x$

$$ 5 \cos x = 5 - \frac{5x^2}{2} + \frac{5x^4}{24} - \dots $$

$$ b x \sin x = b x \left(x - \frac{x^3}{6} + \dots\right) = b x^2 - \frac{b x^4}{6} + \dots $$

Numerator:

$$ \mathcal{N}(x) = \left(5 - \frac{5x^2}{2} + \frac{5x^4}{24}\right) + \left(bx^2 - \frac{bx^4}{6}\right) - 5 + \mathcal{O}(x^6) $$

$$ \mathcal{N}(x) = \left(b - \frac{5}{2}\right)x^2 + \left(\frac{5}{24} - \frac{b}{6}\right)x^4 + \mathcal{O}(x^6) $$

(Marking: Regrouping coefficients of $x^2$ and $x^4$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 4: Eliminating the $x^2$ Term and Finding the Limit
    The denominator contains $x^4$. If the coefficient of $x^2$ is non-zero, then:

$$ \frac{\left(b - \frac{5}{2}\right)x^2}{x^4} = \frac{b - \frac{5}{2}}{x^2} \to \pm\infty $$

which would make the limit diverge. Thus, the coefficient of $x^2$ must vanish:

$$ b - \frac{5}{2} = 0 \implies b = \frac{5}{2} $$

With $b = \frac{5}{2}$, the expression simplifies to:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\left(\frac{5}{24} - \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{5}{2}\right)x^4 + \mathcal{O}(x^6)}{x^4} $$

$$ L = \frac{5}{24} - \frac{5}{12} = \frac{5 - 10}{24} = -\frac{5}{24} $$

Final Result: $a = 5$, $b = \frac{5}{2}$, and the value of the limit is $-\frac{5}{24}$.
(Marking: Finding $b = \frac{5}{2}$ and correctly evaluating $L = -\frac{5}{24}$: [1 Mark])

Question A.5: HOTS / Algebraic Limit Problem

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE HOTS, 3 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Problem Statement:

Evaluate the limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x + x^2 + x^3 + \dots + x^n - n}{x - 1}, \quad n \in \mathbb{N} $$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme:

  • Method 1: Algebraic Splitting of the Constant $n$
  • Note that $n = \underbrace{1 + 1 + 1 + \dots + 1}_{n \text{ times}}$.
  • Rewrite the numerator by grouping each power $x^k$ with one $1$:

$$ \mathcal{N}(x) = (x - 1) + (x^2 - 1) + (x^3 - 1) + \dots + (x^n - 1) $$

*(Marking: Decomposition of $n$: **<span class="step-mark-tag">[1 Mark]</span>**)*
  • Substitute back into the limit:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 1} \left[ \frac{x - 1}{x - 1} + \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} + \frac{x^3 - 1}{x - 1} + \dots + \frac{x^n - 1}{x - 1} \right] $$

  • Use the standard NCERT limit identity $\lim_{x \to a} \frac{x^k - a^k}{x - a} = k a^{k-1}$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^k - 1^k}{x - 1} = k(1)^{k-1} = k $$

*(Marking: Applying identity to each individual term: **<span class="step-mark-tag">[1 Mark]</span>**)*
  • Sum the resulting integers:

$$ L = 1 + 2 + 3 + \dots + n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} $$

*(Marking: Sum formula and final answer $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$: **<span class="step-mark-tag">[1 Mark]</span>**)*
  • Alternative (Derivatives Formulation):
  • Let $f(x) = x + x^2 + \dots + x^n$.
  • Then $f(1) = 1 + 1 + \dots + 1 = n$.
  • The limit is the definition of the derivative $f'(1)$:

$$ L = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{f(x) - f(1)}{x - 1} = f'(1) $$

  • Differentiating: $f'(x) = 1 + 2x + 3x^2 + \dots + nx^{n-1}$.
  • Evaluating at $x = 1$:

$$ f'(1) = 1 + 2(1) + 3(1)^2 + \dots + n(1)^{n-1} = \frac{n(n+1)}{2} $$

Question B.3: First Principle Differentiation of Trigonometric Function

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exercise 13.2 Q11 / Core Standard, 4 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem Statement:

Find the derivative of $f(x) = \cos x$ from the First Principle of Derivatives.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Stating the Definition of First Principle
    By definition, for any differentiable function $f(x)$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} $$

For $f(x) = \cos x$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\cos(x + h) - \cos x}{h} $$

(Marking: Correct mathematical formula for First Principle: [1 Mark])

  • Step 2: Applying the Trigonometric Transformation Formula
    Using the CD transformation identity:

$$ \cos C - \cos D = -2\sin\left(\frac{C + D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C - D}{2}\right) $$

Setting $C = x + h$ and $D = x$:

$$ \frac{C + D}{2} = \frac{2x + h}{2} = x + \frac{h}{2}, \quad \frac{C - D}{2} = \frac{h}{2} $$

$$ \cos(x + h) - \cos x = -2\sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right) $$

(Marking: Correct application of $\cos C - \cos D$ identity: [1 Mark])

  • Step 3: Substituting and Structuring Limits

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-2\sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \left[ -\sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{\sin\left(\frac{h}{2}\right)}{\frac{h}{2}} \right] $$

(Marking: Reorganizing factors to form $\frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2}$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 4: Evaluating the Fundamental Limits
    Using algebra of limits:

$$ f'(x) = -\left(\lim_{h \to 0} \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right)\right) \cdot \left(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin(h/2)}{h/2}\right) $$

Since $\lim_{u \to 0} \frac{\sin u}{u} = 1$ (where $u = \frac{h}{2} \to 0$) and $\lim_{h \to 0} \sin\left(x + \frac{h}{2}\right) = \sin x$:

$$ f'(x) = -(\sin x) \cdot (1) = -\sin x $$

(Marking: Applying $\lim_{u \to 0}\frac{\sin u}{u} = 1$ and final result $-\sin x$: [1 Mark])

Question B.4: First Principle Differentiation of Rational Square-Root Function

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar / Textbook Supplementary, 4 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ [High-Yield]

Problem Statement:

Find the derivative of $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2x + 3}}$ with respect to $x$ from the First Principle, specifying the domain of validity.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Domain Specification & Definition
    For $f(x)$ to be defined in real numbers, $2x + 3 > 0 \implies x \in \left(-\frac{3}{2}, \infty\right)$.
    By the First Principle of Derivatives:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(x+h)+3}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2x+3}}}{h} $$

(Marking: Domain condition and First Principle formula: [1 Mark])

  • Step 2: Common Denominator and Algebraic Simplification

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{2x+3} - \sqrt{2x+2h+3}}{h \cdot \sqrt{2x+2h+3} \cdot \sqrt{2x+3}} $$

(Marking: Algebraic fraction simplification: [1 Mark])

  • Step 3: Rationalization of the Numerator
    Multiply numerator and denominator by $(\sqrt{2x+3} + \sqrt{2x+2h+3})$:

$$ \mathcal{N} = (\sqrt{2x+3} - \sqrt{2x+2h+3})(\sqrt{2x+3} + \sqrt{2x+2h+3}) $$

$$ = (2x + 3) - (2x + 2h + 3) = -2h $$

Substitute back:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-2h}{h \cdot \sqrt{2x+2h+3} \cdot \sqrt{2x+3} \cdot (\sqrt{2x+3} + \sqrt{2x+2h+3})} $$

Cancel $h \ne 0$:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-2}{\sqrt{2x+2h+3} \cdot \sqrt{2x+3} \cdot (\sqrt{2x+3} + \sqrt{2x+2h+3})} $$

(Marking: Rationalizing numerator and canceling non-zero factor $h$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 4: Evaluating the Limit
    Taking $h \to 0$:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{-2}{\sqrt{2x+3} \cdot \sqrt{2x+3} \cdot (\sqrt{2x+3} + \sqrt{2x+3})} $$

$$ = \frac{-2}{(2x+3) \cdot (2\sqrt{2x+3})} = \frac{-1}{(2x+3)^{3/2}} = -(2x+3)^{-3/2} $$

(Marking: Correct limit evaluation and final simplified form: [1 Mark])

$$ f(x) = \frac{(\cos x - \sin x)(1 + \sqrt{2}\sin x)}{\cos x \cos 2x} $$

(Marking: Rationalizing denominator and substituting $\cos 2x = 1 - 2\sin^2 x$: [1 Mark])

(Marking: Factoring $\cos 2x$ and canceling $(\cos x - \sin x)$: [1 Mark])

Question C.3: Exemplar Piecewise Differentiability and Limit Problem

Tags: <span class="step-mark-tag">[NCERT Exemplar HOTS, 5 Marks]</span> | Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Problem Statement:

Let the function $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ be defined by:

$$ f(x) = \begin{cases} m x^2 + n, & x < 1 \\ cx + 2, & 1 \le x \le 2 \\ \frac{d x^3 + k}{x}, & x > 2 \end{cases} $$

If $f'(1)$ exists and $f(x)$ is continuous at $x = 2$, find the mathematical relations between the parameters $m, n, c, d, k$. Further, if $c = 4$ and $\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = 10$, find the specific numerical values of $m, n, d$, and $k$.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme:

  • Step 1: Differentiability at $x = 1$ Requires Continuity at $x = 1$
    For $f'(1)$ to exist, $f(x)$ must be continuous at $x = 1$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 1^+} f(x) = f(1) $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 1^-} (mx^2 + n) = m(1)^2 + n = m + n $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 1^+} (cx + 2) = c(1) + 2 = c + 2 = f(1) $$

Thus:

$$ m + n = c + 2 \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)} $$

(Marking: Applying continuity condition at $x = 1$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 2: Differentiability Condition at $x = 1$
    Equating the left-hand derivative and right-hand derivative:

$$ L[f'(1)] = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h} = \left.\frac{d}{dx}(mx^2 + n)\right|_{x=1} = 2m(1) = 2m $$

$$ R[f'(1)] = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(1+h) - f(1)}{h} = \left.\frac{d}{dx}(cx + 2)\right|_{x=1} = c $$

Since $f'(1)$ exists:

$$ 2m = c \implies m = \frac{c}{2} \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)} $$

(Marking: Formulating $L[f'(1)] = R[f'(1)]$ to obtain $2m = c$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 3: Continuity at $x = 2$
    For $f(x)$ to be continuous at $x = 2$:

$$ \lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = f(2) $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 2^-} (cx + 2) = c(2) + 2 = 2c + 2 = f(2) $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 2^+} \frac{dx^3 + k}{x} = \frac{d(2)^3 + k}{2} = \frac{8d + k}{2} $$

Therefore:

$$ \frac{8d + k}{2} = 2c + 2 \implies 8d + k = 4c + 4 \quad \text{--- (Equation 3)} $$

(Marking: Continuity condition at $x = 2$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 4: Evaluating Numerical Values with Given Conditions
    Given $c = 4$ and $\lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = 10$:
  • From Equation 2:

$$ m = \frac{c}{2} = \frac{4}{2} = 2 $$

  1. From Equation 1:

$$ m + n = c + 2 \implies 2 + n = 4 + 2 = 6 \implies n = 4 $$

  1. From the given right-hand limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = \frac{8d + k}{2} = 10 \implies 8d + k = 20 $$

  1. Cross-check with Equation 3:

$$ 4c + 4 = 4(4) + 4 = 20 $$

 This matches, confirming consistency.

(Marking: Calculating $m = 2$ and $n = 4$: [1 Mark])

  • Step 5: Relation between $d$ and $k$
    From $8d + k = 20$, the parameters $d$ and $k$ satisfy:

$$ k = 20 - 8d \quad (d \in \mathbb{R}) $$

(Marking: Final values $m = 2$, $n = 4$, and the relation $k = 20 - 8d$: [1 Mark])

Question C.4: Exemplar Composite Trigonometric-Exponential Limit

Evaluate the limit:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos(1 - \cos x)}{x^4} $$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & CBSE Step-Marking Scheme:

    Let $\theta = 1 - \cos x$. Then:

$$ 1 - \cos(1 - \cos x) = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{1 - \cos x}{2}\right) $$

(Marking: Correct application of $1 - \cos\theta = 2\sin^2(\theta/2)$: [½ Mark])

  • Step 2: Structuring the Expression for Standard Limits
    Let $u = \frac{1 - \cos x}{2}$. As $x \to 0$, $u \to 0$.

Common Examiner Pitfalls & Tips:

  1. Angle Measure Warning: The standard identity $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$ is valid only when $x$ is measured in radians. If a question specifies $x^{\circ}$ (degrees), students must first convert: $x^{\circ} = \frac{\pi x}{180}\text{ radians}$, giving $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x^{\circ}}{x} = \frac{\pi}{180}$.
  2. First Principle Working: When differentiating from First Principles, students must not apply L'Hôpital's rule or standard differentiation formulas directly; full algebraic expansion, trigonometric factor conversion (e.g. CD formulas), and explicit limit evaluation are mandatory for full marks.
  3. One-Sided Limits: For piecewise functions or expressions containing square roots, absolute values ($|x|$), or floor functions, compute both the Left-Hand Limit (LHL) and Right-Hand Limit (RHL) before concluding whether the limit exists.

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

In CBSE Board exams, step-by-step presentation is mandatory for subjective questions; however, you need lightning-fast mental shortcuts and verification hacks to spot answers within seconds and prevent arithmetic drifts.

1.1 Algebraic & Conjugate Tricks

  • Conjugate Difference Trick for Radicals ($\infty - \infty$ form):

$$ \lim_{x \to \infty} \left(\sqrt{ax^2 + bx + c} - \sqrt{ax^2 + dx + e}\right) = \frac{b - d}{2\sqrt{a}} $$

  • Verification Hack: Directly compute $\frac{b-d}{2\sqrt{a}}$ on your rough margin. If $a=1$, it simplifies to $\frac{b-d}{2}$.
  • Binomial Approximation for Higher Roots: For $x \to 0$:

$$ (1 + kx)^n \approx 1 + nkx $$

  • Example: $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sqrt[3]{1+3x} - \sqrt{1+2x}}{x} = \frac{(1 + x) - (1 + x)}{x} \to$ evaluate second-order or expand:

$$ \sqrt[3]{1+3x} \approx 1 + x, \quad \sqrt{1+2x} \approx 1 + x - \frac{x^2}{2} $$

Saves extensive rationalization cycles in multiple-choice questions (MCQs) and quick verification.

1.2 Trigonometric Identity Shortcuts

  • The $1 - \cos(kx)$ Power Rule:

$$ 1 - \cos(kx) = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{kx}{2}\right) \implies \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1 - \cos(kx)}{x^2} = \frac{k^2}{2} $$

  • Difference of Cosines:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\cos(ax) - \cos(bx)}{x^2} = \frac{b^2 - a^2}{2} $$

(Direct derivation from $\left[\frac{1-\cos(ax)}{x^2} - \frac{1-\cos(bx)}{x^2}\right]$). * Tangent–Sine Difference Trap Rule:

$$ \tan(x) - \sin(x) = \tan(x)(1 - \cos x) = \tan(x)\cdot 2\sin^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\tan(x) - \sin(x)}{x^3} = \lim_{x \to 0}\left(\frac{\tan x}{x}\right)\left(\frac{1-\cos x}{x^2}\right) = 1 \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2} $$

1.3 Derivative Quick Methods

  • Logarithmic Differentiation for Products of $3+$ Factors: If $y = f_1(x) f_2(x) f_3(x) \cdots f_n(x)$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = y \left[\frac{f_1'(x)}{f_1(x)} + \frac{f_2'(x)}{f_2(x)} + \cdots + \frac{f_n'(x)}{f_n(x)}\right] $$

Avoids repeated tedious product rule brackets. * Reciprocal and Quotient Fast Derivatives:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{1}{f(x)}\right) = -\frac{f'(x)}{[f(x)]^2} $$

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{ax+b}{cx+d}\right) = \frac{ad - bc}{(cx+d)^2} $$

(Use the determinant value $ad - bc$ directly for rational linear forms to check quotient rule calculations).

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

CBSE marking schemes strictly allocate marks to individual steps. An error in condition statement, notation, or domain drops scores from $4/4$ to $2/4$ or $2.5/4$.

2.1 The "L'Hôpital's Rule" Disqualification

  • The Trap: Using L'Hôpital's rule directly in a subjective Class 11 question.
  • Penalty: $0$ marks awarded for the method step. CBSE Class 11 syllabus does not prescribe L'Hôpital's rule.
  • Examiner Instruction: If you use L'Hôpital's rule to verify the limit in rough work, you must show standard NCERT algebraic expansion, rationalization, or substitution of standard trigonometric limits on the answer script.

2.2 Left-Hand Limit (LHL) & Right-Hand Limit (RHL) Traps

  • Absolute Value Limits ($|x-a|$):

$$ \lim_{x \to a}\frac{|x-a|}{x-a} $$

  • Trap: Cancelling $\frac{|x-a|}{x-a} = 1$ directly.
  • Required Presentation:

$$ \text{LHL} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|(a-h)-a|}{(a-h)-a} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|-h|}{-h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{-h} = -1 $$

$$ \text{RHL} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|(a+h)-a|}{(a+h)-a} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|h|}{h} = 1 $$

$$ \because \text{LHL} \neq \text{RHL}, \quad \text{Limit does not exist (DNE)}. $$

  • Deduction: Skipping explicit LHL/RHL split costs 1 to $1\frac{1}{2}$ marks.

2.3 Trigonometric Limit Angle Units

  • Degree vs. Radian Trap:

$$ \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin(x^\circ)}{x} $$

  • Mistake: Writing $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin(x^\circ)}{x} = 1$.
  • Correct Step: The standard limit $\lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1$ is valid only when $\theta$ is measured in radians.

$$ x^\circ = \frac{\pi x}{180} \text{ radians} $$

$$ \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{180}\right)}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0}\left[\frac{\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{180}\right)}{\frac{\pi x}{180}} \times \frac{\pi}{180}\right] = 1 \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{180} $$

  • Deduction: Direct answer of $1$ receives zero marks.

2.4 First Principle of Derivatives Formatting Pitfalls

When evaluated under "Differentiate $f(x)$ from first principles": 1. Formula Step ($\frac{1}{2}$ mark):

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} $$

Omission of this formula line incurs a direct $\frac{1}{2}$ mark deduction. 2. Persistent Limit Notation ($\frac{1}{2}$ mark): Dropping the symbol "$\lim_{h \to 0}$" before substitution of $h=0$ is an evaluation penalty. You must write $\lim_{h \to 0}$ on every single line until $h$ is evaluated. 3. Trigonometric First Principle Sign Drift: When expanding $\cos(x+h) - \cos x$:

$$ \cos(C) - \cos(D) = -2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{C-D}{2}\right) = 2\sin\left(\frac{C+D}{2}\right)\sin\left(\frac{D-C}{2}\right) $$

Missing the negative sign inside or outside drops the final step mark.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

  1. Existence of a Limit: $\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L$ holds if and only if both one-sided limits exist and are finite and equal:

$$ \lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = L $$

Edge Case: If a function is defined only on one side of an endpoint of its closed domain (e.g., $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ at $x=0$), NCERT defines the limit at an endpoint as the one-sided limit from within the domain. 2. The Sandwich (Squeeze) Theorem: If $g(x) \le f(x) \le h(x)$ for all $x$ in an open interval containing $c$ (except possibly at $c$ itself), and:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} g(x) = \lim_{x \to c} h(x) = L $$

Then:

$$ \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = L $$

(Used in the rigorous NCERT geometric proof that $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$). 3. Fundamental Trigonometric Inequality (Geometric Foundation): For $0 < |x| < \frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$ \cos x < \frac{\sin x}{x} < 1 $$

  1. Standard Polynomial Limit Theorem:

$$ \lim_{x \to a}\frac{x^n - a^n}{x - a} = n a^{n-1} \quad (\forall n \in \mathbb{Q}, \, a > 0) $$

Examiner Check: In step marking, state this standard formula in the right-hand margin: $\left[\because \lim_{x \to a}\frac{x^n - a^n}{x-a} = n a^{n-1}\right]$. 5. Differentiability Implies Continuity (NCERT Core Principle): If a function $f(x)$ is differentiable at a point $x_0$, it is continuous at $x_0$. The Converse is FALSE: $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $x=0$, but not differentiable at $x=0$. 6. Derivative at a Point (Geometric Interpretation):

$$ f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} $$

Represents the slope of the tangent line to the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $(a, f(a))$.

4.3 Rules of Differentiation

  • Sum / Difference Rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[u(x) \pm v(x)\right] = u'(x) \pm v'(x) $$

  • Product Rule (Leibniz Rule):

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[u(x) \cdot v(x)\right] = u'(x)v(x) + u(x)v'(x) $$

Three functions:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[u v w] = u'vw + uv'w + uvw' $$

  • Quotient Rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{u(x)}{v(x)}\right] = \frac{u'(x)v(x) - u(x)v'(x)}{[v(x)]^2}, \quad \text{provided } v(x) \neq 0 $$

Examiner Note: A frequent mistake is swapping terms in the numerator: $\frac{u v' - u' v}{v^2}$. The rule always differentiates the numerator first: $(u'v - uv') / v^2$.
