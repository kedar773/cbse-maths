1. IN-DEPTH PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & MATHEMATICAL INTUITION

1.1 The Invertibility Crisis of Trigonometric Functions

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Priority 5/5: Fundamental conceptual backbone asked in viva, MCQs, and assertion-reasoning)

A function $f: A \to B$ is invertible if and only if it is a bijection (one-one / injective, and onto / surjective).

Trigonometric functions are periodic:

$$ f(x + T) = f(x) \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R} $$

Hence, for any $y$ in their range, there exist infinitely many pre-images:

$$ f(x_1) = f(x_2) = y \quad (x_1 \neq x_2) $$

Trigonometric functions are many-one over their natural domains. Consequently, their natural inverses do not exist.

The Mathematical Resolution: Domain Restriction

To construct an inverse, we restrict the domain of each trigonometric function to an interval where: 1. The function is strictly monotonic (strictly increasing or strictly decreasing), guaranteeing injectivity (one-one). 2. The restricted function maintains its entire range, guaranteeing surjectivity (onto) relative to that range.

When $f$ is restricted to such an interval, the inverse function:

$$ f^{-1}: \text{Range}(f) \to \text{Restricted Domain}(f) $$

is uniquely defined.

The choice of domain restriction is arbitrary; infinitely many such intervals exist. To ensure worldwide uniformity, mathematicians standardized on a single conventional interval for each function, known as the Principal Value Branch (PVB).

1.3 Geometric & Quadrant Partitioning Intuition

⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Priority 4/5: Crucial for sign handling)

All six inverse trigonometric functions fall into two distinct families based on their behavior when the input argument $x$ is negative:

Family I: The Odd-Symmetric Family (Quadrants I and IV)

$$ \sin^{-1} x, \quad \tan^{-1} x, \quad \operatorname{cosec}^{-1} x $$

  • Range spans $\left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$ (or subsets thereof).
  • For $x \ge 0$: Values lie in Quadrant I $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$.
  • For $x < 0$: Values lie in Quadrant IV $\left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, 0\right)$ (measured clockwise as negative angles, never as $\left[\dfrac{3\pi}{2}, 2\pi\right]$).

Family II: The Supplementary Family (Quadrants I and II)

$$ \cos^{-1} x, \quad \cot^{-1} x, \quad \sec^{-1} x $$

  • Range spans $[0, \pi]$ (or subsets thereof).
  • For $x \ge 0$: Values lie in Quadrant I $\left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$.
  • For $x < 0$: Values lie in Quadrant II $\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right]$.
  • These satisfy the supplementary reflection rule: $f^{-1}(-x) = \pi - f^{-1}(x)$.

2. RIGOROUS STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL PROOFS & DERIVATIONS

Theorem 2.1: Negative Argument Theorems (Family I vs Family II)

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Priority 5/5)

Part (a): Proof of $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1}(x)$ for $x \in [-1, 1]$

Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$, which implies $-x \in [-1, 1]$.
To Prove: $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1}(x)$.

Proof: Let:

$$ \theta = \sin^{-1}(-x) $$

By the definition of the principal value branch of inverse sine:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

Taking the sine on both sides:

$$ \sin(\theta) = -x $$

Multiplying both sides by $-1$:

$$ -\sin(\theta) = x $$

Using the odd symmetry of the trigonometric sine function, $-\sin(\theta) = \sin(-\theta)$:

$$ \sin(-\theta) = x $$

Now, analyze the restriction on $-\theta$:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \implies -\frac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2} \implies \frac{\pi}{2} \ge -\theta \ge -\frac{\pi}{2} \implies -\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

Since $-\theta$ lies strictly inside the principal value branch of $\sin^{-1}$, taking $\sin^{-1}$ on both sides is uniquely well-defined:

$$ \sin^{-1}(\sin(-\theta)) = \sin^{-1}(x) $$

$$ -\theta = \sin^{-1}(x) $$

Multiplying by $-1$:

$$ \theta = -\sin^{-1}(x) $$

Substituting back $\theta = \sin^{-1}(-x)$:

$$ \sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Part (b): Proof of $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x)$ for $x \in [-1, 1]$

Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$, which implies $-x \in [-1, 1]$.
To Prove: $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x)$.

Proof: Let:

$$ \theta = \cos^{-1}(-x) $$

By definition of the principal value branch of inverse cosine:

$$ \theta \in [0, \pi] $$

Taking cosine on both sides:

$$ \cos(\theta) = -x $$

Multiplying both sides by $-1$:

$$ -\cos(\theta) = x $$

Using the trigonometric identity $-\cos(\theta) = \cos(\pi - \theta)$:

$$ \cos(\pi - \theta) = x $$

Now verify the range constraint for $(\pi - \theta)$:

$$ 0 \le \theta \le \pi $$

Multiplying by $-1$:

$$ -\pi \le -\theta \le 0 $$

Adding $\pi$ across all parts of the inequality:

$$ \pi - \pi \le \pi - \theta \le \pi + 0 $$

$$ 0 \le \pi - \theta \le \pi \implies (\pi - \theta) \in [0, \pi] $$

Since $(\pi - \theta)$ lies within the principal value branch of $\cos^{-1}$:

$$ \cos^{-1}(\cos(\pi - \theta)) = \cos^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \pi - \theta = \cos^{-1}(x) $$

Rearranging terms:

$$ \theta = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x) $$

Substituting back $\theta = \cos^{-1}(-x)$:

$$ \cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.2: Composition of Function with its Inverse

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Priority 5/5)

Case 1: Outside In: $f(f^{-1}(x)) = x$

Statement: $\sin(\sin^{-1}(x)) = x \quad \forall x \in [-1, 1]$.
Proof:
Let $\theta = \sin^{-1} x$. By definition, this implies $\sin \theta = x$, where $\theta \in \left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$.
Direct substitution of $\theta$:

$$ \sin(\sin^{-1} x) = x $$

This relation holds unconditionally for all $x$ in the domain of the inverse function.

Case 2: Inside Out: $f^{-1}(f(x)) = x$ (The Conditional Identity)

Statement: $\sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x \iff x \in \left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$.
Analytical Derivation for $x \notin \left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$: If $x$ lies outside the principal branch, we must find an angle $\alpha \in \left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$ such that $\sin \alpha = \sin x$.

  • For $x \in \left[\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{3\pi}{2}\right]$:

$$ \sin(\pi - x) = \sin x $$

Checking the bounds:

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} \le x \le \frac{3\pi}{2} \implies -\frac{3\pi}{2} \le -x \le -\frac{\pi}{2} \implies -\frac{\pi}{2} \le \pi - x \le \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Therefore:

$$ \sin^{-1}(\sin x) = \sin^{-1}(\sin(\pi - x)) = \pi - x $$

  • For $x \in \left[\dfrac{3\pi}{2}, \dfrac{5\pi}{2}\right]$:

$$ \sin(x - 2\pi) = \sin x $$

Checking the bounds:

$$ \frac{3\pi}{2} \le x \le \frac{5\pi}{2} \implies -\frac{\pi}{2} \le x - 2\pi \le \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Therefore:

$$ \sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x - 2\pi $$

Self-Inversion and Reflection Identities (sin⁻¹(sin x), sin(sin⁻¹ x), and Negative Arguments)

Subtopic: Self-Inversion & Reflection Identities

Module 1: Pedagogical Foundations & Conceptual Hierarchy

Inverse Trigonometric Functions (ITF) represent the functional inverses of standard trigonometric functions restricted to their Principal Value Branches (PVB) to guarantee bijectivity (one-one and onto).

1. The Fundamental Duality

In standard functional analysis, if $f: A \to B$ is a bijection, then $f^{-1}: B \to A$ exists such that: 1. $f(f^{-1}(x)) = x \quad \forall x \in B$ 2. $f^{-1}(f(x)) = x \quad \forall x \in A$

Because trigonometric functions are periodic, they are many-to-one over their natural domains ($\mathbb{R}$). Hence, inversion is valid only within defined restricted intervals.

Module 2: Rigorous Mathematical Derivations & Proofs

Category A: External Inversion $T(T^{-1}(x)) = x$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem Statement A1: For any $x \in [-1, 1]$, $\sin(\sin^{-1}(x)) = x$.

  • Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$
  • To Prove: $\sin(\sin^{-1}(x)) = x$
  • Proof: Let $\sin^{-1}(x) = \theta$. By definition of the inverse sine function:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \iff \sin(\theta) = x $$

Substituting $\theta = \sin^{-1}(x)$ back into the equation:

$$ \sin(\sin^{-1}(x)) = x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

This identity is algebraically transparent because $x$ is already restricted to $[-1, 1]$. It never generates multiple branches; outside $[-1, 1]$, the expression is simply undefined in $\mathbb{R}$.

Category B: Internal Inversion $T^{-1}(T(x)) = x$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem Statement B1: $\sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x \iff x \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$. If $x \notin \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$, $\sin^{-1}(\sin x) = (-1)^n (x - n\pi)$, where $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that the resulting value falls within $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$.

  • Derivation for arbitrary $x$: Let $y = \sin^{-1}(\sin x)$. By definition of the principal value branch of $\sin^{-1}$:

$$ y \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

Applying the sine function on both sides:

$$ \sin y = \sin x \implies \sin y - \sin x = 0 $$

Using standard trigonometric factorization:

$$ 2 \sin\left(\frac{y - x}{2}\right) \cos\left(\frac{y + x}{2}\right) = 0 $$

This yields two families of solutions: 1. $\frac{y - x}{2} = k\pi \implies y = x + 2k\pi$ 2. $\frac{y + x}{2} = k\pi + \frac{\pi}{2} \implies y = (2k + 1)\pi - x = -x + (2k+1)\pi$

Combining both branches into standard compact form:

$$ y = (-1)^n x + n\pi \quad \text{for some } n \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ such that } y \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem Statement B2: $\cos^{-1}(\cos x) = x \iff x \in [0, \pi]$. If $x \notin [0, \pi]$, $\cos^{-1}(\cos x) = \pm x + 2n\pi$, choosing the sign and $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that the outcome lies in $[0, \pi]$.

  • Proof: Let $y = \cos^{-1}(\cos x) \implies y \in [0, \pi]$.

$$ \cos y = \cos x \implies y = 2n\pi \pm x, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z} $$

We select $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ and the sign ($+$ or $-$) such that:

$$ 0 \le 2n\pi \pm x \le \pi $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Category C: Reflection Identities (Negative Arguments) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Inverse trigonometric functions fall into two distinct symmetry families under argument negation: 1. Odd Symmetry Family (Origin Symmetric): $\sin^{-1}, \tan^{-1}, \csc^{-1}$ 2. Supplementary / Affine Reflection Family: $\cos^{-1}, \cot^{-1}, \sec^{-1}$

Proof 1: $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1}(x)$

  • Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$, which implies $-x \in [-1, 1]$.
  • To Prove: $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1}(x)$
  • Proof: Let $\theta = \sin^{-1}(-x)$. By definition of the principal branch:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

Taking the sine of both sides:

$$ \sin(\theta) = -x \iff x = -\sin(\theta) $$

Using the odd property of the sine function ($\sin(-\theta) = -\sin(\theta)$):

$$ x = \sin(-\theta) $$

Since $\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$, multiplying by $-1$ gives:

$$ -\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

Therefore, we can safely apply $\sin^{-1}$ to both sides:

$$ \sin^{-1}(x) = -\theta $$

Substituting $\theta = \sin^{-1}(-x)$:

$$ \sin^{-1}(x) = -\sin^{-1}(-x) \iff \sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2: $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x)$

  • Given: $x \in [-1, 1] \implies -x \in [-1, 1]$.
  • To Prove: $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x)$
  • Proof: Let $\theta = \cos^{-1}(-x)$. By definition:

$$ \theta \in [0, \pi] $$

$$ \cos(\theta) = -x \iff x = -\cos(\theta) $$

Using the second-quadrant trigonometric identity $-\cos(\theta) = \cos(\pi - \theta)$:

$$ x = \cos(\pi - \theta) $$

Now examine the interval of $(\pi - \theta)$:

$$ 0 \le \theta \le \pi \implies -\pi \le -\theta \le 0 \implies 0 \le \pi - \theta \le \pi $$

Since $(\pi - \theta) \in [0, \pi]$, it falls squarely within the principal value branch of $\cos^{-1}$. Applying $\cos^{-1}$ to both sides:

$$ \cos^{-1}(x) = \pi - \theta $$

Substituting $\theta = \cos^{-1}(-x)$:

$$ \cos^{-1}(x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(-x) $$

Rearranging gives:

$$ \cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 3: $\tan^{-1}(-x) = -\tan^{-1}(x)$

  • Given: $x \in \mathbb{R}$
  • To Prove: $\tan^{-1}(-x) = -\tan^{-1}(x)$
  • Proof: Let $\theta = \tan^{-1}(-x)$, where $\theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.

$$ \tan \theta = -x \iff x = -\tan \theta = \tan(-\theta) $$

Since $\theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, it follows that $-\theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$. Taking $\tan^{-1}$ on both sides:

$$ \tan^{-1}(x) = -\theta = -\tan^{-1}(-x) \iff \tan^{-1}(-x) = -\tan^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 4: $\cot^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cot^{-1}(x)$

  • Given: $x \in \mathbb{R}$
  • To Prove: $\cot^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cot^{-1}(x)$
  • Proof: Let $\theta = \cot^{-1}(-x)$, where $\theta \in (0, \pi)$.

$$ \cot \theta = -x \iff x = -\cot \theta = \cot(\pi - \theta) $$

Since $0 < \theta < \pi$, multiplying by $-1$ and adding $\pi$:

$$ 0 < \pi - \theta < \pi $$

Thus $(\pi - \theta)$ lies within the PVB of $\cot^{-1}$.

$$ \cot^{-1}(x) = \pi - \theta \implies \cot^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cot^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Geometric Intuition & Co-Function Complementary Angles

Consider a right-angled triangle $\triangle ABC$ right-angled at $C$ ($\angle C = \frac{\pi}{2}$): Let acute angle $\angle A = \theta \implies \angle B = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta$.

By standard trigonometric ratios:

$$ \sin A = \sin \theta = \frac{BC}{AB} = x $$

$$ \cos B = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \frac{BC}{AB} = x $$

Inverting these relations on the interval $x \in (0, 1)$:

$$ \theta = \sin^{-1} x \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta = \cos^{-1} x $$

Adding the two angles:

$$ \sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \theta + \left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

This elementary right-triangle geometry extends across the complete symmetric domain $x \in [-1, 1]$ using unit-circle quadrant projections.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs

Group I: Co-Function (Complementary Angle) Identities

Theorem 1: $\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}, \quad \forall x \in [-1, 1]$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$
  • To Prove: $\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$

Proof:

Let:

$$ \sin^{-1} x = \theta $$

By definition of the principal value branch:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \quad \text{and} \quad \sin \theta = x $$

Using the fundamental complementary angle identity of trigonometry:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \sin \theta = x $$

We must verify that $\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right)$ lies within the principal value branch of $\cos^{-1}$, namely $[0, \pi]$:

$$ -\frac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Multiplying across by $-1$ (which reverses the inequalities):

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} \ge -\theta \ge -\frac{\pi}{2} \iff -\frac{\pi}{2} \le -\theta \le \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Adding $\frac{\pi}{2}$ throughout:

$$ 0 \le \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta \le \pi $$

Since $\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) \in [0, \pi]$ and $\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = x$, we apply the definition of $\cos^{-1}$:

$$ \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta $$

Substituting back $\theta = \sin^{-1} x$:

$$ \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} - \sin^{-1} x \implies \sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: $\tan^{-1} x + \cot^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}, \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R}$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Given: $x \in \mathbb{R}$
  • To Prove: $\tan^{-1} x + \cot^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$

Proof:

Let:

$$ \tan^{-1} x = \theta \implies \theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \quad \text{and} \quad \tan \theta = x $$

Using the co-function identity:

$$ \cot\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \tan \theta = x $$

Verify the range:

$$ -\frac{\pi}{2} < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2} $$

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} > -\theta > -\frac{\pi}{2} \iff -\frac{\pi}{2} < -\theta < \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Adding $\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$ 0 < \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta < \pi $$

Since $\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) \in (0, \pi)$, which is the principal value branch of $\cot^{-1} x$:

$$ \cot^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta $$

Substituting $\theta = \tan^{-1} x$:

$$ \cot^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} - \tan^{-1} x \implies \tan^{-1} x + \cot^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: $\sec^{-1} x + \csc^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}, \quad \forall x \in (-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty)$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Given: $|x| \ge 1$
  • To Prove: $\sec^{-1} x + \csc^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$

Proof:

Let:

$$ \csc^{-1} x = \theta \implies \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \setminus \{0\} \quad \text{and} \quad \csc \theta = x $$

By complementary trigonometry:

$$ \sec\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = \csc \theta = x $$

Verify range bounds:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \setminus \{0\} \implies -\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \setminus \{0\} $$

Adding $\frac{\pi}{2}$:

$$ \left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) \in [0, \pi] \setminus \left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\right\} $$

This corresponds to the principal value branch of $\sec^{-1} x$. Therefore:

$$ \sec^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta $$

Substituting $\theta = \csc^{-1} x$:

$$ \sec^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} - \csc^{-1} x \implies \sec^{-1} x + \csc^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Group II: Reciprocal Relations

Theorem 4: $\csc^{-1} x = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), \quad \text{for } |x| \ge 1$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Given: $x \in (-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty)$
  • To Prove: $\csc^{-1} x = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$

Proof:

Let:

$$ \csc^{-1} x = \theta $$

Then:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \setminus \{0\} \quad \text{and} \quad \csc \theta = x $$

Taking the reciprocal on both sides:

$$ \frac{1}{\csc \theta} = \frac{1}{x} \implies \sin \theta = \frac{1}{x} $$

Since $|x| \ge 1 \implies 0 < \left|\frac{1}{x}\right| \le 1 \implies \frac{1}{x} \in [-1, 1] \setminus \{0\}$. Furthermore, $\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \setminus \{0\} \subset \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ (the principal value branch of $\sin^{-1}$).

Therefore:

$$ \theta = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) $$

Equating the expressions for $\theta$:

$$ \csc^{-1} x = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

(An identical procedure applies to $\sec^{-1} x = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$ for $|x| \ge 1$.)

Theorem 5: The Cotangent Reciprocal Split Theorem ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$ \cot^{-1} x = \begin{cases} \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), & x > 0 \\ \pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), & x < 0 \end{cases} $$

  • Given: $x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$
  • To Prove: The two branch conditions above.

Case 1: $x > 0$

Let $\cot^{-1} x = \theta$. Because $x > 0$, $\theta$ lies in the first quadrant:

$$ \theta \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \quad \text{and} \quad \cot \theta = x $$

Taking the reciprocal:

$$ \tan \theta = \frac{1}{x} $$

Since $\theta \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, it lies strictly within the principal branch of $\tan^{-1}$, which is $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$:

$$ \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \implies \cot^{-1} x = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) $$

Case 2: $x < 0$

Let $\cot^{-1} x = \theta$. Because $x < 0$, $\theta$ lies in the second quadrant of its principal branch $(0, \pi)$:

$$ \theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right) \quad \text{and} \quad \cot \theta = x $$

Taking the reciprocal:

$$ \tan \theta = \frac{1}{x} $$

Notice that if we directly apply $\tan^{-1}$ to $\tan \theta$, we run into an interval mismatch, because $\theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right)$, whereas the range of $\tan^{-1}$ is $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.

We adjust $\theta$ by subtracting $\pi$:

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} < \theta < \pi \implies \frac{\pi}{2} - \pi < \theta - \pi < \pi - \pi \implies -\frac{\pi}{2} < \theta - \pi < 0 $$

Now, $(\theta - \pi)$ lies inside the principal branch $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$. Using the periodicity of the tangent function:

$$ \tan(\theta - \pi) = -\tan(\pi - \theta) = -(-\tan \theta) = \tan \theta = \frac{1}{x} $$

Applying $\tan^{-1}$ to both sides:

$$ \theta - \pi = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) $$

Adding $\pi$:

$$ \theta = \pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) $$

Substituting back $\theta = \cot^{-1} x$:

$$ \cot^{-1} x = \pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), \quad \text{for } x < 0 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Question: Evaluate the principal value of $\cos^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right) - 2\sin^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$.
[CBSE 2020, 2 Marks]

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Let $\theta_1 = \cos^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)$. Using the identity $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x)$:

$$ \cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = \pi - \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) $$

Since $\cos\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and $\dfrac{\pi}{3} \in [0, \pi]$:

$$ \theta_1 = \pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3} $$

  1. Let $\theta_2 = \sin^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$. Using the identity $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1}(x)$:

$$ \sin^{-1}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = -\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) $$

Since $\sin\left(\dfrac{\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\dfrac{\pi}{3} \in \left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$:

$$ \theta_2 = -\frac{\pi}{3} $$

  1. Substitute $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ into the original expression:

$$ E = \theta_1 - 2\theta_2 = \frac{2\pi}{3} - 2\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{2\pi}{3} + \frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{4\pi}{3} $$

Final Answer: $\dfrac{4\pi}{3}$

[CBSE 2022 All India, 2 Marks]

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Evaluate $T_1 = \tan^{-1}\left(\tan \dfrac{7\pi}{6}\right)$: * PVB of $\tan^{-1} x$ is $\left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$. * $\dfrac{7\pi}{6} = \pi + \dfrac{\pi}{6}$ lies in Quadrant III. * $\tan\left(\pi + \dfrac{\pi}{6}\right) = \tan\left(\dfrac{\pi}{6}\right)$. * Since $\dfrac{\pi}{6} \in \left(-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$:

  2. PVB of $\cos^{-1} x$ is $[0, \pi]$.

💡 Example 5: Non-Identity Composition with Negative Radical Argument

Question: Find the principal value of $\sec^{-1}\left(\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right) + \operatorname{cosec}^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{2}\right) - \cot^{-1}(-\sqrt{3})$.
[CBSE 2023, 3 Marks]

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Term 1: Let $\alpha = \sec^{-1}\left(\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)$.

$$ \sec(\alpha) = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \implies \cos(\alpha) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \implies \alpha = \frac{\pi}{6} \in [0, \pi] \setminus \left\{\frac{\pi}{2}\right\} $$

  1. Term 2: Let $\beta = \operatorname{cosec}^{-1}\left(-\sqrt{2}\right)$. Using Family I odd symmetry:

$$ \operatorname{cosec}^{-1}(-x) = -\operatorname{cosec}^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \beta = -\operatorname{cosec}^{-1}(\sqrt{2}) = -\frac{\pi}{4} \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \setminus \{0\} $$

  1. Term 3: Let $\gamma = \cot^{-1}(-\sqrt{3})$. Using Family II supplementary identity:

$$ \cot^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cot^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \gamma = \pi - \cot^{-1}(\sqrt{3}) = \pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6} \in (0, \pi) $$

  1. Combine terms:

$$ E = \alpha + \beta - \gamma = \frac{\pi}{6} + \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) - \frac{5\pi}{6} = \left(\frac{\pi}{6} - \frac{5\pi}{6}\right) - \frac{\pi}{4} = -\frac{4\pi}{6} - \frac{\pi}{4} = -\frac{2\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{4} = -\frac{11\pi}{12} $$

💡 Example 6: Domain-Restricted Inverse Algebraic Verification
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Question: Evaluate $\cos\left(\sin^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{3}{5}\right) + \cos^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{4}{5}\right)\right)$.
[CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Let $\theta_1 = \sin^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{3}{5}\right)$. * $\theta_1 \in \left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, 0\right)$ (Quadrant IV). * $\sin \theta_1 = -\dfrac{3}{5}$. * Since $\cos \theta > 0$ in Quadrant IV:

$$ \cos \theta_1 = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 \theta_1} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{9}{25}} = \frac{4}{5} $$

  1. Let $\theta_2 = \cos^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{4}{5}\right)$.
  2. $\theta_2 \in \left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right]$ (Quadrant II).
  3. $\cos \theta_2 = -\dfrac{4}{5}$.
  4. Since $\sin \theta > 0$ in Quadrant II:

$$ \sin \theta_2 = \sqrt{1 - \cos^2 \theta_2} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{16}{25}} = \frac{3}{5} $$

  1. Apply the cosine addition formula:

$$ \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = \cos \theta_1 \cos \theta_2 - \sin \theta_1 \sin \theta_2 $$

Substitute the computed values:

$$ \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) = \left(\frac{4}{5}\right)\left(-\frac{4}{5}\right) - \left(-\frac{3}{5}\right)\left(\frac{3}{5}\right) = -\frac{16}{25} - \left(-\frac{9}{25}\right) = -\frac{16}{25} + \frac{9}{25} = -\frac{7}{25} $$

Final Answer: $-\dfrac{7}{25}$

4. EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

Trap 1: Blind Cancellation of $f^{-1}(f(x))$

  • Error: Writing $\cos^{-1}\left(\cos \dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \dfrac{4\pi}{3}$.
  • Why it loses marks: The candidate fails to check whether $\dfrac{4\pi}{3} \in [0, \pi]$. It does not, violating the range of the principal value branch.
  • Correction Protocol: Transform using periodicity and reflection:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\left(2\pi - \frac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) $$

Since $\dfrac{2\pi}{3} \in [0, \pi]$, the correct value is $\dfrac{2\pi}{3}$.

Trap 2: Incorrect Range for Quadrant IV in Family I

  • Error: Stating that $\sin^{-1}\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right) = \dfrac{11\pi}{6}$.
  • Why it loses marks: While $\sin\left(\dfrac{11\pi}{6}\right) = -\dfrac{1}{2}$, the angle $\dfrac{11\pi}{6} \approx 5.76$ lies outside the interval $\left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$.
  • Correction Protocol: Family I angles for negative inputs must always be reported as negative acute angles:

$$ \sin^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\frac{\pi}{6} $$

Trap 3: Confusing $\cot^{-1}(-x)$ with Odd Symmetry

  • Error: Writing $\cot^{-1}(-x) = -\cot^{-1}(x)$.
  • Why it loses marks: The range of $\cot^{-1}(x)$ is $(0, \pi)$. It has no negative values whatsoever. Thus, $-\cot^{-1}(x)$ would produce a negative output, falling outside the range.
  • Correction Protocol: Use the supplementary relationship for Family II:

$$ \cot^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cot^{-1}(x) $$

Trap 4: Open vs. Closed Intervals at Branch Boundaries

  • Error: Specifying the range of $\tan^{-1} x$ as $\left[-\dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$ or $\sec^{-1} x$ without removing $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$.
  • Why it loses marks: $\tan\left(\pm\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$ and $\sec\left(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$ are undefined. Including these points invalidates the bijection.

5. SPEED HACKS & GOLDEN TEACHING POINTS

Hack 1: The "Two-Team" Quadrant Rule

Remember the six inverse trigonometric functions by dividing them into two non-overlapping teams:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{\bf Team I (Origin-Symmetric):} \quad & \sin^{-1}, \; \tan^{-1}, \; \operatorname{cosec}^{-1} \implies \text{Outputs live in Quadrants } \mathbf{I \text{ and } IV} \; \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \\ & f^{-1}(-x) = -f^{-1}(x) \\ \text{\bf Team II (Supplementary):} \quad & \cos^{-1}, \; \cot^{-1}, \; \sec^{-1} \implies \text{Outputs live in Quadrants } \mathbf{I \text{ and } II} \; [0, \pi] \\ & f^{-1}(-x) = \pi - f^{-1}(x) \end{aligned} $$

Hack 2: Nearest Multiple of $\pi$ Method for Radians

To evaluate $f^{-1}(f(\theta))$ where $\theta$ is large: 1. Divide $\theta$ by $\pi$ to write it as $k\pi \pm \phi$, where $\phi \in \left[0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right]$. 2. For $\sin^{-1}(\sin \theta)$: * If $k$ is even: $\sin(k\pi + \phi) = \sin \phi \implies \text{Answer} = \phi$. * If $k$ is odd: $\sin(k\pi - \phi) = \sin \phi \implies \text{Answer} = -\phi$ (or write as $\pi - \text{angle}$). * General rule: $\sin^{-1}(\sin \theta) = (-1)^k (\theta - k\pi)$, where $k$ is the integer nearest to $\dfrac{\theta}{\pi}$. 3. For $\cos^{-1}(\cos \theta)$: * General rule: $\cos^{-1}(\cos \theta) = |\theta - 2k\pi|$, where $2k\pi$ is the nearest even multiple of $\pi$.

Hack 3: Instant Complementary Pair Identifiers

Whenever evaluating sums of inverse functions at identical inputs, look for complementary pairs:

$$ \sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \forall x \in [-1, 1] $$

$$ \tan^{-1} x + \cot^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R} $$

$$ \sec^{-1} x + \operatorname{cosec}^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \forall x \in (-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty) $$

Classroom Demo:

$$ \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) + \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \text{(Instant, no calculation needed)} $$

Module 5: Examiner Traps & Common Student Errors

Trap 1: The "Blind Cancellation" Fallacy

  • The Error: Writing $\cos^{-1}\left(\cos \frac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{4\pi}{3}$.
  • Why Examiners Target This: Students often cancel inverse operations automatically without validating the output range.
  • The Correction: The range of $\cos^{-1}$ is $[0, \pi]$. Because $\frac{4\pi}{3} > \pi$, it cannot be the answer.

$$ \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\left(2\pi - \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) \implies \cos^{-1}\left(\cos \frac{4\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{2\pi}{3} $$

Trap 2: Uniform Negation Error in Reflections

  • The Error: Assuming all inverse functions pull out a simple negative sign: $\cos^{-1}(-x) = -\cos^{-1}(x)$.
  • Why Examiners Target This: Students conflate the odd function property of $\sin^{-1}$ and $\tan^{-1}$ with that of $\cos^{-1}, \cot^{-1}$, and $\sec^{-1}$.
  • The Correction: The range of $\cos^{-1}(x)$ is $[0, \pi]$. An inverse cosine output can never be negative. Thus:

$$ \cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x) $$

Trap 3: Open vs. Closed Boundary Disregard

  • The Error: Stating the range of $\tan^{-1}(x)$ is $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ or $\cot^{-1}(x)$ is $[0, \pi]$.
  • Consequence: Automatic deduction of $0.5$ to $1$ mark in board marking schemes.
  • The Correction: $\tan\left(\pm\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ and $\cot(0)$ or $\cot(\pi)$ are undefined. The ranges must be strictly open intervals:

$$ \text{Range of } \tan^{-1}(x) = \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right), \quad \text{Range of } \cot^{-1}(x) = (0, \pi) $$

Module 6: Speed Hacks & Golden Rules

1. The "Distance from Multiples of $\pi$" Rule for $\sin^{-1}(\sin x)$

For any large angle $\theta$: 1. Find the integer multiple of $\pi$ closest to $\theta$ (let it be $k\pi$). 2. The magnitude of the answer is always $|\theta - k\pi|$. 3. Determine the sign ($+$ or $-$) using the ASTC rule for the quadrant of the original angle $\theta$.

Example: $\sin^{-1}(\sin 10)$ * Closest multiple of $\pi$: $3\pi \approx 9.42$. * Deviation: $10 - 3\pi \approx +0.58$ radians (falls in Quadrant III, where sine is negative). * Hence, the result must be negative: $-(10 - 3\pi) = 3\pi - 10$.

2. The "Even Multiple of $\pi$" Shortcut for $\cos^{-1}(\cos x)$

Because the graph of $\cos^{-1}(\cos x)$ has period $2\pi$ and turns at every multiple of $\pi$: * For any angle $x$, locate the closest even multiple of $\pi$, say $2k\pi$. * The answer is simply:

$$ |x - 2k\pi| $$

Example: $\cos^{-1}(\cos 10)$ * Closest even multiple: $4\pi \approx 12.57$. * Result: $|10 - 4\pi| = 4\pi - 10$.

3. The Co-function Complementary Sum Identities

Keep these handy as quick verification checkpoints:

$$ \sin^{-1}(x) + \cos^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \forall x \in [-1, 1] $$

$$ \tan^{-1}(x) + \cot^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \forall x \in \mathbb{R} $$

$$ \sec^{-1}(x) + \csc^{-1}(x) = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad \forall x \in (-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty) $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Mistakes

Trap 1: The "Blind" Reciprocal of Cotangent

  • The Error: Students write $\cot^{-1}(-1) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{-1}\right) = \tan^{-1}(-1) = -\frac{\pi}{4}$.
  • Why it loses marks: The range of $\cot^{-1}$ is $(0, \pi)$. A negative output like $-\frac{\pi}{4}$ lies entirely outside the range and is automatically awarded $0$ marks.
  • The Correction:

$$ \cot^{-1}(-1) = \pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{-1}\right) = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4} \in (0, \pi) $$

Trap 2: Omitting Domain Constraints in Co-function Sums

  • The Error: Claiming $\sin^{-1}(2) + \cos^{-1}(2) = \frac{\pi}{2}$.
  • Why it loses marks: Neither $\sin^{-1}(2)$ nor $\cos^{-1}(2)$ exists in real numbers because the domain of both functions is $[-1, 1]$.
  • Rule: Always state and verify that $|x| \le 1$ before applying $\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

Trap 3: Sign Errors in Negative Arguments for Co-functions

Students frequently confuse:

$$ \cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1} x \quad \text{(Quadrant II)} $$

with:

$$ \sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1} x \quad \text{(Quadrant IV)} $$

If an equation involves $\sin^{-1}(-x) + \cos^{-1}(-x)$, students sometimes compute:

$$ -\sin^{-1} x - \cos^{-1} x = -\frac{\pi}{2} \quad \text{(INCORRECT!)} $$

Correct Evaluation:

$$ \sin^{-1}(-x) + \cos^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1} x + \left(\pi - \cos^{-1} x\right) = \pi - \left(\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x\right) = \pi - \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

(Notice that the identity $\sin^{-1} u + \cos^{-1} u = \frac{\pi}{2}$ holds for all $u \in [-1, 1]$, including $u = -x$.)

1. The Right-Triangle Complement Rule

Whenever an equation has the form:

$$ \sin^{-1}(\alpha) + \cos^{-1}(\beta) = \frac{\pi}{2} \implies \alpha = \beta \quad (\text{provided } \alpha, \beta \in [-1, 1]) $$

$$ \tan^{-1}(\alpha) + \cot^{-1}(\beta) = \frac{\pi}{2} \implies \alpha = \beta \quad (\forall \alpha, \beta \in \mathbb{R}) $$

$$ \sec^{-1}(\alpha) + \csc^{-1}(\beta) = \frac{\pi}{2} \implies \alpha = \beta \quad (\text{provided } |\alpha| \ge 1, |\beta| \ge 1) $$

Exam Tip: Use this symmetry shortcut to equate variables directly in 1-mark objective questions without lengthy conversions.

2. Calculus Bridge Shortcut

When evaluating derivatives or integrals involving co-function pairs:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left(\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x\right) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0 $$

$$ \implies \frac{d}{dx}(\cos^{-1} x) = -\frac{d}{dx}(\sin^{-1} x) = -\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} $$

Similarly, for definite integrals:

$$ \int_{0}^{1} \left(\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x\right) dx = \int_{0}^{1} \frac{\pi}{2} \, dx = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

This eliminates the need to integrate by parts individually.

This guarantees the answer remains inside $(0, \pi)$ every time.

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Invertibility of Trigonometric Functions ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

A function $f: X \to Y$ is invertible if and only if it is a bijective function (both one-one / injective and onto / surjective).

Standard trigonometric functions are periodic across their natural domains:

$$ f(x + T) = f(x) $$

Because of periodicity, trigonometric functions are many-one over their entire domains $\mathbb{R}$. For instance, $\sin(0) = \sin(\pi) = 0$. Consequently, their inverses cannot exist globally.

To define an inverse: 1. Restricting Domain: We restrict the natural domain to a specific interval where the function is strictly monotonic (strictly increasing or strictly decreasing), making it one-one (injective). 2. Restricting Codomain: We set the codomain equal to the actual range of the trigonometric function over that interval, ensuring the function is onto (surjective).

Under this restricted domain and range, a bijection is established:

$$ f: X \to Y \implies f^{-1}: Y \to X $$

such that:

$$ f^{-1}(y) = x \iff f(x) = y $$

1.3 Geometric Interpretation & Graph Reflection Principle ⭐⭐⭐⭐

For any invertible function $f(x)$, the graph of $y = f^{-1}(x)$ is the geometric reflection of the graph of $y = f(x)$ across the line of symmetry:

$$ y = x $$

  • If $(a, b)$ lies on the curve $y = f(x)$, then $(b, a)$ must lie on the curve $y = f^{-1}(x)$.
  • Horizontal Asymptotes in $f(x)$ become Vertical Asymptotes in $f^{-1}(x)$, and vice versa.

Graphical Profiles of the Six Inverse Functions

  1. $y = \sin^{-1} x$:
  2. Bounded in $[-1, 1] \times [-\pi/2, \pi/2]$.
  3. Passes through $(-1, -\pi/2)$, $(0, 0)$, $(1, \pi/2)$.
  4. Tangent at $(0, 0)$ is $y = x$; vertical tangents occur at $x = \pm 1$.

  5. Possesses odd symmetry (symmetric about the origin): $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1} x$.

  6. $y = \cos^{-1} x$:

  7. Bounded in $[-1, 1] \times [0, \pi]$.
  8. Passes through $(-1, \pi)$, $(0, \pi/2)$, $(1, 0)$.
  9. Monotonically decreases from $\pi$ down to $0$.

  10. Center of inversion / point symmetry at $(0, \pi/2)$.

  11. $y = \tan^{-1} x$:

  12. Entire real axis $\mathbb{R}$ mapped into $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$.
  13. Horizontal asymptotes at $y = \frac{\pi}{2}$ as $x \to \infty$ and $y = -\frac{\pi}{2}$ as $x \to -\infty$.

  14. Strictly increasing, inflection point at the origin $(0, 0)$.

  15. $y = \cot^{-1} x$:

  16. Entire real axis $\mathbb{R}$ mapped into $(0, \pi)$.
  17. Horizontal asymptotes at $y = \pi$ as $x \to -\infty$ and $y = 0$ as $x \to \infty$.

  18. Strictly decreasing, passes through $(0, \pi/2)$.

  19. $y = \sec^{-1} x$:

  20. Domain is $(-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty)$; range is $[0, \pi] \setminus \{\pi/2\}$.
  21. Discontinuous branch break between $x \in (-1, 1)$.

  22. Horizontal asymptote at $y = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

  23. $y = \csc^{-1} x$:

  24. Domain is $(-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty)$; range is $[-\pi/2, \pi/2] \setminus \{0\}$.
  25. Horizontal asymptote at $y = 0$.

2. Rigorous Step-by-Step Derivations & Analytical Proofs

Theorem 1: Self-Composition Identity (The Identity Map) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$ \text{For } x \in [-1, 1], \quad \sin(\sin^{-1} x) = x $$

$$ \text{For } \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right], \quad \sin^{-1}(\sin \theta) = \theta $$

Proof:

Case 1: Let $\theta = \sin^{-1} x$ where $x \in [-1, 1]$. By the definition of the inverse function:

$$ \sin \theta = x $$

Substitute $\theta = \sin^{-1} x$ into this equation:

$$ \sin(\sin^{-1} x) = x \quad \forall \, x \in [-1, 1] $$

Case 2: Let $x = \sin \theta$ where $\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$. Since $\theta$ lies strictly inside the principal value branch of $\sin^{-1}$:

$$ \sin^{-1}(x) = \theta $$

Substitute $x = \sin \theta$:

$$ \sin^{-1}(\sin \theta) = \theta \quad \forall \, \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Negative Argument Identities (Parity & Quadrant Shift) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

  1. $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1} x, \quad x \in [-1, 1]$
  2. $\tan^{-1}(-x) = -\tan^{-1} x, \quad x \in \mathbb{R}$
  3. $\csc^{-1}(-x) = -\csc^{-1} x, \quad |x| \ge 1$
  4. $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1} x, \quad x \in [-1, 1]$
  5. $\cot^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cot^{-1} x, \quad x \in \mathbb{R}$
  6. $\sec^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \sec^{-1} x, \quad |x| \ge 1$

Proof of $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1} x$:

  • Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$, which implies $-x \in [-1, 1]$.
  • To Prove: $\sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1} x$.

Step 1: Let $\theta = \sin^{-1}(-x)$. By definition of the principal branch:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \quad \text{and} \quad \sin \theta = -x $$

Step 2: Multiply both sides by $-1$:

$$ -\sin \theta = x $$

Step 3: Using the odd identity of sine, $-\sin \theta = \sin(-\theta)$:

$$ \sin(-\theta) = x $$

Step 4: Since $\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$, multiplying by $-1$ gives:

$$ -\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

Since $-\theta$ falls within the principal branch:

$$ -\theta = \sin^{-1}(x) \implies \theta = -\sin^{-1}(x) $$

Step 5: Equating expressions for $\theta$:

$$ \sin^{-1}(-x) = -\sin^{-1} x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof of $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1} x$:

  • Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$, which implies $-x \in [-1, 1]$.
  • To Prove: $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1} x$.

Step 1: Let $\theta = \cos^{-1}(-x)$. By definition of the principal branch of $\cos^{-1}$:

$$ \theta \in [0, \pi] \quad \text{and} \quad \cos \theta = -x $$

Step 2: Isolate $x$:

$$ x = -\cos \theta $$

Step 3: Use the trigonometric identity $-\cos \theta = \cos(\pi - \theta)$:

$$ \cos(\pi - \theta) = x $$

Step 4: Determine the interval of $(\pi - \theta)$:

$$ 0 \le \theta \le \pi \implies -\pi \le -\theta \le 0 \implies 0 \le \pi - \theta \le \pi $$

Thus, $(\pi - \theta)$ lies within the principal value branch $[0, \pi]$ of $\cos^{-1}$.

Step 5: Apply $\cos^{-1}$ to both sides:

$$ \pi - \theta = \cos^{-1} x \implies \theta = \pi - \cos^{-1} x $$

Step 6: Substitute back $\theta = \cos^{-1}(-x)$:

$$ \cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1} x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: Complementary Angle Addition Theorem ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

$$ \sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}, \quad \forall \, x \in [-1, 1] $$

Proof:

  • Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$.
  • To Prove: $\sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$.

Step 1: Let $\theta = \sin^{-1} x$. By definition:

$$ \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \quad \text{and} \quad \sin \theta = x $$

Step 2: Use the co-function identity $\sin \theta = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right)$:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right) = x $$

Step 3: Check the bounds of $\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right)$:

$$ -\frac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2} \implies -\frac{\pi}{2} \le -\theta \le \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Add $\frac{\pi}{2}$ across all parts:

$$ 0 \le \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta \le \pi $$

The angle $\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta\right)$ lies within $[0, \pi]$, which is the principal branch of $\cos^{-1}$.

Step 4: Taking the inverse cosine:

$$ \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta $$

Step 5: Substitute $\theta = \sin^{-1} x$:

$$ \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} - \sin^{-1} x \implies \sin^{-1} x + \cos^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 4: Reciprocal Argument Theorem ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

$$ \csc^{-1} x = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), \quad \text{for } |x| \ge 1 $$

$$ \sec^{-1} x = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), \quad \text{for } |x| \ge 1 $$

$$ \cot^{-1} x = \begin{cases} \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), & x > 0 \\ \pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right), & x < 0 \end{cases} $$

Proof of the $\cot^{-1} x$ identity for $x < 0$:

  • Given: $x < 0$.
  • To Prove: $\cot^{-1} x = \pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)$.

Step 1: Let $\theta = \cot^{-1} x$. For $x < 0$, by definition of the principal branch of cotangent inverse:

$$ \theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right) \quad \text{and} \quad \cot \theta = x $$

Step 2: Take the reciprocal:

$$ \tan \theta = \frac{1}{\cot \theta} = \frac{1}{x} $$

Step 3: Since $\theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right)$, the angle $\theta$ is in Quadrant II. The principal value branch of $\tan^{-1}$ is $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, so we cannot write $\theta = \tan^{-1}(1/x)$ directly. Shift the angle into the range of $\tan^{-1}$:

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} < \theta < \pi \implies -\frac{\pi}{2} < \theta - \pi < 0 $$

Thus, $(\theta - \pi) \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, 0\right) \subset \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.

Step 4: Evaluate $\tan(\theta - \pi)$:

$$ \tan(\theta - \pi) = -\tan(\pi - \theta) = -(-\tan \theta) = \tan \theta = \frac{1}{x} $$

Step 5: Since $(\theta - \pi)$ lies inside the PVB of arctangent:

$$ \theta - \pi = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \implies \theta = \pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

3. High-Yield Examples & 5-Year Board PYQs (2020–2025)

Example 1: Boundary Value Evaluation [CBSE 2020, 1M]

Problem: Find the principal value of $\cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$.

Solution: 1. Let $y = \cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)$. 2. The principal value branch of $\cos^{-1}$ is $[0, \pi]$. 3. Using the identity $\cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x)$:

$$ \cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \pi - \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) $$

  1. Since $\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\frac{\pi}{4} \in [0, \pi]$:

$$ \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right) = \frac{\pi}{4} $$

  1. Therefore:

$$ y = \pi - \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{3\pi}{4} $$

Since $\frac{3\pi}{4} \in [0, \pi]$, the final answer is $\frac{3\pi}{4}$.

Example 2: Out-of-Branch Sine Evaluation [CBSE 2021 Term-1, 1M]

Problem: Find the value of $\sin^{-1}\left(\sin \frac{3\pi}{5}\right)$.

Solution: 1. The expression $\sin^{-1}(\sin \theta) = \theta$ holds only if $\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$. 2. Here, $\theta = \frac{3\pi}{5} = 108^\circ \notin \left[-90^\circ, 90^\circ\right]$. 3. Rewrite the argument using the supplementary identity $\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x$:

$$ \sin\left(\frac{3\pi}{5}\right) = \sin\left(\pi - \frac{2\pi}{5}\right) = \sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right) $$

  1. Now check the angle:

$$ \frac{2\pi}{5} = 72^\circ \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

  1. Hence:

$$ \sin^{-1}\left(\sin \frac{3\pi}{5}\right) = \sin^{-1}\left(\sin \frac{2\pi}{5}\right) = \frac{2\pi}{5} $$

Example 3: Multi-Term Linear Combination [CBSE 2023, 2M]

Problem: Evaluate the exact value of:

$$ \tan^{-1}(1) + \cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) + \sin^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) $$

Solution:

Method A (Direct Value Calculation): 1. $\tan^{-1}(1)$: Since $1 = \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)$ and $\frac{\pi}{4} \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$,

$$ \tan^{-1}(1) = \frac{\pi}{4} $$

  1. $\cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = \pi - \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \pi - \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{2\pi}{3}$.
  2. $\sin^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = -\frac{\pi}{6}$.
  3. Summing all three terms:

$$ S = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{2\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{3\pi + 8\pi - 2\pi}{12} = \frac{9\pi}{12} = \frac{3\pi}{4} $$

Method B (Applying Identity Shortcut): Using $\cos^{-1} x + \sin^{-1} x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ for any $x \in [-1, 1]$:

$$ \cos^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) + \sin^{-1}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Thus:

$$ S = \tan^{-1}(1) + \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{3\pi}{4} $$

Example 4: Out-of-Branch Cosine Reduction [CBSE 2024, 2M]

Problem: Find the value of $\cos^{-1}\left(\cos \frac{13\pi}{6}\right)$.

Solution: 1. The domain for $\cos^{-1}(\cos \theta) = \theta$ is $\theta \in [0, \pi]$. 2. $\frac{13\pi}{6} = 2\pi + \frac{\pi}{6} \approx 390^\circ > \pi$. 3. Using the periodicity of cosine, $\cos(2\pi + \alpha) = \cos \alpha$:

$$ \cos\left(\frac{13\pi}{6}\right) = \cos\left(2\pi + \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) $$

  1. Since $\frac{\pi}{6} \in [0, \pi]$:

$$ \cos^{-1}\left(\cos \frac{13\pi}{6}\right) = \cos^{-1}\left(\cos \frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{\pi}{6} $$

Example 5: Non-Standard Composite Evaluation [CBSE 2025 Sample Question Paper, 3M]

Problem: Find the exact value of $\tan\left(2\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{5}\right) - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$.

Solution: 1. Let $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{5}\right) \implies \tan \theta = \frac{1}{5}$. 2. Compute $\tan(2\theta)$ using the double-angle identity:

$$ \tan(2\theta) = \frac{2\tan \theta}{1 - \tan^2 \theta} = \frac{2\left(\frac{1}{5}\right)}{1 - \left(\frac{1}{5}\right)^2} = \frac{\frac{2}{5}}{1 - \frac{1}{25}} = \frac{\frac{2}{5}}{\frac{24}{25}} = \frac{2}{5} \times \frac{25}{24} = \frac{5}{12} $$

  1. We need to evaluate $\tan\left(2\theta - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)$. Use the angle difference identity:

$$ \tan(A - B) = \frac{\tan A - \tan B}{1 + \tan A \tan B} $$

where $A = 2\theta$ and $B = \frac{\pi}{4}$. 4. Substitute $\tan(2\theta) = \frac{5}{12}$ and $\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = 1$:

$$ \tan\left(2\theta - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\frac{5}{12} - 1}{1 + \left(\frac{5}{12}\right)(1)} = \frac{-\frac{7}{12}}{\frac{17}{12}} = -\frac{7}{17} $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap 1: Blithe Cancellation of Functions

$$ \sin^{-1}(\sin x) \neq x \quad \text{when } x \notin \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] $$

  • Wrong: $\sin^{-1}\left(\sin \frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{2\pi}{3}$
  • Why it loses marks: $\frac{2\pi}{3} = 120^\circ$ is outside the range $\left[-90^\circ, 90^\circ\right]$. $\sin^{-1}$ cannot output values outside its principal branch.
  • Right: $\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) = \sin\left(\pi - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) \implies \sin^{-1}\left(\sin \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{\pi}{3}$.

Trap 2: Negative Arguments in Cosine Family ($\cos^{-1}$, $\cot^{-1}$, $\sec^{-1}$)

  • Common Student Blunder: Writing $\cos^{-1}(-x) = -\cos^{-1}(x)$.
  • Correction: The range of $\cos^{-1}$ is $[0, \pi]$. It cannot produce negative angles.

$$ \cos^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cos^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \sec^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \sec^{-1}(x) $$

$$ \cot^{-1}(-x) = \pi - \cot^{-1}(x) $$

Trap 3: Confusion between $(\sin x)^{-1}$ and $\sin^{-1} x$

  • Algebraic Distinction:

$$ (\sin x)^{-1} = \frac{1}{\sin x} = \csc x $$

$$ \sin^{-1} x \neq \frac{1}{\sin x} $$

$\sin^{-1} x$ represents the inverse mapping, not a multiplicative power.

Trap 4: Open vs. Closed Interval Boundaries

Examiners check interval notation on step marks: * $\text{Range of } \tan^{-1} x \text{ is } \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$ (Open brackets: $\tan(\pm \pi/2)$ is undefined). * $\text{Range of } \sin^{-1} x \text{ is } \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$ (Closed brackets: $\sin(\pm \pi/2) = \pm 1$). * Writing $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ for $\tan^{-1} x$ results in an immediate deduction.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

5.1 The Two-Family Classification Rule

Group all six inverse trigonometric functions into two families based on their principal range:

Family I (Centered at 0):
Range: Subsets of [-pi/2, pi/2]
Members: sin⁻¹, tan⁻¹, csc⁻¹
Rule: f⁻¹(-x) = -f⁻¹(x)  (Odd Symmetry)

Family II (Centered at pi/2):
Range: Subsets of [0, pi]
Members: cos⁻¹, cot⁻¹, sec⁻¹
Rule: f⁻¹(-x) = pi - f⁻¹(x)  (Supplementary Symmetry)

5.2 Real-Number Argument Reduction Shortcut

When evaluating $f^{-1}(f(x))$ where $x$ is a pure real number (e.g., $\sin^{-1}(\sin 5)$ or $\cos^{-1}(\cos 10)$): * Express the input in terms of $\pi \approx 3.14159$. * For $x = 5$:

$$ 5 \text{ radians} \approx 5 - 2\pi \approx 5 - 6.28 = -1.28 \text{ rad} $$

Since $-1.28 \in [-\pi/2, \pi/2] \approx [-1.57, 1.57]$:

$$ \sin(5) = \sin(5 - 2\pi) \implies \sin^{-1}(\sin 5) = 5 - 2\pi $$

5.3 Piecewise Linear Representation of $y = \sin^{-1}(\sin x)$

For graphing and advanced evaluations, $y = \sin^{-1}(\sin x)$ forms a continuous triangular wave:

$$ y = \begin{cases} \dots \\ -\pi - x, & -\frac{3\pi}{2} \le x \le -\frac{\pi}{2} \\ x, & -\frac{\pi}{2} \le x \le \frac{\pi}{2} \\ \pi - x, & \frac{\pi}{2} \le x \le \frac{3\pi}{2} \\ x - 2\pi, & \frac{3\pi}{2} \le x \le \frac{5\pi}{2} \\ \dots \end{cases} $$

5.4 Piecewise Linear Representation of $y = \cos^{-1}(\cos x)$

Forms a continuous saw-tooth wave:

$$ y = \begin{cases} \dots \\ -x, & -\pi \le x \le 0 \\ x, & 0 \le x \le \pi \\ 2\pi - x, & \pi \le x \le 2\pi \\ x - 2\pi, & 2\pi \le x \le 3\pi \\ \dots \end{cases} $$

5.5 Quick Reference Checklist

  1. Is the argument of $f^{-1}(x)$ inside the valid domain? (If not, the expression is undefined).
  2. Is the final angle inside the designated Principal Value Branch?
  3. Did you apply $\pi - \theta$ for negative arguments in $\cos^{-1}, \sec^{-1}, \cot^{-1}$?
  4. Are you using parentheses vs square brackets accurately?

1. The Core Philosophy of Simplification in ITF

Simplifying an inverse trigonometric expression means transforming an algebraic or composite transcendental expression into an elementary form $k \cdot f^{-1}(x)$ or a simple linear/rational combination thereof.

The central mechanism relies on the Identity/Cancellation Principles:

$$ f^{-1}(f(\theta)) = \theta \quad \text{if and only if} \quad \theta \in \text{Principal Value Branch (PVB)} $$

$$ f(f^{-1}(x)) = x \quad \text{for all } x \in \text{Domain of } f^{-1} $$

When an expression contains algebraic structures like $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, $\frac{a \cos x - b \sin x}{b \cos x + a \sin x}$, or $\frac{3x - x^3}{1 - 3x^2}$, algebraic manipulation alone is tedious. By selecting a substitution $x = g(\theta)$, we map the inner algebraic structure into a known trigonometric multiple-angle or compound-angle identity, collapsing the argument into a single trigonometric ratio.

⭐⭐⭐ $4x^3 - 3x$ $x = \cos \theta$ $\cos 3\theta = 4\cos^3 \theta - 3\cos \theta$ $\cos 3\theta$

Section 2: Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: Simplification and Form Conversion of Multiple-Angle Inverses (CBSE Core Focus)

$$ \text{Prove that: } 2\tan^{-1}x = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1 + x^2}\right) = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1 - x^2}{1 + x^2}\right) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1 - x^2}\right) $$

(Restricted to their principal branch domains: $x \in [-1, 1]$ for $\sin^{-1}$, $x \ge 0$ for $\cos^{-1}$, and $x \in (-1, 1)$ for $\tan^{-1}$.)

Part A: Derivation of $2\tan^{-1}x = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1 + x^2}\right)$ for $|x| \le 1$

Given: $x \in [-1, 1]$.
To Prove: $\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1 + x^2}\right) = 2\tan^{-1}x$.

Proof: Let:

$$ \tan^{-1} x = \theta \implies x = \tan \theta $$

Since $x \in [-1, 1]$, we have:

$$ -1 \le \tan \theta \le 1 \implies -\frac{\pi}{4} \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{4} $$

Multiplying across by $2$:

$$ -\frac{\pi}{2} \le 2\theta \le \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Substitute $x = \tan \theta$ into the RHS argument:

$$ \text{RHS} = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1 + x^2}\right) = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2\tan \theta}{1 + \tan^2 \theta}\right) $$

By the double-angle identity $\sin 2\theta = \frac{2\tan \theta}{1 + \tan^2 \theta}$:

$$ \text{RHS} = \sin^{-1}(\sin 2\theta) $$

Since $2\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$, which is the exact Principal Value Branch of $\sin^{-1}$:

$$ \sin^{-1}(\sin 2\theta) = 2\theta $$

Substituting back $\theta = \tan^{-1}x$:

$$ \text{RHS} = 2\tan^{-1}x = \text{LHS} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Part B: Derivation of $2\tan^{-1}x = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1 - x^2}{1 + x^2}\right)$ for $x \ge 0$

Given: $x \ge 0$.
To Prove: $\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1 - x^2}{1 + x^2}\right) = 2\tan^{-1}x$.

Proof: Let $\tan^{-1} x = \theta \implies x = \tan \theta$.
Since $x \ge 0$, we have $\theta \in \left[0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.
Consequently:

$$ 0 \le 2\theta < \pi $$

Now substitute into the expression:

$$ \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1 - x^2}{1 + x^2}\right) = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1 - \tan^2 \theta}{1 + \tan^2 \theta}\right) $$

Using the identity $\cos 2\theta = \frac{1 - \tan^2 \theta}{1 + \tan^2 \theta}$:

$$ = \cos^{-1}(\cos 2\theta) $$

Because $2\theta \in [0, \pi)$, which lies inside the Principal Value Branch of $\cos^{-1}$:

$$ \cos^{-1}(\cos 2\theta) = 2\theta = 2\tan^{-1}x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: The Compound Quotient Form Conversion

$$ \text{Prove that: } \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a \cos x - b \sin x}{b \cos x + a \sin x}\right) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) - x, \quad \text{where } \frac{a}{b}\tan x > -1 $$

Given: The expression $E = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a \cos x - b \sin x}{b \cos x + a \sin x}\right)$.
To Prove: $E = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) - x$.

Proof: Divide the numerator and denominator inside the argument by $b \cos x$ (assuming $b \cos x \ne 0$):

$$ \frac{a \cos x - b \sin x}{b \cos x + a \sin x} = \frac{\frac{a \cos x}{b \cos x} - \frac{b \sin x}{b \cos x}}{\frac{b \cos x}{b \cos x} + \frac{a \sin x}{b \cos x}} = \frac{\frac{a}{b} - \tan x}{1 + \frac{a}{b} \tan x} $$

Thus, the expression becomes:

$$ E = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\frac{a}{b} - \tan x}{1 + \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)(\tan x)}\right) $$

Recall the subtraction theorem: $\tan^{-1} u - \tan^{-1} v = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{u - v}{1 + uv}\right)$, valid for $uv > -1$.
Setting $u = \frac{a}{b}$ and $v = \tan x$:

$$ E = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) - \tan^{-1}(\tan x) $$

Assuming $x$ lies in the principal branch $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$:

$$ \tan^{-1}(\tan x) = x $$

Therefore:

$$ E = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right) - x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Section 3: High-Yield Solved Examples & 5-Year PYQs (2020–2025)

Example 1: Standard Algebraic Radical Simplification

[CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
Write the following function in the simplest form:

$$ f(x) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}}\right), \quad -a < x < a $$

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Identify the Radical Form:
The denominator contains $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$. By standard substitution rules, substitute:

$$ x = a \sin \theta \implies \sin \theta = \frac{x}{a} \implies \theta = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) $$

  1. Domain Analysis:
    Given $-a < x < a \implies -1 < \frac{x}{a} < 1$.
    Therefore:

$$ \theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) $$

  1. Algebraic Reduction:

$$ \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} = \sqrt{a^2 - a^2 \sin^2 \theta} = \sqrt{a^2(1 - \sin^2 \theta)} = \sqrt{a^2 \cos^2 \theta} = a |\cos \theta| $$

Since $\theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, $\cos \theta > 0$, so $|\cos \theta| = \cos \theta$.
Thus, $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} = a \cos \theta$. 4. Evaluate the Inner Ratio:

$$ \frac{x}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} = \frac{a \sin \theta}{a \cos \theta} = \tan \theta $$

  1. Inverse Cancellation:

$$ f(x) = \tan^{-1}(\tan \theta) $$

Since $\theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, which is the principal interval of $\tan^{-1}$:

$$ f(x) = \theta = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) $$

Final Answer: $\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)$

Example 2: Conjugate Radical Simplification

[CBSE 2023, 4 Marks]
Simplify:

$$ \tan^{-1}\left[\frac{\sqrt{1 + x^2} - 1}{x}\right], \quad x \ne 0 $$

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Choose Substitution:
The expression involves $1 + x^2$. Substitute:

$$ x = \tan \theta \implies \theta = \tan^{-1} x, \quad \theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \setminus \{0\} $$

  1. Transform Inner Algebraic Terms:

$$ \sqrt{1 + x^2} - 1 = \sqrt{1 + \tan^2 \theta} - 1 = \sqrt{\sec^2 \theta} - 1 = |\sec \theta| - 1 $$

Since $\theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, $\sec \theta > 0$, so $|\sec \theta| = \sec \theta$. 3. Simplify the Fraction:

$$ \frac{\sec \theta - 1}{\tan \theta} = \frac{\frac{1}{\cos \theta} - 1}{\frac{\sin \theta}{\cos \theta}} = \frac{1 - \cos \theta}{\sin \theta} $$

  1. Apply Half-Angle Formulas:

$$ 1 - \cos \theta = 2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right), \quad \sin \theta = 2\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) $$

$$ \implies \frac{1 - \cos \theta}{\sin \theta} = \frac{2\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}{2\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)} = \frac{\sin\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)} = \tan\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) $$

  1. Apply Cancellation:
    The expression becomes $\tan^{-1}\left(\tan \frac{\theta}{2}\right)$.
    Check range of $\frac{\theta}{2}$:

$$ \theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \implies \frac{\theta}{2} \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \subset \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) $$

Therefore:

$$ \tan^{-1}\left(\tan \frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{\theta}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}x $$

Final Answer: $\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}x$

Example 3: Symmetrical Four-Root Radical Form

[CBSE 2022 Term-2 / Sample Paper, 3 Marks]
Express in simplest form:

$$ f(x) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1 + x} - \sqrt{1 - x}}{\sqrt{1 + x} + \sqrt{1 - x}}\right), \quad -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \le x \le 1 $$

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Select Optimal Substitution:
Both $(1 + x)$ and $(1 - x)$ appear inside square roots. Let:

$$ x = \cos 2\theta \implies 2\theta = \cos^{-1} x \implies \theta = \frac{1}{2}\cos^{-1} x $$

  1. Domain Conversion:

$$ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \le x \le 1 \implies 0 \le \cos^{-1} x \le \frac{3\pi}{4} $$

$$ \implies 0 \le 2\theta \le \frac{3\pi}{4} \implies 0 \le \theta \le \frac{3\pi}{8} $$

  1. Simplify the Radicals:

$$ \sqrt{1 + x} = \sqrt{1 + \cos 2\theta} = \sqrt{2\cos^2 \theta} = \sqrt{2}\cos \theta \quad \left(\text{as } 0 \le \theta \le \frac{3\pi}{8} < \frac{\pi}{2} \implies \cos \theta > 0\right) $$

$$ \sqrt{1 - x} = \sqrt{1 - \cos 2\theta} = \sqrt{2\sin^2 \theta} = \sqrt{2}\sin \theta \quad \left(\text{as } \sin \theta \ge 0\right) $$

  1. Substitute into the Fraction:

$$ \frac{\sqrt{1 + x} - \sqrt{1 - x}}{\sqrt{1 + x} + \sqrt{1 - x}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}\cos \theta - \sqrt{2}\sin \theta}{\sqrt{2}\cos \theta + \sqrt{2}\sin \theta} = \frac{\cos \theta - \sin \theta}{\cos \theta + \sin \theta} $$

  1. Divide by $\cos \theta$:

$$ \frac{1 - \tan \theta}{1 + \tan \theta} = \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4} - \theta\right) $$

  1. Cancellation Verification:

$$ f(x) = \tan^{-1}\left(\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4} - \theta\right)\right) $$

Check if $\left(\frac{\pi}{4} - \theta\right) \in \text{PVB}$:
Since $0 \le \theta \le \frac{3\pi}{8}$:

$$ -\frac{3\pi}{8} \le -\theta \le 0 \implies \frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{3\pi}{8} \le \frac{\pi}{4} - \theta \le \frac{\pi}{4} + 0 $$

$$ \implies -\frac{\pi}{8} \le \frac{\pi}{4} - \theta \le \frac{\pi}{4} $$

The interval $\left[-\frac{\pi}{8}, \frac{\pi}{4}\right]$ lies entirely within $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$. Hence:

$$ f(x) = \frac{\pi}{4} - \theta = \frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{1}{2}\cos^{-1} x $$

Final Answer: $\frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{1}{2}\cos^{-1} x$

Example 4: Cubic Tangent Expansion

[CBSE 2020, 3 Marks]
Simplify the function:

$$ f(x) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3a^2x - x^3}{a^3 - 3ax^2}\right), \quad a > 0, \; -\frac{a}{\sqrt{3}} < x < \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}} $$

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Divide Inner Terms by $a^3$:

$$ f(x) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) - \left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^3}{1 - 3\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^2}\right) $$

  1. Substitute:
    Let $\frac{x}{a} = \tan \theta \implies \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)$.
  2. Check Boundary Values:
    Given $-\frac{a}{\sqrt{3}} < x < \frac{a}{\sqrt{3}} \implies -\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} < \frac{x}{a} < \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$.

$$ \implies -\frac{\pi}{6} < \theta < \frac{\pi}{6} \implies -\frac{\pi}{2} < 3\theta < \frac{\pi}{2} $$

  1. Collapse via Triple-Angle Formula:

$$ \frac{3\tan \theta - \tan^3 \theta}{1 - 3\tan^2 \theta} = \tan 3\theta $$

$$ f(x) = \tan^{-1}(\tan 3\theta) $$

  1. Evaluate:
    Since $3\theta \in \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$:

$$ f(x) = 3\theta = 3\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) $$

Final Answer: $3\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)$

Example 5: Non-Trivial Branch Mapping

[CBSE Competency-Focused / Exemplar Advanced, 3 Marks]
Evaluate $g(x) = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1 + x^2}\right)$ when $x > 1$.

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Substitution:
Let $x = \tan \theta$. Since $x > 1$, we have:

$$ \theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{4}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \implies 2\theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi\right) $$

  1. PVB Violation Check:
    $2\theta$ is outside the principal range of $\sin^{-1}$, which is $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$.
    Writing $\sin^{-1}(\sin 2\theta) = 2\theta$ is an invalid branch mapping error.
  2. Angle Reduction:
    By the identity $\sin(\pi - \phi) = \sin \phi$:

$$ \sin 2\theta = \sin(\pi - 2\theta) $$

Check the range of $(\pi - 2\theta)$:

$$ \frac{\pi}{2} < 2\theta < \pi \implies -\pi < -2\theta < -\frac{\pi}{2} \implies 0 < \pi - 2\theta < \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Now $(\pi - 2\theta) \in \left(0, \frac{\pi}{2}\right) \subset \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$. 4. Evaluate:

$$ \sin^{-1}(\sin 2\theta) = \sin^{-1}(\sin(\pi - 2\theta)) = \pi - 2\theta = \pi - 2\tan^{-1}x $$

Final Answer: $\pi - 2\tan^{-1}x$

Section 4: Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap 1: The Absolute Value Blindness in Radicals

  • The Error: Writing $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2} = x^2 - a^2$ or $\sqrt{\cos^2 \theta} = \cos \theta$ without checking the sign.
  • Mathematical Reality:

$$ \sqrt{u^2} = |u| $$

If $\theta \in \left(\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}\right)$, $\cos \theta < 0$, which means $\sqrt{\cos^2 \theta} = -\cos \theta$. In CBSE questions, check the given interval of $x$ to verify that the trigonometric ratio remains positive.

Trap 2: Blind Cancellation Without PVB Domain Checks

  • The Error: Assuming $\cos^{-1}(\cos \alpha) = \alpha$ universally.
  • Example: In $\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+x} - \sqrt{1-x}}{\sqrt{1+x} + \sqrt{1-x}}\right)$, students often skip checking whether $\left(\frac{\pi}{4} - \theta\right)$ falls inside $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$.
  • Board Marking Consequence: Up to 1 mark is deducted on CBSE marking schemes if domain verification steps are omitted.

Trap 3: Sign Errors in Inverse Tangent Difference Formulas

  • The Error: Misapplying:

$$ \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x - y}{1 + xy}\right) = \tan^{-1} x - \tan^{-1} y $$

without verifying $xy > -1$. If $xy < -1$ and $x > 0, y < 0$, the identity requires an extra term: $\pi + \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x - y}{1 + xy}\right)$.

Trap 4: Overlooking the Constant Dimension Factor $a$

  • The Error: Substituting $x = \sin \theta$ into expressions containing $a^2 - x^2$ instead of $x = a \sin \theta$.
  • Consequence: Dimensions do not match, leaving unresolved terms like $\sqrt{a^2 - \sin^2 \theta}$ that cannot be simplified with $1 - \sin^2 \theta = \cos^2 \theta$.

Section 5: Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Class 12

1. The "Derivative Preview" Verification Hack

To verify a simplification in an exam: * Rule: If $y = f(x)$ simplifies to $y = g(x)$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ must match $\frac{d}{dx}[g(x)]$. * Practical Check: For $f(x) = \tan^{-1}\left[\frac{\sqrt{1+x^2}-1}{x}\right]$, you found $\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}x$.
Differentiating $\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}x$ gives $\frac{1}{2(1+x^2)}$.
Testing a simple value like $x = 1$:

$$ f(1) = \tan^{-1}(\sqrt{2}-1) = \frac{\pi}{8}, \quad \frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}(1) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{8} \quad \checkmark $$

2. The Single Point Substitution Hack (MCQ Elimination)

When a question asks for the simplest form of an algebraic ITF expression with four options: 1. Pick a convenient test value for $x$ (e.g., $x = 0$, $x = 1$, or $x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$). 2. Compute the numerical value of the given expression. 3. Plug the same $x$ into the four options. 4. Eliminate incorrect options.

Example: For $\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{1+x^2}-1}{x}\right)$ at $x = 1$:

$$ \text{Value} = \tan^{-1}(\sqrt{2}-1) = 22.5^\circ = \frac{\pi}{8} $$

  • Option A: $\tan^{-1} x \implies \tan^{-1}(1) = \frac{\pi}{4}$ (Eliminated)
  • Option B: $\frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1} x \implies \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{8}$ (Correct candidate)

3. Structural Recognition Map (Mental Flashcards)

Keep these structural mappings in mind for quick identification:

$$ \frac{2x}{1-x^2} \longleftrightarrow \tan 2\theta \implies \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1-x^2}\right) = 2\tan^{-1}x $$

$$ \frac{2x}{1+x^2} \longleftrightarrow \sin 2\theta \implies \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{2x}{1+x^2}\right) = 2\tan^{-1}x $$

$$ \frac{1-x^2}{1+x^2} \longleftrightarrow \cos 2\theta \implies \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1-x^2}{1+x^2}\right) = 2\tan^{-1}x $$

$$ \frac{3x-x^3}{1-3x^2} \longleftrightarrow \tan 3\theta \implies \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{3x-x^3}{1-3x^2}\right) = 3\tan^{-1}x $$

$$ \sqrt{\frac{1-x}{1+x}} \longleftrightarrow \sqrt{\frac{1-\cos\phi}{1+\cos\phi}} = \tan\frac{\phi}{2} \implies \text{Set } x = \cos\phi $$
