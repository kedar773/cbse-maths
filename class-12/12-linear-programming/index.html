Introduction to Linear Programming: Mathematical Formulation, Objective Function & Constraints

Class 12 Mathematics: Linear Programming

Topic: Introduction to Linear Programming — Mathematical Formulation, Objective Function & Constraints

1. Pedagogical Theory, Core Definitions & Geometric Intuition

1.1 What is an Optimization Problem? ⭐⭐⭐

In industry, economics, agriculture, and defense, decision-makers are repeatedly faced with allocating scarce resources (such as capital, raw materials, labor, and machine-hours) to achieve a defined goal: * Maximizing profit, production output, or operational efficiency, or * Minimizing manufacturing cost, travel time, wastage, or risk.

A problem that seeks to maximize or minimize a given mathematical function subject to a set of limitations or restrictions is termed an Optimization Problem.

When both the function to be optimized and all the limiting conditions are strictly linear functions of the decision variables, it is termed a Linear Programming Problem (LPP).

1.2 Core Terminology & NCERT Definitions

1. Decision Variables ($x, y$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The unknown independent physical quantities whose values are to be decided by the problem solver to achieve the optimal objective. * Example: Number of units of Product $A$ manufactured per day ($x$) and Product $B$ ($y$). * Domain Restriction: In practical settings, these quantities can never be negative:

$$ x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0 $$

2. Objective Function ($Z$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

A linear algebraic function of the decision variables that must be maximized or minimized. * Canonical representation in two variables:

$$ Z = ax + by $$

where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ are predetermined constants (e.g., unit profit, unit cost), and $a$ and $b$ are not both zero. * $Z$ is strictly a scalar value representing total gain or loss.

3. Constraints & System of Linear Inequalities ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The physical or operational restrictions imposed on the decision variables due to resource scarcity, statutory rules, or market demand. * Expressed mathematically as a system of linear inequalities or equations:

$$ a_i x + b_i y \le c_i \quad \text{or} \quad a_i x + b_i y \ge c_i \quad (i = 1, 2, \dots, m) $$

4. Non-Negative Restrictions (Non-Negativity Constraints) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The conditions $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$. * Geometric Significance: Confines the entire solution space strictly to the First Quadrant of the Cartesian coordinate system.

5. Feasible Region & Infeasible Region ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Feasible Region: The common intersection region determined by all the constraints simultaneously, including non-negativity conditions $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$. Every point $(x, y)$ inside or on the boundary of this region satisfies all constraints.
  • Infeasible Region: Any region outside this common intersection. Any point in this region violates at least one constraint.

6. Feasible Solution vs. Optimal Feasible Solution ⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Feasible Solution: Any point $(x_0, y_0)$ belonging to the feasible region.
  • Optimal Feasible Solution: A feasible solution $(x^*, y^*)$ that attains the maximum (or minimum) value of the objective function $Z$:

$$ Z(x^*, y^*) \ge Z(x, y) \quad \forall (x, y) \in \text{Feasible Region (for Maximization)} $$

$$ Z(x^*, y^*) \le Z(x, y) \quad \forall (x, y) \in \text{Feasible Region (for Minimization)} $$

1.3 Geometric Intuition: The Level Curves ($Z$-Lines)

Consider the linear objective function $Z = ax + by$. For any fixed value of $Z = k$, the equation:

$$ ax + by = k \implies y = -\frac{a}{b}x + \frac{k}{b} $$

represents a family of parallel straight lines, each having slope $m = -\frac{a}{b}$.

                 y ^
                   |      / Z = k3 (Maximized)
                   |     /  / Z = k2
                   |    /  /  / Z = k1
                   |   /  /  /
                   |  +--+  /

                   |  +--+/

As the scalar $k$ increases, the line translates parallel to itself across the coordinate plane. * To maximize $Z$, push the line in the direction of increasing $k$ until it is on the verge of leaving the feasible region. * The very last point (or edge) of the feasible region touched by this translating line is the optimal point. * Because the boundary of the feasible region consists of straight line segments (polygonal boundary), the last point of contact will inevitably occur at an extreme point—a vertex (corner point) of the region.

2. Fundamental Theorems & Complete Mathematical Proofs

Linear Programming relies on two foundational theorems of convex geometry and functional analysis.

2.1 Convex Sets & Extreme Points

  • A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ is called convex if, for every pair of points $P_1, P_2 \in S$, the entire line segment joining $P_1$ and $P_2$ lies completely within $S$:

$$ \lambda P_1 + (1 - \lambda)P_2 \in S \quad \forall \lambda \in [0, 1] $$

  • The feasible region of an LPP is an intersection of a finite number of closed half-planes:

$$ H_i = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : a_i x + b_i y \le c_i\} $$

  • Since every closed half-plane is a convex set, and the intersection of any collection of convex sets is always convex, the feasible region of an LPP is always a convex polygonal region.

2.2 Theorem 1 (Fundamental Extreme Point Theorem) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Let $R$ be the feasible region (convex polygon) for a linear programming problem, and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. When $R$ is bounded, the objective function $Z$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum value on $R$, and each of these extreme values occurs at at least one corner point (vertex) of $R$.

Complete Step-by-Step Proof

Given: 1. A bounded, closed convex polygonal feasible region $R$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$ with a finite set of vertices:

$$ V = \{P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n\} $$

where each $P_i = (x_i, y_i)$ for $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$. 2. A linear objective function:

$$ Z(P) = Z(x, y) = ax + by $$

To Prove: There exist vertices $P_{\min}, P_{\max} \in V$ such that for all $P(x, y) \in R$:

$$ Z(P_{\min}) \le Z(P) \le Z(P_{\max}) $$

Construction / Convex Combination Property: By the Krein-Milman Theorem (or the Representation Theorem for Bounded Polyhedra in $\mathbb{R}^2$), any point $P(x, y)$ in a bounded convex polygon $R$ can be uniquely expressed as a convex combination of its vertices $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_n$. That is, there exist scalars $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \dots, \lambda_n$ such that:

$$ P = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i P_i $$

subject to:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_i \ge 0 \quad (\forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}) $$

Algebraic Derivation:

Let the objective function evaluated at each vertex $P_i(x_i, y_i)$ be denoted by $Z_i$:

$$ Z_i = Z(P_i) = a x_i + b y_i \quad (i = 1, 2, \dots, n) $$

Since the set of vertices is finite, the set of real numbers $\{Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_n\}$ has a finite minimum and a finite maximum:

$$ Z_{\min} = \min_{1 \le i \le n} \{Z_i\} = Z(P_{\min}) $$

$$ Z_{\max} = \max_{1 \le i \le n} \{Z_i\} = Z(P_{\max}) $$

Now, evaluate $Z$ at an arbitrary point $P(x, y) \in R$:

$$ Z(P) = Z\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i P_i\right) = a\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i x_i\right) + b\left(\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i y_i\right) $$

Rearranging the finite sums:

$$ Z(P) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i (a x_i + b y_i) = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i Z_i $$

Step 1: Establishing the Upper Bound Since $Z_i \le Z_{\max}$ for all $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$, and each $\lambda_i \ge 0$:

$$ \lambda_i Z_i \le \lambda_i Z_{\max} $$

Summing over all $i$:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i Z_i \le \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i Z_{\max} $$

$$ Z(P) \le Z_{\max} \left(\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\right) $$

Since $\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i = 1$:

$$ Z(P) \le Z_{\max} \cdot 1 = Z(P_{\max}) $$

Step 2: Establishing the Lower Bound Similarly, since $Z_i \ge Z_{\min}$ for all $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, n\}$:

$$ \lambda_i Z_i \ge \lambda_i Z_{\min} $$

Summing over all $i$:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i Z_i \ge \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i Z_{\min} $$

$$ Z(P) \ge Z_{\min} \left(\sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i\right) = Z(P_{\min}) \cdot 1 = Z(P_{\min}) $$

Combining both inequalities:

$$ Z(P_{\min}) \le Z(P) \le Z(P_{\max}) \quad \forall P \in R $$

Since $P_{\min}, P_{\max} \in V$, the optimal values (both maximum and minimum) occur at the corner points (vertices) of the feasible region.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.3 Theorem 2 (Unbounded Region & Existence Condition) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Let $R$ be the feasible region for an LPP and $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. If $R$ is unbounded, then a maximum or minimum value of $Z$ may or may not exist. However, if it exists, it must occur at a corner point of $R$.

Rigorous Analytical Condition for Unbounded Regions:

  1. Let $M$ be the maximum value obtained among all the corner points of the unbounded feasible region $R$.
  2. $M$ is the absolute maximum of $Z$ over $R$ if and only if the open half-plane:

$$ ax + by > M $$

 has **no point in common** with the feasible region $R$.

  • If the open half-plane $ax + by > M$ shares even a single point with $R$, then $Z$ has no maximum value (it grows without bound).

  • Let $m$ be the minimum value obtained among all the corner points of $R$.

  • $m$ is the absolute minimum of $Z$ over $R$ if and only if the open half-plane:

$$ ax + by < m $$

 has **no point in common** with the feasible region $R$.
  • If the open half-plane $ax + by < m$ shares any point with $R$, $Z$ has no minimum value.

2.4 Multiple Optimal Solutions (Alternative Optima) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

If the objective function $Z = ax + by$ attains the same optimal value (say $M$) at two distinct corner points $A(x_1, y_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2)$, then:

$$ Z(A) = ax_1 + by_1 = M \quad \text{and} \quad Z(B) = ax_2 + by_2 = M $$

For any point $Q$ lying on the line segment connecting $A$ and $B$, $Q$ can be expressed parametrically as:

$$ Q = \lambda A + (1 - \lambda) B, \quad \lambda \in [0, 1] $$

Evaluating $Z$ at $Q$:

$$ Z(Q) = Z(\lambda A + (1 - \lambda) B) = \lambda Z(A) + (1 - \lambda) Z(B) = \lambda M + (1 - \lambda) M = M $$

Result: Every point on the line segment joining $A$ and $B$ is also an optimal solution. Therefore, the problem possesses an infinite number of optimal solutions.

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Fundamental Definitions (Strict NCERT Terminology)

Linear Objective Function ($Z$) [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

A linear function of the form:

$$ Z = ax + by $$

where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ are fixed real constants (not both zero), and $x, y$ are decision variables. The goal is to either maximize or minimize $Z$ subject to a set of specified linear constraints.

Decision Variables ($x, y$) [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐]

The non-negative variables whose optimal numerical values are to be determined. In standard two-variable Class 12 problems, these denote quantities of items, production units, or resource allocations.

Constraints & Non-Negative Restrictions [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

  • Linear Constraints: Linear inequalities or equations representing limitations on available resources (e.g., labour hours, storage capacity, capital). Standard form:

$$ a_i x + b_i y \le c_i \quad \text{or} \quad a_i x + b_i y \ge c_i \quad (i = 1, 2, \dots, m) $$

  • Non-Negative Restrictions (Trivial Constraints):

$$ x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0 $$

Geometrically, these restrict the entire analysis strictly to the First Quadrant (including the coordinate axes).

Feasible Region ($\mathcal{R}$) & Infeasible Region [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

  • Feasible Region ($\mathcal{R}$): The common region determined by the simultaneous intersection of all given constraints, including the non-negative restrictions $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$. Every point $(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$ is called a feasible solution.
  • Infeasible Region: The set of all points in the Cartesian plane lying outside the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$. Any point in this region violates at least one constraint and constitutes an infeasible solution.

Bounded vs. Unbounded Feasible Region [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

  • Bounded Region: A feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ is said to be bounded if it can be enclosed completely within a circle or disc of sufficiently large, finite radius $r$:

$$ \exists \, r > 0 \quad \text{such that} \quad \mathcal{R} \subseteq \left\{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x^2 + y^2 \le r^2\right\} $$

In planar geometry, a bounded feasible region is always a closed, convex polygon having a finite area and a finite set of vertices (corner points). - Unbounded Region: A region that extends infinitely in at least one direction (it cannot be enclosed in any finite disc).

Optimal Solution [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

A point $(x^*, y^*) \in \mathcal{R}$ such that:

$$ Z(x^*, y^*) \ge Z(x, y) \quad \forall \, (x, y) \in \mathcal{R} \quad \text{(for Maximization)} $$

$$ Z(x^*, y^*) \le Z(x, y) \quad \forall \, (x, y) \in \mathcal{R} \quad \text{(for Minimization)} $$

1.2 Fundamental Theorems of Linear Programming

Theorem 1 (Convex Polygon / Extreme Point Theorem) [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Statement: Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the feasible region (convex polygon) for a linear programming problem and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. When $\mathcal{R}$ is bounded, the objective function $Z$ attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum value on $\mathcal{R}$, and each of these extreme values occurs at an extreme point (corner point / vertex) of $\mathcal{R}$.

Theorem 2 (Multiple Optimal Solutions Theorem) [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Statement: Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the feasible region for an LPP with objective function $Z = ax + by$. If the optimal value (maximum or minimum) of $Z$ occurs at two distinct corner points of $\mathcal{R}$, say $P(x_1, y_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2)$, then $Z$ attains the same optimal value at every point lying on the line segment joining $P$ and $Q$.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

2.1 Geometric Proof of the Corner Point Property for a Bounded Region

Given:

  1. Feasible region $\mathcal{R} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is a non-empty, closed, bounded convex polygon with $k$ vertices $V_1, V_2, \dots, V_k$, where $V_i = (x_i, y_i)$.
  2. Objective function $Z(x, y) = ax + by$, where $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ are not both zero.

To Prove:

The maximum value of $Z(x, y)$ on $\mathcal{R}$ occurs at one of the vertices $V_i$ ($i \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}$).

Mathematical Basis:

By the Krein-Milman Theorem (finite-dimensional case) and the definition of a convex polygon, any point $P(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$ can be expressed as a convex combination of its vertices $V_1, V_2, \dots, V_k$:

$$ (x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i (x_i, y_i) $$

subject to:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_i \ge 0 \quad \forall \, i \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\} $$

Step-by-Step Algebraic Manipulation:

Evaluate the objective function at an arbitrary point $P(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$:

$$ Z(x, y) = ax + by = a\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i x_i\right) + b\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i y_i\right) $$

Using the distributive law of real numbers:

$$ Z(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i (ax_i + by_i) $$

Notice that $ax_i + by_i = Z(V_i)$, the value of the objective function evaluated at vertex $V_i$:

$$ Z(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i Z(V_i) $$

Let $M$ be the maximum value among all corner points:

$$ M = \max_{1 \le i \le k} \left\{ Z(V_i) \right\} $$

Because $\lambda_i \ge 0$ for all $i$:

$$ \lambda_i Z(V_i) \le \lambda_i M \quad \forall \, i \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\} $$

Summing over all $i = 1, 2, \dots, k$:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i Z(V_i) \le \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i M $$

Factor out the constant $M$:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i Z(V_i) \le M \left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i\right) $$

Since $\sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i = 1$:

$$ Z(x, y) \le M(1) \implies Z(x, y) \le M $$

Similarly, defining $m = \min_{1 \le i \le k} \{ Z(V_i) \}$:

$$ Z(x, y) \ge m \left(\sum_{i=1}^{k} \lambda_i\right) \implies Z(x, y) \ge m $$

Hence, for every point $(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$:

$$ m \le Z(x, y) \le M $$

Since $m = Z(V_{\text{min}})$ and $M = Z(V_{\text{max}})$ for some vertices $V_{\text{min}}, V_{\text{max}} \in \{V_1, \dots, V_k\}$, the extreme values are attained at the vertices.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.2 Proof of Theorem 2: Infinite / Multiple Optimal Solutions

Given:

Two distinct corner points $P(x_1, y_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2)$ of $\mathcal{R}$ both yield the same optimal maximum value $M$:

$$ Z(P) = ax_1 + by_1 = M $$

$$ Z(Q) = ax_2 + by_2 = M $$

To Prove:

Every point $R$ lying on the line segment $PQ$ yields $Z(R) = M$.

Step-by-Step Proof:

Any point $R(x, y)$ on the line segment $PQ$ can be written parametrically using parameter $\lambda \in [0, 1]$:

$$ x = \lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda) x_2 $$

$$ y = \lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda) y_2 $$

Now evaluate $Z(R)$:

$$ Z(R) = ax + by = a\big[\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda) x_2\big] + b\big[\lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda) y_2\big] $$

Rearranging terms:

$$ Z(R) = \lambda (ax_1 + by_1) + (1 - \lambda)(ax_2 + by_2) $$

Substitute $ax_1 + by_1 = M$ and $ax_2 + by_2 = M$:

$$ Z(R) = \lambda M + (1 - \lambda) M = M(\lambda + 1 - \lambda) = M(1) = M $$

Thus, $Z(R) = M$ for all $\lambda \in [0, 1]$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Fundamental Definitions & NCERT Terminology

Definition 1: Optimization Problem

An optimization problem seeks to maximize or minimize a linear objective function subject to a set of linear constraints (equalities or inequalities).

Definition 2: Objective Function

A linear function of two or more decision variables $x$ and $y$:

$$ Z = ax + by \quad (a, b \in \mathbb{R},\text{ not both zero}) $$

which is to be maximized or minimized subject to constraints.

Definition 3: Constraints

The linear inequalities or equations in decision variables $x$ and $y$ defining the restrictions on the problem:

$$ \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} x_j \le b_i \quad \text{or} \quad \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} x_j \ge b_i $$

Non-negativity restrictions ($x \ge 0, y \ge 0$) ensure real-world validity in physical domains.

Definition 4: Feasible Region ($\mathcal{R}$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The common region determined by all the constraints including non-negative constraints $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ is called the feasible region (or solution region) for the problem. Every point $(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$ is a feasible solution.

Definition 5: Bounded vs. Unbounded Feasible Region ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Bounded Region: A feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ is said to be bounded if it can be enclosed within a circle/disk of finite radius $R > 0$. That is:

$$ \exists R \in \mathbb{R}^+ \quad \text{such that} \quad x^2 + y^2 \le R^2 \quad \forall (x, y) \in \mathcal{R} $$

  • Unbounded Region: A feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ is said to be unbounded if it extends indefinitely in at least one direction. No finite circle can enclose it.

Definition 6: Corner Point (Extreme Point) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

A corner point of a feasible region is a point in the region which is the intersection of two boundary lines.

1.2 Core Theorems: Formal NCERT Statements

Theorem 1 (Fundamental Theorem of Linear Programming) ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the feasible region (convex polygon) for a linear programming problem and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. When $\mathcal{R}$ is bounded, the objective function $Z$ has both a maximum and a minimum value on $\mathcal{R}$ and each of these occurs at a corner point (vertex) of $\mathcal{R}$.

Theorem 2 (Existence Theorem for Unbounded Regions) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the feasible region for a linear programming problem and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. If $\mathcal{R}$ is unbounded, then: 1. An optimum value (maximum or minimum) of $Z$ may or may not exist. 2. However, if it exists, it must occur at a corner point of $\mathcal{R}$.

1.3 The Open Half-Plane Verification Principle ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

When the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ is unbounded, evaluating $Z$ at all corner points yields candidate values:

$$ M = \max \{Z(P_i)\} \quad \text{or} \quad m = \min \{Z(P_i)\} $$

To confirm whether $M$ or $m$ is the true global optimum across the entire unbounded region $\mathcal{R}$, we must establish the existence or non-existence of points in $\mathcal{R}$ that yield strictly superior values:

  1. For Minimization (Candidate minimum $m$):
  2. Construct the strictly lower open half-plane:

$$ H_{<} = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : ax + by < m\} $$

  • Draw the dashed line $ax + by = m$.
  • Condition for Optimality:

$$ \mathcal{R} \cap H_{<} = \emptyset \iff \text{Minimum value of } Z \text{ is } m $$

  • If $\mathcal{R} \cap H_{<} \neq \emptyset$, then $Z$ can attain values strictly less than $m$, meaning no minimum value exists.

  • For Maximization (Candidate maximum $M$):

  • Construct the strictly higher open half-plane:

$$ H_{>} = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : ax + by > M\} $$

  • Draw the dashed line $ax + by = M$.
  • Condition for Optimality:

$$ \mathcal{R} \cap H_{>} = \emptyset \iff \text{Maximum value of } Z \text{ is } M $$

  • If $\mathcal{R} \cap H_{>} \neq \emptyset$, then $Z$ can take arbitrarily large values, meaning no maximum value exists.

2. Complete Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

2.1 Theorem: Convexity of the Feasible Region

$$ \begin{aligned} &Given: \quad \mathcal{R} = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : a_i x + b_i y \le c_i, \; i = 1, 2, \dots, k; \; x \ge 0, y \ge 0\} \\ &To Prove: \quad \mathcal{R} \text{ is a convex set, i.e., } \forall P_1(x_1, y_1), P_2(x_2, y_2) \in \mathcal{R} \text{ and } \lambda \in [0, 1], \\ &\phantom{To Prove:} \quad P_\lambda = \lambda P_1 + (1 - \lambda) P_2 \in \mathcal{R}. \end{aligned} $$

Proof: Let $P_1 = (x_1, y_1) \in \mathcal{R}$ and $P_2 = (x_2, y_2) \in \mathcal{R}$. Then for each constraint $i \in \{1, \dots, k\}$:

$$ a_i x_1 + b_i y_1 \le c_i \quad \text{and} \quad a_i x_2 + b_i y_2 \le c_i $$

with $x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2 \ge 0$.

Consider the convex combination point $P_\lambda = (x_\lambda, y_\lambda)$, where:

$$ x_\lambda = \lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda)x_2, \quad y_\lambda = \lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda)y_2 \quad (\lambda \in [0, 1]) $$

Since $\lambda \ge 0$ and $1 - \lambda \ge 0$, and $x_1, x_2, y_1, y_2 \ge 0$:

$$ x_\lambda \ge 0, \quad y_\lambda \ge 0 $$

Now evaluate the $i$-th linear constraint at $P_\lambda$:

$$ \begin{aligned} a_i x_\lambda + b_i y_\lambda &= a_i [\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda)x_2] + b_i [\lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda)y_2] \\ &= \lambda (a_i x_1 + b_i y_1) + (1 - \lambda)(a_i x_2 + b_i y_2) \end{aligned} $$

Substitute the inequalities $a_i x_1 + b_i y_1 \le c_i$ and $a_i x_2 + b_i y_2 \le c_i$:

$$ \begin{aligned} a_i x_\lambda + b_i y_\lambda &\le \lambda c_i + (1 - \lambda) c_i \\ &= c_i (\lambda + 1 - \lambda) \\ &= c_i \end{aligned} $$

Since this holds for every $i \in \{1, \dots, k\}$ and non-negativity holds, $P_\lambda \in \mathcal{R}$.
Hence, $\mathcal{R}$ is a convex set.

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

2.2 Theorem: Location of Extrema at Extreme Points (Linear Function over Convex Polytope)

$$ \begin{aligned} &Given: \quad Z = \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} \text{ defined over convex feasible region } \mathcal{R} \text{ with corner points } \{V_1, V_2, \dots, V_p\}. \\ &To Prove: \quad \text{If } Z \text{ attains a minimum (or maximum) on } \mathcal{R}, \text{ that value occurs at some vertex } V_k. \end{aligned} $$

Proof: By the Minkowski-Weyl representation theorem for polyhedra, any point $\mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{R}$ can be expressed as a convex combination of its extreme points plus a non-negative combination of extreme rays. In any bounded sub-polyhedron:

$$ \mathbf{x} = \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j V_j, \quad \text{where } \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j = 1 \text{ and } \lambda_j \ge 0 \; \forall j $$

Evaluate $Z(\mathbf{x})$:

$$ \begin{aligned} Z(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} &= \mathbf{c}^T \left(\sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j V_j\right) \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j (\mathbf{c}^T V_j) \\ &= \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j Z(V_j) \end{aligned} $$

Let $m = \min_{1 \le j \le p} Z(V_j)$ and $M = \max_{1 \le j \le p} Z(V_j)$.
Because $\lambda_j \ge 0$:

$$ \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j Z(V_j) \ge \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j m = m \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j = m $$

$$ \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j Z(V_j) \le \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j M = M \sum_{j=1}^p \lambda_j = M $$

Thus:

$$ m \le Z(\mathbf{x}) \le M \quad \forall \mathbf{x} \in \mathcal{R} $$

Equality $Z(\mathbf{x}) = m$ holds when $\lambda_k = 1$ for the index $k$ where $Z(V_k) = m$ and $\lambda_{j \ne k} = 0$.
Hence, the minimum must occur at a corner point.

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

2.3 Mathematical Proof of the Open Half-Plane Verification Method

$$ \begin{aligned} &Given: \quad \text{Feasible region } \mathcal{R} \text{ is unbounded. Corner points yield candidate minimum } m = \min_i Z(V_i). \\ &To Prove: \quad Z \ge m \; \forall (x, y) \in \mathcal{R} \iff \mathcal{R} \cap \{(x, y) : ax + by < m\} = \emptyset. \end{aligned} $$

Proof:

  1. Sufficiency ($\Leftarrow$): Assume $\mathcal{R} \cap \{(x, y) : ax + by < m\} = \emptyset$.
    Let $(x_0, y_0)$ be an arbitrary point in $\mathcal{R}$.
    Since $(x_0, y_0) \in \mathcal{R}$, it cannot belong to the set $\{(x, y) : ax + by < m\}$ (by the null intersection assumption).
    Therefore, by trichotomy of real numbers:

$$ ax_0 + by_0 \not< m \implies ax_0 + by_0 \ge m $$

Since this holds for every $(x_0, y_0) \in \mathcal{R}$, $m$ is a lower bound for $Z$ on $\mathcal{R}$.
Because $m = Z(V_k)$ for some $V_k \in \mathcal{R}$, $m$ is attained in $\mathcal{R}$.
Therefore, $\min_{(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}} Z(x, y) = m$.

  1. Necessity ($\Rightarrow$): Assume the minimum value of $Z$ on $\mathcal{R}$ is $m$.
    Then:

$$ ax + by \ge m \quad \forall (x, y) \in \mathcal{R} $$

Suppose for contradiction that $\mathcal{R} \cap \{(x, y) : ax + by < m\} \ne \emptyset$.
Then there exists some point $(x^*, y^*) \in \mathcal{R}$ such that:

$$ ax^* + by^* < m $$

This directly contradicts that $m$ is the absolute minimum of $Z$ on $\mathcal{R}$.
Hence, the intersection must be empty.

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & NCERT Foundations

1.1 Core Definitions & Terminology

1. Optimization Problem

A problem which seeks to maximize or minimize a given linear function subject to certain linear constraints is called an Optimization Problem. Linear programming is a specialized branch dealing with linear objective functions and linear constraints.

2. Objective Function ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

A linear function $Z = ax + by$, where $a, b$ are real constants and $x, y$ are decision variables, which is to be maximized or minimized subject to certain constraints, is called the Objective Function.

3. Decision Variables ⭐⭐⭐⭐

The non-negative variables $x$ and $y$ whose optimal values are to be determined to maximize or minimize $Z$. In manufacturing and resource allocation problems, these typically represent quantities of items produced, batches processed, or hours allocated.

4. Linear Constraints ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The linear inequalities, equations, or restrictions imposed on the decision variables:

$$ a_i x + b_i y \le c_i \quad \text{or} \quad a_i x + b_i y \ge c_i \quad (i = 1, 2, \dots, m) $$

5. Non-Negative Restrictions ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The constraints $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$. In physical contexts (manufacturing, units, resources), quantities cannot be negative. Geometrically, these restrict the problem to the First Quadrant of the Cartesian plane.

6. Feasible Region & Infeasible Region ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Feasible Region: The common region determined by all the constraints including non-negative constraints $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ simultaneously. It is the intersection of all half-planes defined by the constraints.
  • Infeasible Region: Any point outside the feasible region is infeasible.
  • Feasible Solution: Any point $(x_0, y_0)$ lying within or on the boundary of the feasible region.
  • Infeasible Solution: Any point lying outside the feasible region.
  • Optimal Solution: Any feasible solution that optimizes (maximizes or minimizes) the objective function $Z$.

7. Bounded vs. Unbounded Feasible Region ⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Bounded Region: A feasible region is said to be bounded if it can be enclosed within a circle of sufficiently large radius $R$ centered at the origin:

$$ \exists R > 0 \quad \text{such that} \quad x^2 + y^2 \le R^2 \quad \forall (x, y) \in \mathcal{R} $$

  • Unbounded Region: A feasible region is unbounded if it extends indefinitely in any direction (it cannot be contained within any circle of finite radius).

1.2 Core Theorems & Geometric Interpretations

Fundamental Theorem 1 (Corner Point Theorem) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the feasible region (convex polygon) for a linear programming problem and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. When $\mathcal{R}$ is bounded, the maximum and minimum values of $Z$ occur at corner points (vertices) of $\mathcal{R}$.

Fundamental Theorem 2 ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the feasible region for a linear programming problem and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. If $\mathcal{R}$ is bounded, then $Z$ has both a maximum and a minimum value on $\mathcal{R}$ and each of these occurs at a corner point of $\mathcal{R}$.

If $\mathcal{R}$ is unbounded, then a maximum or a minimum value of the objective function may or may not exist. However, if it exists, it must occur at a corner point of $\mathcal{R}$.

Condition for Unbounded Region ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

For an unbounded feasible region $\mathcal{R}$: 1. Let $M$ be the maximum value obtained among all corner points. $M$ is the actual maximum of $Z = ax + by$ over $\mathcal{R}$ if and only if the open half-plane determined by:

$$ ax + by > M $$

has no point in common with the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$. If it has common points, $Z$ has no maximum. 2. Let $m$ be the minimum value obtained among all corner points. $m$ is the actual minimum of $Z = ax + by$ over $\mathcal{R}$ if and only if the open half-plane determined by:

$$ ax + by < m $$

has no point in common with the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$. If it has common points, $Z$ has no minimum.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 1: Convexity of the Feasible Region ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definition: Convex Set

A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ is convex if for every pair of points $P_1(x_1, y_1), P_2(x_2, y_2) \in S$, the entire line segment joining $P_1$ and $P_2$ lies in $S$:

$$ \lambda P_1 + (1 - \lambda)P_2 \in S \quad \forall \lambda \in [0, 1] $$

Theorem

The feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ defined by a finite system of linear inequalities is a convex set.

Given:
Let $H_i = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : a_i x + b_i y \le c_i\}$ for $i = 1, 2, \dots, m$, with $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$.
The feasible region is:

$$ \mathcal{R} = \bigcap_{i=1}^{m} H_i \cap \{(x, y) : x \ge 0\} \cap \{(x, y) : y \ge 0\} $$

To Prove:
$\mathcal{R}$ is a convex set.

Step 1: Prove that each individual half-plane $H_i$ is convex.
Let $(x_1, y_1) \in H_i$ and $(x_2, y_2) \in H_i$. By definition:

$$ a_i x_1 + b_i y_1 \le c_i \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

$$ a_i x_2 + b_i y_2 \le c_i \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Let $\lambda \in [0, 1]$. Consider a convex combination point $P_\lambda = (\bar{x}, \bar{y})$ where:

$$ \bar{x} = \lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda) x_2 $$

$$ \bar{y} = \lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda) y_2 $$

Now evaluate the linear expression at $P_\lambda$:

$$ a_i \bar{x} + b_i \bar{y} = a_i [\lambda x_1 + (1 - \lambda) x_2] + b_i [\lambda y_1 + (1 - \lambda) y_2] $$

Regrouping terms:

$$ a_i \bar{x} + b_i \bar{y} = \lambda (a_i x_1 + b_i y_1) + (1 - \lambda)(a_i x_2 + b_i y_2) $$

Since $\lambda \ge 0$ and $1 - \lambda \ge 0$, we substitute inequalities (1) and (2):

$$ a_i \bar{x} + b_i \bar{y} \le \lambda c_i + (1 - \lambda) c_i = c_i[\lambda + 1 - \lambda] = c_i $$

Hence:

$$ a_i \bar{x} + b_i \bar{y} \le c_i \implies P_\lambda \in H_i $$

Thus, each half-plane $H_i$ is a convex set.

Step 2: Intersection of convex sets is convex.
Let $S_1$ and $S_2$ be convex sets, and let $S = S_1 \cap S_2$.
Let $P_1, P_2 \in S \implies P_1, P_2 \in S_1$ and $P_1, P_2 \in S_2$.
Because $S_1$ is convex, the segment $[\lambda P_1 + (1-\lambda) P_2] \in S_1$.
Because $S_2$ is convex, the segment $[\lambda P_1 + (1-\lambda) P_2] \in S_2$.
Therefore, $[\lambda P_1 + (1-\lambda) P_2] \in S_1 \cap S_2 = S$.
Extending by mathematical induction to any finite number of half-planes:

$$ \mathcal{R} = \left(\bigcap_{i=1}^m H_i\right) \cap H_x \cap H_y $$

is the intersection of convex sets.

Conclusion:
The feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ is a convex set. $\quad \blacksquare$

Proof 2: The Corner Point Theorem (Extreme Point Optimality) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem

If the linear objective function $Z = ax + by$ attains an optimum (maximum or minimum) value on a bounded feasible region $\mathcal{R}$, then this optimum value must occur at least at one of the extreme points (corner points/vertices) of $\mathcal{R}$.

Given:
1. A bounded convex polygonal region $\mathcal{R} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ having $k$ corner points $V_1, V_2, \dots, V_k$. 2. Every point $P(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$ can be expressed as a convex combination of its vertices (by the Krein-Milman Theorem / Minkowski's Theorem for polytopes):

$$ P = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \lambda_j V_j \quad \text{where } \lambda_j \ge 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \sum_{j=1}^{k} \lambda_j = 1 $$

  1. Objective function $Z(P) = ax + by = \mathbf{c}^T P$, where $\mathbf{c} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$.

To Prove:

$$ \max_{P \in \mathcal{R}} Z(P) = \max_{j \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}} Z(V_j) $$

Derivation:
Let the value of the objective function at each corner point $V_j$ be $Z_j = Z(V_j)$.
Let:

$$ M = \max \{Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_k\} $$

$$ m = \min \{Z_1, Z_2, \dots, Z_k\} $$

Take an arbitrary point $P(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$. By the convex representation of $\mathcal{R}$:

$$ P = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \lambda_j V_j $$

Applying the linear function $Z$:

$$ Z(P) = Z\left(\sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j V_j\right) $$

Because $Z(x, y) = ax + by$ is a linear operator:

$$ Z\left(\sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j V_j\right) = \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j Z(V_j) = \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j Z_j $$

Using the fact that $Z_j \le M$ for all $j \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}$:

$$ Z(P) = \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j Z_j \le \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j M = M \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j $$

Since $\sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j = 1$:

$$ Z(P) \le M(1) = M $$

Similarly, using $Z_j \ge m$ for all $j$:

$$ Z(P) = \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j Z_j \ge \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j m = m \sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j = m $$

Thus, for all points $P \in \mathcal{R}$:

$$ m \le Z(P) \le M $$

Because $M = Z(V_r)$ for some corner point $V_r$, the value $M$ is actually attained at $V_r \in \mathcal{R}$.
Likewise, $m = Z(V_s)$ is attained at some corner point $V_s \in \mathcal{R}$.

Conclusion:
The maximum and minimum values of $Z$ must occur at the corner points of $\mathcal{R}$. $\quad \blacksquare$

Proof 3: Condition for Multiple Optimal Solutions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem

If the objective function $Z = ax + by$ achieves its optimal value at two distinct corner points $V_1(x_1, y_1)$ and $V_2(x_2, y_2)$, then $Z$ achieves the same optimal value at every point on the line segment joining $V_1$ and $V_2$.

Given:
$Z(V_1) = Z(V_2) = M$ (maximum value).
$V_1, V_2 \in \mathcal{R}$.

To Prove:
For any point $Q$ on the segment $[V_1, V_2]$, $Z(Q) = M$.

Derivation:
Any point $Q$ on the line segment connecting $V_1$ and $V_2$ can be parameterized by:

$$ Q = \mu V_1 + (1 - \mu) V_2 \quad \text{for } 0 \le \mu \le 1 $$

Evaluating $Z(Q)$:

$$ Z(Q) = Z(\mu V_1 + (1 - \mu) V_2) $$

By linearity of $Z$:

$$ Z(Q) = \mu Z(V_1) + (1 - \mu) Z(V_2) $$

Substitute $Z(V_1) = M$ and $Z(V_2) = M$:

$$ Z(Q) = \mu M + (1 - \mu) M = M(\mu + 1 - \mu) = M $$

Geometric Condition:
The level curve (isoprofit/isocost line) $ax + by = M$ is parallel to and coincides with the boundary edge connecting $V_1$ and $V_2$.
Therefore, the slope of the objective line equals the slope of the constraint boundary line:

$$ -\frac{a}{b} = -\frac{a_k}{b_k} $$

Conclusion:
There are infinitely many optimal solutions along the edge $[V_1, V_2]$. $\quad \blacksquare$

From $L_1$, $x = 10 - 2y$. Substitute into $L_2$:

$$ 3(10 - 2y) + 4y = 24 \implies 30 - 6y + 4y = 24 \implies -2y = -6 \implies y = 3 $$

$$ x = 10 - 2(3) = 4 $$

Intersection point $P = (4, 3)$.

Step 3: Vertices of Feasible Region
The region lies between $L_1$ and $L_2$ in the first quadrant: - On the $y$-axis: between $(0, 5)$ and $(0, 6)$. - Intersection point: $(4, 3)$. - Notice that on the $x$-axis, $L_1$ intersects at $(10, 0)$ while $L_2$ intersects at $(8, 0)$. Since the region requires $x + 2y \ge 10$ and $3x + 4y \le 24$, at $y=0$, $x \ge 10$ and $x \le 8$, which is impossible. Hence the region does not touch the $x$-axis. The corner points of the closed bounded region are:

$$ A(0, 5), \quad B(0, 6), \quad C(4, 3) $$

Step 4: Corner Point Evaluation

Corner Point $Z = 200x + 500y$ Value

$A(0, 5)$ $200(0) + 500(5) = 2500$ 2500
$B(0, 6)$ $200(0) + 500(6) = 3000$ 3000

The minimum value of $Z$ is 2300, achieved at $x = 4, y = 3$.

Problem 4: Strict Unbounded Minimization with Open Half-Plane Test [CBSE 2020, 5 Marks]

Statement

Minimize $Z = 3x + 2y$ subject to:

$$ x + y \ge 8 $$

$$ 3x + 5y \ge 15 $$

$$ x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0 $$

Full Step-by-Step Solution

Step 1: Constraint Lines
1. $L_1: x + y = 8 \implies (0, 8)$ and $(8, 0)$.
$x + y \ge 8$: region away from origin. 2. $L_2: 3x + 5y = 15 \implies (0, 3)$ and $(5, 0)$.
$3x + 5y \ge 15$: region away from origin.

Notice that for all points where $x+y \ge 8$, we have:

$$ 3x + 5y = 3(x+y) + 2y \ge 3(8) + 0 = 24 > 15 $$

Thus, the constraint $3x + 5y \ge 15$ is redundant! The feasible region is entirely governed by $x + y \ge 8, x \ge 0, y \ge 0$.

Step 2: Region Analysis
The feasible region is unbounded, extending infinitely away from the origin in the first quadrant, with corner points:

$$ A(8, 0) \quad \text{and} \quad B(0, 8) $$

Step 3: Corner Point Evaluation

Corner Point $Z = 3x + 2y$ Value
$A(8, 0)$ $3(8) + 2(0) = 24$ 24
$B(0, 8)$ $3(0) + 2(8) = \mathbf{16}$ Smallest Corner Value ($m$)

Step 4: The Mandatory Half-Plane Test for Unbounded Feasible Region
Since the region is unbounded, $m = 16$ may or may not be the minimum value.
We must check whether the open half-plane:

$$ 3x + 2y < 16 $$

shares any common points with the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$.

  1. Graph the boundary line $3x + 2y = 16$ as a dashed line.
  2. Intercepts: $(0, 8)$ and $\left(\frac{16}{3}, 0\right) = (5.33, 0)$.
  3. Test point $(0, 0)$: $3(0) + 2(0) = 0 < 16$ (True). The open half-plane lies strictly towards the origin.
  4. Compare with feasible region $\mathcal{R}$:
    The feasible region lies on or above the line $x + y = 8$.
    Any point in $\mathcal{R}$ satisfies $x + y \ge 8$.
    For any $(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$:

$$ 3x + 2y = 2(x + y) + x \ge 2(8) + x = 16 + x $$

Since $x \ge 0$ in the first quadrant, $3x + 2y \ge 16$.
Hence, $3x + 2y < 16$ contains NO point in common with the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ (except that the dashed line touches $B(0, 8)$, but the inequality is strictly less than).

Conclusion:
Since the open half-plane $3x + 2y < 16$ has no points in common with the feasible region, the minimum value of $Z$ is indeed 16, occurring at $x = 0, y = 8$.

5. Examiner Traps & Common Board Mistakes

Trap 1: Failing the Unbounded Feasible Region Test ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • The Mistake: Stating the minimum or maximum value directly from the corner point table for an unbounded region without drawing the open half-plane.
  • Board Penalty: Direct deduction of $1.5$ to $2$ marks.
  • Remedy: Always write:

    "Since the feasible region is unbounded, we must check the open half-plane $ax + by < m$ (for minimum) or $ax + by > M$ (for maximum). Drawing the dashed line $ax + by = m$, we observe that the half-plane has no common points with the feasible region. Hence, $m$ is the minimum value."

Trap 2: Incompatible Time Units ⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • The Mistake: Mixing hours and minutes (e.g., writing $4x + 6y \le 8$ when machine times are in minutes and available capacity is 8 hours).
  • Remedy: Convert all time quantities into the smallest given unit (minutes) before setting up inequalities:

$$ \text{Capacity} = 8 \text{ hours} = 480 \text{ minutes} $$

Trap 3: Solid vs. Dashed Lines ⭐⭐⭐

  • The Mistake: Drawing strict inequalities or open test lines as solid lines.
  • Remedy:
  • Standard constraints ($\le, \ge$) $\implies$ Solid lines.
  • Half-plane test lines ($<, >$) $\implies$ Dashed / dotted lines.

Trap 4: Forgetting Non-Negative Restrictions ⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • The Mistake: Omitting $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ in the mathematical formulation.
  • Board Penalty: Loss of $1$ mark in the formulation stage.
  • Remedy: State non-negative restrictions explicitly on a separate line at the end of every formulation.

Trap 5: Misidentifying the Region of Intersection ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • The Mistake: Shading the region satisfying either constraint instead of all constraints simultaneously.
  • Remedy: Test $(0,0)$ on every single line individually. Shade small directional arrows on each boundary line first; the region where all arrows converge is the true feasible region.

1. Intercept Form Shortcut for Lightning Fast Plotting

Do not create long $T$-tables of values for graphing. Convert $Ax + By = C$ directly to intercept form:

$$ \frac{x}{C/A} + \frac{y}{C/B} = 1 $$

  • Plot $\left(\frac{C}{A}, 0\right)$ on the $x$-axis.
  • Plot $\left(0, \frac{C}{B}\right)$ on the $y$-axis.
  • Join with a straight ruler edge immediately.

2. Cramer's Rule for Vertex Calculation

For intersecting boundary lines:

$$ a_1 x + b_1 y = c_1 $$

$$ a_2 x + b_2 y = c_2 $$

Compute determinants mentally:

$$ D = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1, \quad D_x = c_1 b_2 - c_2 b_1, \quad D_y = a_1 c_2 - a_2 c_1 $$

$$ x = \frac{D_x}{D}, \quad y = \frac{D_y}{D} $$

This eliminates algebraic substitution slips under exam conditions.

3. Gradient / Slope Check for Multiple Optimal Solutions

  • Slope of constraint line $= -\frac{A}{B}$
  • Slope of objective function $= -\frac{a}{b}$
  • Golden Rule: If the ratio of coefficients in the objective function equals the ratio of coefficients in any binding constraint line:

$$ \frac{a}{b} = \frac{A_k}{B_k} $$

then multiple optimal solutions exist along that entire edge segment.

4. Memory Mnemonics

  • "At most" $\le$ (Cap / Ceiling): Resources, available hours, raw material stock cannot be exceeded.
  • "At least" $\ge$ (Floor / Target): Minimum nutritional intake, minimum production quotas, guaranteed output.

The store must deliver grain to three fair-price ration shops, $A, B,$ and $C$, whose weekly demands are 25, 35, and 30 quintals, respectively. The transportation cost per quintal (in ₹) is given below:

Godown P 16 10 15
Godown Q 10 12 10

Formulate an LPP to minimize total transportation cost.

Step 1: Allocation Diagram & Variable Selection Notice that the total supply is $40 + 50 = 90$ quintals, and total demand is $25 + 35 + 30 = 90$ quintals (Balanced problem).

Then: * Godown $P$ supply to Shop $C = 40 - (x + y)$ quintals.

5. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap 1: Omitting Non-Negativity Constraints ($x \ge 0, y \ge 0$)

  • The Error: A student writes the constraints $3x + 2y \le 12$ and $x + 2y \le 8$, but fails to write $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$.
  • Examiner Penalty: Deducts $\frac{1}{2}$ to 1 Mark immediately under CBSE marking criteria.
  • Mathematical Consequence: Without $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$, the feasible region extends into Quadrants II, III, and IV, making the system unbounded or physically invalid.

Trap 2: Inequality Reversal ("At least" vs "At most")

  • The Error: Seeing the word "minimum requirement of 50 units" and writing $x + y \le 50$.
  • The Fix:
  • "At least", "Minimum", "No less than" $\implies \mathbf{\ge}$
  • "At most", "Maximum", "Not exceeding" $\implies \mathbf{\le}$

Trap 3: Unit Inconsistency Across Variables and Constants

  • The Error: Time per unit given in minutes ($20\text{ min}$ for $A$, $30\text{ min}$ for $B$), while total available machine time is given in hours ($5\text{ hours}$).

$$ \text{Incorrect: } 20x + 30y \le 5 $$

  • The Fix: Convert all quantities to identical units before writing the inequality:

$$ 20x + 30y \le 5 \times 60 \implies 20x + 30y \le 300 \implies 2x + 3y \le 30 $$

Trap 4: Ratio and Percentage Translation Errors

  • The Error: "Type $A$ artifacts must be at least $30\%$ of total production."

$$ \text{Incorrect: } x \ge 0.30y $$

  • The Fix: Total production is $(x + y)$. Thus:

$$ x \ge 0.30(x + y) \implies x \ge 0.3x + 0.3y \implies 0.7x - 0.3y \ge 0 \implies 7x - 3y \ge 0 $$

Trap 5: Forgetting to Check the Open Half-Plane in Unbounded Regions

  • The Error: In an unbounded feasible region, finding the minimum value $m$ at a corner point and directly concluding that $m$ is the answer without verifying whether the open half-plane $ax + by < m$ intersects the feasible region.
  • The Fix: Always graph $ax + by < m$ as a dashed line. Explicitly state whether the open half-plane shares any common points with the feasible region.

6. Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Board Exams

1. The Intercept Form Rapid Plotting Rule

Never plot boundary lines by making a table of arbitrary $(x, y)$ values. Use the two-intercept form:

$$ \frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1 $$

  • For $3x + 4y = 24$, divide by 24:

$$ \frac{x}{8} + \frac{y}{6} = 1 $$

Instantly mark $(8, 0)$ on the $x$-axis and $(0, 6)$ on the $y$-axis, then connect them with a straight line.

2. Origin Test for Half-Plane Shading

For any linear constraint of the form $ax + by \le c$ or $ax + by \ge c$ where $c \ne 0$: 1. Substitute the origin $(0, 0)$ into the inequality. 2. If the inequality holds true ($0 \le c$), shade the region containing $(0, 0)$. 3. If it is false ($0 \ge c$, where $c > 0$), shade the opposite region away from $(0, 0)$. * Exception: If the line passes through the origin ($ax + by \le 0$), test $(1, 0)$ or $(0, 1)$ instead.

3. Objective Line ($Z$-Line) Sweep Shortcut

  • Write $Z = ax + by \implies y = -\frac{a}{b}x + \frac{Z}{b}$.
  • Visualize a ruler with slope $m = -\frac{a}{b}$.
  • For Maximization, slide the ruler parallel to itself upwards and rightwards; the last vertex it touches is your optimal point.
  • For Minimization, slide it downwards and leftwards; the first vertex it touches is your minimum point.

4. Bounded Region Vertex Counter Check

  • An $n$-line constraint system in the first quadrant typically yields a convex polygon with at most $n + 2$ vertices.
  • Every corner point is the intersection of two boundary lines. Set their equations simultaneously to find the exact coordinates:

$$ \begin{cases} a_1 x + b_1 y = c_1 \\ a_2 x + b_2 y = c_2 \end{cases} \implies (x, y) = \left(\frac{c_1 b_2 - c_2 b_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}, \frac{a_1 c_2 - a_2 c_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}\right) $$

5. Speed Hacks, Mental Math & Verification Shortcuts

5.1 The Slope-Vector / Level Curve ($Z$-Line) Mental Check

The objective function can be viewed as a family of parallel lines (level curves / iso-profit lines):

$$ ax + by = k \implies y = -\frac{a}{b}x + \frac{k}{b} $$

  • The slope of the objective function line is:

$$ m_Z = -\frac{a}{b} $$

  • If you arrange the boundary lines of your feasible polygon in order of their slopes:
  • As $k$ increases, the line moves in the direction of the gradient vector $\nabla Z = (a, b)$.
  • The last vertex the level curve touches before completely exiting the feasible region is the maximum.
  • The first vertex it encounters upon entering is the minimum.

5.2 The Multiple Optima Parallelism Indicator

Before computing values, calculate the slope of the objective line and compare it with the slopes of the boundary constraints:

$$ m_Z = -\frac{a}{b}, \quad m_i = -\frac{a_i}{b_i} $$

$$ \text{If } m_Z = m_i \text{ for any active boundary constraint,} $$

then the objective line is parallel to that edge of the feasible polygon. If that edge happens to contain the optimal corner point, Theorem 2 applies immediately, yielding infinite solutions along that entire edge.

5.3 Quick Intercept Memory Rule

To plot $a_i x + b_i y \le c_i$ in under 5 seconds:

$$ x\text{-intercept} = \frac{c_i}{a_i}, \quad y\text{-intercept} = \frac{c_i}{b_i} $$

Mark these two dots directly on the axes and draw the line immediately without constructing a scratch table.

Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2024, 5 Marks]

Problem Statement:
Minimize $Z = 50x + 70y$ subject to the constraints:

$$ 2x + y \ge 8 $$

$$ x + 2y \ge 10 $$

$$ x, y \ge 0 $$

Step 1: Conversion to Equations & Determination of Intercepts

  1. For line $L_1: 2x + y = 8$:
  2. At $y = 0 \implies x = 4 \implies (4, 0)$
  3. At $x = 0 \implies y = 8 \implies (0, 8)$
  4. For line $L_2: x + 2y = 10$:
  5. At $y = 0 \implies x = 10 \implies (10, 0)$
  6. At $x = 0 \implies y = 5 \implies (0, 5)$

Step 2: Feasible Region Identification

  • Test $(0, 0)$ in $2x + y \ge 8 \implies 0 \ge 8$ (False $\implies$ Region excludes origin).
  • Test $(0, 0)$ in $x + 2y \ge 10 \implies 0 \ge 10$ (False $\implies$ Region excludes origin).
  • With $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$, the region $\mathcal{R}$ lies in the first quadrant, above both lines. $\mathcal{R}$ is unbounded.

Step 3: Intersection of Boundary Lines

Solve $2x + y = 8$ and $x + 2y = 10$:

$$ y = 8 - 2x \implies x + 2(8 - 2x) = 10 \implies -3x + 16 = 10 \implies 3x = 6 \implies x = 2 $$

$$ y = 8 - 2(2) = 4 $$

Corner point of intersection: $B(2, 4)$.

Step 4: Corner Point Table

The corner points of the unbounded region $\mathcal{R}$ are: - $A(0, 8)$ - $B(2, 4)$ - $C(10, 0)$

Corner Point $(x, y)$ $Z = 50x + 70y$ Value
$A(0, 8)$ $50(0) + 70(8) = 0 + 560$ $560$
$B(2, 4)$ $50(2) + 70(4) = 100 + 280$ $\mathbf{380}$
$C(10, 0)$ $50(10) + 70(0) = 500 + 0$ $500$

Candidate minimum value: $m = 380$ at $B(2, 4)$.

Step 5: Open Half-Plane Verification (Crucial Step)

Since the region is unbounded, $m = 380$ may or may not be the minimum value.
We determine whether the open half-plane:

$$ 50x + 70y < 380 \iff 5x + 7y < 38 $$

has any common points with the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$.

  1. Graph the boundary line $5x + 7y = 38$ as a dashed line:
  2. $x = 0 \implies y = \frac{38}{7} \approx 5.43 \implies (0, 5.43)$
  3. $y = 0 \implies x = \frac{38}{5} = 7.6 \implies (7.6, 0)$
  4. Passing through corner point $B(2, 4)$: $5(2) + 7(4) = 10 + 28 = 38$ (Point $B$ is on the line).
  5. The open half-plane $5x + 7y < 38$ represents the region strictly towards the origin below the dashed line.
  6. Feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ lies entirely on and above the envelope formed by segment $AB$ and $BC$.
    The dashed line touches $\mathcal{R}$ only at $B(2, 4)$. But the open half-plane $5x + 7y < 38$ excludes its boundary line.
  7. Hence:

$$ \mathcal{R} \cap \{(x, y) : 5x + 7y < 38\} = \emptyset $$

Conclusion:
The minimum value of $Z$ is $\mathbf{380}$, occurring at $(2, 4)$.

Problem Statement:
Minimize $Z = -50x + 20y$ subject to:

$$ 2x - y \ge -5 \iff -2x + y \le 5 $$

$$ 3x + y \ge 3 $$

$$ 2x - 3y \le 12 $$

$$ x, y \ge 0 $$

Step 1: Lines & Feasible Region

  1. $L_1: -2x + y = 5$: Points $(0, 5), (-2.5, 0)$. Test $(0,0): 0 \le 5$ (True).
  2. $L_2: 3x + y = 3$: Points $(1, 0), (0, 3)$. Test $(0,0): 0 \ge 3$ (False).
  3. $L_3: 2x - 3y = 12$: Points $(6, 0), (0, -4)$. Test $(0,0): 0 \le 12$ (True).

The feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ is unbounded in the first quadrant, extending rightward and upward.

Step 2: Corner Points

  • $A(0, 5)$ (Intersection of $y$-axis and $L_1$)
  • $B(0, 3)$ (Intersection of $y$-axis and $L_2$)
  • $C(1, 0)$ (Intersection of $x$-axis and $L_2$)
  • $D(6, 0)$ (Intersection of $x$-axis and $L_3$)
Corner Point $Z = -50x + 20y$ Value
$A(0, 5)$ $-50(0) + 20(5)$ $100$
$B(0, 3)$ $-50(0) + 20(3)$ $60$
$C(1, 0)$ $-50(1) + 20(0)$ $-50$
$D(6, 0)$ $-50(6) + 20(0)$ $\mathbf{-300}$

Candidate minimum: $m = -300$ at $D(6, 0)$.

Construct the inequality:

$$ -50x + 20y < -300 \iff -5x + 2y < -30 \iff 5x - 2y > 30 $$

Trap 1: Halting at the Corner Point Table

  • Error: Student identifies the smallest value in the table and declares it as the final answer without mentioning the open half-plane.
  • CBSE Marking Scheme Penalty: For a 5-mark question, 2 full marks are allocated specifically to the open half-plane statement and its verification diagram. Skipping this yields a maximum of 3/5.

Trap 2: Drawing the Auxiliary Line as Solid

  • Error: Drawing $ax + by = m$ as a solid line instead of a broken/dashed line.
  • Correction: The inequality is strictly open ($ax + by < m$). The boundary line is not included in the open half-plane. A solid line implies the boundary belongs to the set, creating confusion about intersections at corner points.

Trap 3: Confusing Boundary Intersection with Open Region Intersection

  • Scenario: The dashed line $ax + by = m$ passes through the optimal corner point $B(x_k, y_k)$.
  • False Conclusion: Student writes: "The line passes through $B$, so it has a common point with $\mathcal{R}$; therefore, no minimum exists."
  • Mathematical Reality: The point $B$ satisfies $ax + by = m$, not $ax + by < m$. The point $B$ is not in the open half-plane. Unless the open region (the area strictly beyond the dashed line) contains points of $\mathcal{R}$, the intersection remains empty: $\mathcal{R} \cap H_< = \emptyset$.

Trap 4: Reversed Inequalities with Negative Coefficients

  • Given $Z = -3x + 4y$, candidate minimum $-12$.
  • Dividing $-3x + 4y < -12$ by $-1$:

$$ 3x - 4y > 12 $$

  • Students frequently fail to flip the inequality sign when dividing or multiplying by negative scalars, leading to testing the wrong half-plane.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

5.1 The Slope-Ray Gradient Test (30-Second Verification)

To confirm whether an open half-plane shares points with an unbounded region without drawing extensive test grids: 1. Identify the rays extending to infinity in $\mathcal{R}$. These rays are determined by their directional vectors $\mathbf{d} = (d_x, d_y)$ where $d_x \ge 0, d_y \ge 0$. 2. For $Z = ax + by$, compute the directional derivative:

$$ \nabla Z \cdot \mathbf{d} = a d_x + b d_y $$

  1. Rules:
  2. If $\nabla Z \cdot \mathbf{d} > 0$ for all infinite rays $\mathbf{d}$ in $\mathcal{R}$, then $Z$ grows without bound as you move along any ray $\implies$ Minimum is guaranteed to exist.
  3. If $\nabla Z \cdot \mathbf{d} < 0$ for any ray $\mathbf{d}$ in $\mathcal{R}$, then $Z \to -\infty$ along that ray $\implies$ No minimum exists.

5.2 The Non-Negative Objective Shortcut

Golden Rule:
If all constraints are of the form $\sum a_{ij} x_j \ge b_i$ with $x, y \ge 0$ and $b_i > 0$, and the objective function $Z = ax + by$ has strictly positive coefficients ($a > 0, b > 0$), then: 1. $\lim_{x, y \to \infty} Z(x, y) = +\infty$. 2. A minimum ALWAYS exists and is guaranteed to be the minimum of the corner points. 3. A maximum NEVER exists (it is unbounded above).

Board Application: If you see $Z = 50x + 70y$ with standard positive constraints, you know the minimum must validate successfully. If your half-plane test shows common points, double-check your algebra—you made an arithmetic error.

    The feasible region is unbounded.
    The corner points of this unbounded region are:
  2. $A(0, 5)$
  3. $B(0, 3)$
  4. $C(1, 0)$ (intersection of $3x + y = 3$ and $y = 0$)
  5. $D(6, 0)$ (intersection of $2x - 3y = 12$ and $y = 0$)

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 Fast Dual-Intercept Method for Plotting Lines

Never compute table values of $(x, y)$ with 3–4 points. Use the two-intercept form $\frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} = 1$: * For $Ax + By \le C$ or $Ax + By \ge C$: * Set $y = 0 \implies x = \frac{C}{A} \implies \text{Point } P\left(\frac{C}{A}, 0\right)$ * Set $x = 0 \implies y = \frac{C}{B} \implies \text{Point } Q\left(0, \frac{C}{B}\right)$ * Plot these two axis intercepts directly and draw the straight line.

1.2 The "Origin Test" & Sign Rule for Half-Planes

  • If $C \ne 0$: Substitute $(0,0)$ into $Ax + By \le C$ (or $\ge C$).
  • If $0 \le C$ is True, shade towards $(0,0)$.
  • If False, shade strictly away from $(0,0)$.
  • If $C = 0$ ($Ax + By \le 0$ or $y \ge mx$ passing through origin):
  • Never test $(0,0)$ (yields $0 = 0$, inconclusive).
  • Test a clear axis point: $(1, 0)$ on the positive x-axis or $(0, 1)$ on the positive y-axis.

1.3 Rapid Corner-Point Verification (Cramer's Rule / Cross-Multiplication)

For two intersecting lines $a_1 x + b_1 y = c_1$ and $a_2 x + b_2 y = c_2$, solve the intersection point mentally:

$$ x = \frac{c_1 b_2 - c_2 b_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1}, \quad y = \frac{a_1 c_2 - a_2 c_1}{a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1} $$

This avoids cluttered rough working and ensures error-free coordinates for vertices.

1.4 Level Curve (Iso-Profit / Iso-Cost Line) Technique for Instant Verification

Before calculating $Z = ax + by$ at every corner: * Rewrite $Z = ax + by \implies y = -\frac{a}{b}x + \frac{Z}{b}$. * The slope of the objective function is $m = -\frac{a}{b}$. * Comparing this slope with the slopes of the boundary lines ($m_i = -\frac{A_i}{B_i}$) instantly identifies which extreme corner point will be hit last when sweeping the line across the feasible region.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

3.1 Fundamental Theorems (Chapter 12 NCERT)

  • Theorem 1: Let $R$ be the feasible region (convex polygon) for an LPP and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. When $Z$ has an optimal value (maximum or minimum), where the variables $x$ and $y$ are subject to constraints described by linear inequalities, this optimal value must occur at a corner point (vertex) of the feasible region.
  • Theorem 2: Let $R$ be the feasible region for a linear programming problem, and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function:
  • If $R$ is bounded, then the objective function $Z$ attains both a maximum and a minimum value on $R$ and each of these occurs at a corner point of $R$.
  • If $R$ is unbounded, then a maximum or minimum value of the objective function may or may not exist. However, if it exists, it must occur at a corner point of $R$.

3.2 High-Yield Edge Cases

  • Convex Feasible Region: The feasible region of an LPP is always a convex polygonal set. Any line segment joining any two points within the region lies completely inside the region.
  • Multiple Optimal Solutions (Infinite Solutions):
  • If the objective function $Z = ax + by$ assumes the same optimal value at two distinct adjacent vertices $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, then $Z$ attains the same optimal value at every point on the line segment joining these two vertices.
  • Infeasible Problem (No Solution):
  • When there is no common region satisfying all constraints simultaneously, the feasible region is empty $\implies$ LPP has no feasible solution.
  • Redundant Constraints:
  • Constraints whose inequality boundary does not touch or define the feasible region. Removing them does not alter the optimal solution.

Linear Programming: Formulation & Graphical Solution of Diet, Cost Minimization, and Transportation Problems

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Foundations

1.1 Fundamental Definitions (Strict NCERT Framework)

1. Optimization Problem $\left(\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\right)$

A problem that seeks to maximize or minimize a linear function subject to constraints determined by a set of linear inequalities.

2. Objective Function $\left(\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\right)$

A linear function of the form:

$$ Z = ax + by $$

where $a, b$ are real constants and $x, y$ are decision variables, which is to be maximized or minimized under given constraints.

3. Constraints & Non-Negative Restrictions $\left(\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\right)$

Linear inequalities or equations representing restrictions on the decision variables:

$$ a_i x + b_i y \lesseqqgtr d_i \quad (i = 1, 2, \dots, m) $$

The conditions $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ are the non-negative restrictions, ensuring physical feasibility (e.g., non-negative quantities of food or goods).

4. Feasible Region & Feasible Solutions $\left(\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\right)$

  • Feasible Region: The common intersection region determined by all constraints, including the non-negative conditions $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$.
  • Convex Polygon Property: The feasible region of a system of linear inequalities is always a convex set. A set $S \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ is convex if for any two points $P_1, P_2 \in S$, the line segment connecting them lies entirely within $S$:

$$ \lambda P_1 + (1 - \lambda)P_2 \in S \quad \forall \lambda \in [0, 1] $$

  • Feasible Solution: Any coordinate point $(x, y)$ situated inside or on the boundary of the feasible region.
  • Infeasible Solution: Any point lying outside the feasible region.
  • Optimal Solution: Any feasible solution that produces the extreme (maximum or minimum) value of the objective function $Z$.

5. Bounded vs. Unbounded Feasible Regions $\left(\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\right)$

  • Bounded Region: A feasible region that can be completely enclosed inside a disk or circle of sufficiently large radius $R$:

$$ \exists R > 0 \quad \text{such that} \quad \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \le R \quad \forall (x, y) \in \mathcal{R} $$

  • Unbounded Region: A feasible region that extends indefinitely in at least one direction (it cannot be contained within any circle of finite radius). Cost minimization and diet problems frequently yield unbounded regions.

1.2 Fundamental Theorems of Linear Programming

Theorem 1 (Corner Point Theorem — Existence) $\left(\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\right)$

Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the feasible region (convex polygon) for a linear programming problem and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. When $\mathcal{R}$ is bounded, the optimal value (maximum or minimum) of $Z$ occurs at an extreme point (corner point/vertex) of the feasible region.

Theorem 2 (Optimality in Bounded vs. Unbounded Domains) $\left(\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\right)$

Let $\mathcal{R}$ be the feasible region for a linear programming problem and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. 1. If $\mathcal{R}$ is bounded, both a maximum and a minimum of $Z$ exist and each occurs at a corner point of $\mathcal{R}$. 2. If $\mathcal{R}$ is unbounded: - Let $M$ be the maximum value obtained across all corner points. $M$ is the actual maximum of $Z$ over $\mathcal{R}$ if and only if the open half-plane determined by:

$$ ax + by > M $$

 has **no common point** with the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$. Otherwise, $Z$ has no maximum value.
  • Let $m$ be the minimum value obtained across all corner points. $m$ is the actual minimum of $Z$ over $\mathcal{R}$ if and only if the open half-plane determined by:

$$ ax + by < m $$

 has **no common point** with the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$. Otherwise, $Z$ has no minimum value.

2. Complete Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: The Corner Point Theorem for Convex Polygonal Regions

Aim: Prove that the linear objective function $Z = ax + by$ attains its global extrema (maximum/minimum) on a bounded, closed convex polygon $\mathcal{R}$ at one of its vertices (corner points).

Step 1: Formulation and Assumptions

  • Let $\mathcal{R} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$ be a non-empty, closed, bounded convex polygon formed by the intersection of a finite number of closed half-planes.
  • Let the corner points (vertices) of $\mathcal{R}$ be denoted by:

$$ V = \{P_1, P_2, \dots, P_k\} $$

where each vertex has coordinates $P_i = (x_i, y_i)$ for $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}$. * Let the objective function be:

$$ Z(P) = Z(x, y) = ax + by = \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} $$

where $\mathbf{c} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}$. Note that $Z$ is a linear operator:

$$ Z(\lambda \mathbf{u} + \mu \mathbf{v}) = \lambda Z(\mathbf{u}) + \mu Z(\mathbf{v}) \quad \forall \lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{R} $$

Step 2: Convex Combination Representation

By the Minkowski-Weyl Representation Theorem for compact convex polyhedra in $\mathbb{R}^2$, any point $P(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$ can be expressed as a convex linear combination of its extreme points (vertices):

$$ \mathbf{x} = \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i \mathbf{x}_i $$

subject to the conditions:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad \lambda_i \ge 0 \quad \forall i \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\} $$

Step 3: Evaluation of the Objective Function

Evaluate $Z$ at an arbitrary feasible point $P \in \mathcal{R}$:

$$ Z(P) = \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{c}^T \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i \mathbf{x}_i \right) $$

Applying linearity:

$$ Z(P) = \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i (\mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x}_i) = \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i Z(P_i) $$

Step 4: Bounding the Expression

Let the minimum and maximum values of $Z$ among the finite set of vertices $V$ be:

$$ m = \min_{1 \le i \le k} Z(P_i) \quad \text{and} \quad M = \max_{1 \le i \le k} Z(P_i) $$

For every $i \in \{1, 2, \dots, k\}$, we have:

$$ m \le Z(P_i) \le M $$

Because $\lambda_i \ge 0$ for all $i$:

$$ \lambda_i m \le \lambda_i Z(P_i) \le \lambda_i M $$

Summing these inequalities from $i = 1$ to $k$:

$$ \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i m \le \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i Z(P_i) \le \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i M $$

Factoring out $m$ and $M$:

$$ m \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i \right) \le Z(P) \le M \left( \sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i \right) $$

Since $\sum_{i=1}^k \lambda_i = 1$:

$$ m \le Z(P) \le M $$

Step 5: Conclusion

  • The value of $Z(P)$ at any arbitrary point $P \in \mathcal{R}$ cannot exceed $M = \max_{1 \le i \le k} Z(P_i)$, which is attained at a vertex.
  • The value of $Z(P)$ at any arbitrary point $P \in \mathcal{R}$ cannot fall below $m = \min_{1 \le i \le k} Z(P_i)$, which is attained at a vertex.
  • If two adjacent corner points yield the same optimal value (e.g., $Z(P_1) = Z(P_2) = M$), then for any point $Q$ on the segment joining them:

$$ Q = \lambda P_1 + (1 - \lambda) P_2, \quad 0 \le \lambda \le 1 $$

$$ Z(Q) = \lambda Z(P_1) + (1 - \lambda) Z(P_2) = \lambda M + (1 - \lambda) M = M $$

Every point on that boundary edge is also an optimal solution.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Condition for the Minimum of an Unbounded Feasible Region

Aim: Prove that if the feasible region $\mathcal{R}$ is unbounded and $m = \min_{i} Z(P_i)$ at the corner points, then $m$ is the global minimum of $Z$ over $\mathcal{R}$ if and only if:

$$ \mathcal{H} = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : ax + by < m\} \cap \mathcal{R} = \emptyset $$

Proof by Contradiction:

  1. Sufficiency ($\Leftarrow$): Suppose $\mathcal{H} \cap \mathcal{R} = \emptyset$. Assume, for contradiction, that $m$ is not the minimum value over $\mathcal{R}$. Then there must exist some point $P^*(x^*, y^*) \in \mathcal{R}$ such that:

$$ Z(P^*) = ax^* + by^* < m $$

By definition of the open half-plane $\mathcal{H}$:

$$ ax^* + by^* < m \implies P^*(x^*, y^*) \in \mathcal{H} $$

Since $P^* \in \mathcal{R}$ as well, we have:

$$ P^* \in (\mathcal{H} \cap \mathcal{R}) $$

This implies $\mathcal{H} \cap \mathcal{R} \ne \emptyset$, which contradicts the hypothesis that $\mathcal{H} \cap \mathcal{R} = \emptyset$. Therefore, no such point $P^*$ can exist, and $Z(P) \ge m$ for all $P \in \mathcal{R}$.

  1. Necessity ($\Rightarrow$): Suppose $m$ is the absolute minimum value of $Z$ on $\mathcal{R}$. Then for every point $P(x, y) \in \mathcal{R}$, we must have:

$$ ax + by \ge m $$

Consequently, no point in $\mathcal{R}$ can satisfy $ax + by < m$. Hence, the intersection:

$$ \{(x, y) : ax + by < m\} \cap \mathcal{R} = \emptyset $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

  2. $x = 0 \implies y = \frac{38}{7} \approx 5.43 \implies (0, 5.43)$
  3. $y = 0 \implies x = \frac{38}{5} = 7.6 \implies (7.6, 0)$
  4. Notice that $E(2, 4)$ yields: $5(2) + 7(4) = 10 + 28 = 38$, so $L_3$ passes through $E(2, 4)$.
  5. The open half-plane $5x + 7y < 38$ includes the origin $(0, 0)$ since $0 < 38$.
  6. The boundary line $5x + 7y = 38$ is strictly excluded from the open half-plane.
  7. All points in the feasible region satisfy:

$$ 2x + y \ge 8 \quad \text{and} \quad x + 2y \ge 10 $$

Multiplying these inequalities by non-negative weights $k_1 = \frac{1}{3}$ and $k_2 = \frac{13}{3}$:

$$ \frac{1}{3}(2x + y) + \frac{13}{3}(x + 2y) \ge \frac{1}{3}(8) + \frac{13}{3}(10) $$

$$ \left(\frac{2}{3} + \frac{13}{3}\right)x + \left(\frac{1}{3} + \frac{26}{3}\right)y \ge \frac{8 + 130}{3} $$

$$ 5x + 9y \ge 46 > 38 $$

Since $y \ge 0$, $5x + 7y \ge 38$ for every feasible point.

Therefore, the open half-plane $5x + 7y < 38$ has no points in common with the feasible region.

Conclusion: * Minimum Cost: $₹\,380$ * Optimal Mix: $2\text{ kg}$ of Food $F_1$ and $4\text{ kg}$ of Food $F_2$.

Problem Type B: Cost Minimization & Manufacturing Allocation (Bounded Region)

Solved Board Question 2

<span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE 2023, 5 Marks]</span>

Statement: A company produces two types of goods, $A$ and $B$, that require processing on two machines, $M_1$ and $M_2$. Machine $M_1$ has at most $12\text{ hours}$ of available time per day, and Machine $M_2$ has at most $10\text{ hours}$. - Each unit of $A$ requires $2\text{ hours}$ on $M_1$ and $1\text{ hour}$ on $M_2$. - Each unit of $B$ requires $1\text{ hour}$ on $M_1$ and $2\text{ hours}$ on $M_2$. The cost of holding inventory and manufacturing is $₹\,40$ per unit of $A$ and $₹\,60$ per unit of $B$. The market demand requires producing at least $2$ units of $A$ and at least $1$ unit of $B$ daily. Formulate the LPP and determine the production plan that minimizes total operational cost.

Step 1: Formulation

  • Decision variables:
  • $x =$ number of units of Product $A$ produced.
  • $y =$ number of units of Product $B$ produced.

Objective Function:

$$ \text{Minimize } Z = 40x + 60y $$

Subject to the constraints:

$$ 2x + y \le 12 \tag{Machine $M_1$} $$

$$ x + 2y \le 10 \tag{Machine $M_2$} $$

$$ x \ge 2 \tag{Demand for $A$} $$

$$ y \ge 1 \tag{Demand for $B$} $$

Step 2: Line Equations and Boundary Intercepts

  1. $L_1$: $2x + y = 12 \implies (0, 12)$ and $(6, 0)$. Region: toward origin $(0, 0)$.
  2. $L_2$: $x + 2y = 10 \implies (0, 5)$ and $(10, 0)$. Region: toward origin $(0, 0)$.
  3. $L_3$: $x = 2$ (Vertical line). Region: right side ($x \ge 2$).
  4. $L_4$: $y = 1$ (Horizontal line). Region: upper side ($y \ge 1$).

Step 3: Determining All Corner Points

The feasible region is the closed polygon formed by the intersection of the four inequalities:

  1. Intersection of $x = 2$ and $y = 1$:

$$ P_1 = (2, 1) $$

  1. Intersection of $x = 2$ and $L_1$ ($2x + y = 12$):

$$ 2(2) + y = 12 \implies y = 8 $$

Check $L_2$: $x + 2y = 2 + 2(8) = 18 \not\le 10$ (Infeasible). Intersection of $x = 2$ with $L_2$ ($x + 2y = 10$):

$$ 2 + 2y = 10 \implies 2y = 8 \implies y = 4 $$

Check $L_1$: $2(2) + 4 = 8 \le 12$ (Feasible).

$$ P_2 = (2, 4) $$

  1. Intersection of $L_1$ and $L_2$:

$$ \begin{aligned} 2x + y &= 12 \implies y = 12 - 2x \\ x + 2(12 - 2x) &= 10 \implies x + 24 - 4x = 10 \implies 3x = 14 \implies x = \frac{14}{3} \\ y &= 12 - 2\left(\frac{14}{3}\right) = \frac{8}{3} \end{aligned} $$

Both $x = \frac{14}{3} \approx 4.67 \ge 2$ and $y = \frac{8}{3} \approx 2.67 \ge 1$.

$$ P_3 = \left(\frac{14}{3}, \frac{8}{3}\right) $$

  1. Intersection of $y = 1$ with $L_1$ ($2x + y = 12$):

$$ 2x + 1 = 12 \implies 2x = 11 \implies x = \frac{11}{2} = 5.5 $$

Check $L_2$: $\frac{11}{2} + 2(1) = 7.5 \le 10$ (Feasible).

$$ P_4 = \left(\frac{11}{2}, 1\right) $$

Step 4: Corner Point Evaluation Table

Corner Point $(x, y)$ $Z = 40x + 60y$ Evaluated Value ($₹$)
$P_1(2, 1)$ $40(2) + 60(1) = 80 + 60$ $\mathbf{140}$ $\leftarrow \text{Minimum}$
$P_2(2, 4)$ $40(2) + 60(4) = 80 + 240$ $320$
$P_3\left(\frac{14}{3}, \frac{8}{3}\right)$ $40\left(\frac{14}{3}\right) + 60\left(\frac{8}{3}\right) = \frac{560 + 480}{3} = \frac{1040}{3}$ $\approx 346.67$
$P_4\left(\frac{11}{2}, 1\right)$ $40\left(\frac{11}{2}\right) + 60(1) = 220 + 60$ $280$

Conclusion: * Minimum cost is $₹\,140$ at $x = 2, y = 1$.

Problem Type C: Transportation Model (Two-Variable Reduction)

Theoretical Framework & Network Topology

In a standard Class 12 transportation problem, supply originates from two depots ($A$ and $B$) and is transported to three consumption centers ($D, E, F$).

Depot A (Supply: S_A) --------(x)--------> Center D (Demand: d_1)
       |               --------(y)--------> Center E (Demand: d_2)
       |               ----(S_A - x - y)-> Center F (Demand: d_3)
       |
Depot B (Supply: S_B) ---(d_1 - x)-------> Center D
                       ---(d_2 - y)-------> Center E
                       ---(Remainder)-----> Center F

By balance of supply and demand:

$$ \text{Total Supply } = S_A + S_B = d_1 + d_2 + d_3 = \text{Total Demand} $$

The six transport routes can be parameterized using just two decision variables, $x$ and $y$.

Solved Board Question 3

<span class="step-mark-tag">[CBSE 2020, 6 Marks / Exemplar]</span>

Statement: Two godowns $A$ and $B$ have grain storage capacities of $100\text{ quintals}$ and $50\text{ quintals}$ respectively. They supply grain to three ration shops, $D, E$, and $F$, whose requirements are $60, 50$, and $40\text{ quintals}$ respectively. The transportation costs per quintal from the godowns to the shops are given in the matrix below:

From / To $D$ ($₹/\text{q}$) $E$ ($₹/\text{q}$) $F$ ($₹/\text{q}$)
Godown A $6$ $3$ $2.50$
Godown B $4$ $2$ $3$

Formulate this as a Linear Programming Problem and find how the supply should be distributed to minimize the total transportation cost. What is the minimum cost?

Step 1: Systematic Variable Assignment

  • Total supply $= 100 + 50 = 150\text{ quintals}$.
  • Total demand $= 60 + 50 + 40 = 150\text{ quintals}$. The system is balanced.

Let: * Godown $A$ transport $x\text{ quintals}$ to $D$. * Godown $A$ transport $y\text{ quintals}$ to $E$. * Remaining capacity of Godown $A$ to shop $F = 100 - (x + y)\text{ quintals}$.

Now satisfy remaining requirements from Godown $B$: * Shop $D$ requires $60\text{ q} \implies$ receives $(60 - x)\text{ quintals}$ from $B$. * Shop $E$ requires $50\text{ q} \implies$ receives $(50 - y)\text{ quintals}$ from $B$. * Shop $F$ requires $40\text{ q} \implies$ receives:

$$ 40 - [100 - (x + y)] = x + y - 60\text{ quintals from } B $$

Check Godown $B$'s total supply:

$$ (60 - x) + (50 - y) + (x + y - 60) = 50\text{ quintals (matches capacity)} \quad \checkmark $$

Step 2: Formulation of Constraints

All quantities transported must be non-negative: 1. $x \ge 0$ 2. $y \ge 0$ 3. $100 - (x + y) \ge 0 \implies x + y \le 100$ 4. $60 - x \ge 0 \implies x \le 60$ 5. $50 - y \ge 0 \implies y \le 50$ 6. $x + y - 60 \ge 0 \implies x + y \ge 60$

Combining these gives the constraint set:

$$ \begin{aligned} x + y &\le 100 \\ x + y &\ge 60 \\ x &\le 60 \\ y &\le 50 \\ x &\ge 0, \quad y \ge 0 \end{aligned} $$

Step 3: Derivation of the Objective Function

Calculate total transportation cost $Z$:

$$ \begin{aligned} Z &= 6x + 3y + 2.5(100 - x - y) + 4(60 - x) + 2(50 - y) + 3(x + y - 60) \\ &= 6x + 3y + 250 - 2.5x - 2.5y + 240 - 4x + 100 - 2y + 3x + 3y - 180 \\ &= (6 - 2.5 - 4 + 3)x + (3 - 2.5 - 2 + 3)y + (250 + 240 + 100 - 180) \\ &= 2.5x + 1.5y + 410 \end{aligned} $$

Objective:

$$ \text{Minimize } Z = 2.5x + 1.5y + 410 $$

Step 4: Corner Points of the Feasible Polygon

The feasible region is bounded by: * $L_1$: $x + y = 60$ * $L_2$: $x + y = 100$ * $L_3$: $x = 60$ * $L_4$: $y = 50$ * Axes: $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$

Find the vertices by solving boundary intersections:

  1. Intersection of $x = 60$ and $x + y = 60$:

$$ x = 60 \implies y = 0 \implies P_1(60, 0) $$

  1. Intersection of $x = 60$ and $x + y = 100$:

$$ x = 60 \implies y = 40 \implies P_2(60, 40) $$

Check $y \le 50$: $40 \le 50$ (Valid). 3. Intersection of $y = 50$ and $x + y = 100$:

$$ y = 50 \implies x = 50 \implies P_3(50, 50) $$

Check $x \le 60$: $50 \le 60$ (Valid). 4. Intersection of $y = 50$ and $x + y = 60$:

$$ y = 50 \implies x = 10 \implies P_4(10, 50) $$

Check $x \ge 0$: $10 \ge 0$ (Valid). 5. Intersection on the boundary $x + y = 60$ when $y = 0$: Point is $P_1(60, 0)$. 6. Intersection of $x + y = 60$ with $x = 0$: $y = 60$, but this violates $y \le 50$.

The vertices of the bounded polygon are:

$$ P_1(60, 0), \quad P_2(60, 40), \quad P_3(50, 50), \quad P_4(10, 50) $$

Step 5: Corner Point Evaluation Table

Corner Point $(x, y)$ $Z = 2.5x + 1.5y + 410$ Evaluated Cost ($₹$)
$P_1(60, 0)$ $2.5(60) + 1.5(0) + 410 = 150 + 0 + 410$ $560$
$P_2(60, 40)$ $2.5(60) + 1.5(40) + 410 = 150 + 60 + 410$ $620$
$P_3(50, 50)$ $2.5(50) + 1.5(50) + 410 = 125 + 75 + 410$ $610$
$P_4(10, 50)$ $2.5(10) + 1.5(50) + 410 = 25 + 75 + 410$ $\mathbf{510}$ $\leftarrow \text{Minimum}$

The minimum transportation cost is $₹\,510$, attained at $x = 10, y = 50$.

   │    Always verify coordinates in BOTH line equations.   │

Pitfall 1: Omitting the Open Half-Plane Test in Unbounded Problems

  • The Error: Finding the smallest corner value $m$ in an unbounded region and declaring it the minimum without further verification.
  • Marking Penalty: Up to $2$ marks deducted out of $5$.
  • Correct Procedure:
  • Write down $ax + by < m$.
  • Draw its boundary as a dashed (dotted) line (since the inequality is strict).
  • Test $(0, 0)$ to determine the correct half-plane.
  • State explicitly: "The open half-plane $ax + by < m$ has no point in common with the feasible region. Hence, the minimum value is $m$."

Pitfall 2: Translating Keywords to Inequality Signs

  • "At least", "Not less than", "Minimum of" $\implies \mathbf{\ge}$ (Greater than or equal to).
  • "At most", "Not more than", "Exceeding at most" $\implies \mathbf{\le}$ (Less than or equal to).
  • A single inverted sign leads to an entirely wrong region and zero credit for graphical steps.

Pitfall 3: Missing Non-Negative Constraints

  • Failing to write $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ in the formulation loses $1$ mark.
  • Graphically, this means forgetting that the feasible region must lie entirely in the first quadrant.

Pitfall 4: Miscalculating Corner Coordinates

  • Estimating vertex coordinates by eye from the graph instead of solving the system algebraically:

$$ \begin{cases} a_1 x + b_1 y = c_1 \\ a_2 x + b_2 y = c_2 \end{cases} $$

Always use substitution or elimination to compute exact coordinates, especially when they are fractions.

5. Speed Hacks, Mental Math & Verification Shortcuts

5.1 Two-Point Intercept Form for Fast Plotting

Do not build large tables of values. Write lines in intercept form:

$$ \frac{x}{\left(\frac{c}{a}\right)} + \frac{y}{\left(\frac{c}{b}\right)} = 1 $$

  • $x$-intercept is $(c/a, 0)$
  • $y$-intercept is $(0, c/b)$ Plot these two points immediately on the axes and join them.

5.2 The Iso-Cost / Iso-Profit Vector Method (Gradient Trick)

The objective function $Z = ax + by$ has a normal gradient vector:

$$ \nabla Z = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} $$

  • To minimize $Z$, mentally shift the line $ax + by = k$ inward (in the direction opposite $\nabla Z$). The last corner point the line touches before leaving the feasible region is the minimum point.
  • To maximize $Z$, shift the line outward (in the direction of $\nabla Z$). The last corner point it touches before leaving the region is the maximum point.
  • This takes 5 seconds and confirms which vertex must be the optimal solution before you do any arithmetic.

5.3 Determinant Formula for Vertex Coordinates

To quickly find the intersection of $a_1 x + b_1 y = c_1$ and $a_2 x + b_2 y = c_2$:

$$ D = a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1, \quad D_x = c_1 b_2 - c_2 b_1, \quad D_y = a_1 c_2 - a_2 c_1 $$

$$ x = \frac{D_x}{D}, \qquad y = \frac{D_y}{D} $$

This Cramer's rule calculation avoids messy algebraic elimination steps on your scratch paper.

5.4 Multiple Optimal Solutions Test

If the slope of the objective function line matches the slope of any boundary line of the feasible region:

$$ m_Z = -\frac{a}{b} = -\frac{a_i}{b_i} = m_{\text{constraint}} $$

and that boundary segment forms an outer edge of the feasible region, then: * $Z$ takes the same optimal value at both endpoints of that edge. * Infinitely many optimal solutions exist along the entire line segment connecting those vertices.
