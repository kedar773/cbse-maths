1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Scalars vs. Vectors (Foundation) ⭐⭐⭐

  • Scalar Quantity: A quantity that possesses only magnitude and no directional dependence in space (e.g., mass, time, volume, distance, speed, work done). Scalars obey standard algebraic addition.
  • Vector Quantity: A quantity that possesses both magnitude and a specified direction in space, and strictly obeys the Triangle Law / Parallelogram Law of Vector Addition (e.g., displacement, velocity, acceleration, momentum, force, electric field).

Pedagogical Note: Having both magnitude and direction is a necessary but not sufficient condition to be a vector. For example, electric current has magnitude and direction, but it adds according to scalar Kirchhoff's Current Law, not the vector triangle law. Hence, current is a scalar (or tensor of rank 0), not a vector.

  • Geometric Representation: A directed line segment $\vec{AB}$ where:
  • $A$ is the initial point (tail).
  • $B$ is the terminal point (tip/head).
  • Magnitude is denoted by $|\vec{AB}|$ or simply $AB$, representing the Euclidean length of the segment.
  • Direction is the sense from initial point $A$ to terminal point $B$.

1.2 Position Vector of a Point in 3D Space ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Consider a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system $OXYZ$ with origin $O(0, 0, 0)$. Let $P(x, y, z)$ be any arbitrary point in space.

  • The vector having its initial point at the origin $O$ and its terminal point at $P$ is defined as the Position Vector of point $P$ with respect to origin $O$, denoted by:

$$ \vec{r} = \vec{OP} $$

  • In terms of standard orthonormal unit basis vectors $\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$ along the positive directions of the $x$, $y$, and $z$ axes respectively:

$$ \vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k} $$

  • The magnitude (Euclidean distance from origin $O$ to $P$) is given by:

$$ |\vec{r}| = |\vec{OP}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

1.3 Direction Angles, Direction Cosines (DC's) & Direction Ratios (DR's) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definitions & Geometric Conception

Let a directed line (or vector $\vec{r} = \vec{OP}$) pass through the origin $O$ and make angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ with the positive directions of the coordinate axes $OX, OY, OZ$ respectively, where $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in [0, \pi]$. 1. Direction Angles: The angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$. 2. Direction Cosines (DCs): The cosines of these direction angles, conventionally denoted by $l, m, n$:

$$ l = \cos\alpha, \quad m = \cos\beta, \quad n = \cos\gamma $$

  1. Direction Ratios (DRs): Any three real numbers $a, b, c$ proportional to the direction cosines $l, m, n$:

$$ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = k \quad (k \neq 0) $$

1.4 Comprehensive Classification of Vectors ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Type of Vector Formal Definition Mathematical Condition / Representation Geometric / Physical Insight
Zero / Null Vector Initial and terminal points coincide $\vec{0}$, $|\vec{0}| = 0$ Has zero magnitude; direction is indeterminate (can be assigned any arbitrary direction).
Unit Vector A vector whose magnitude is exactly unity ($1$ unit) $|\hat{a}| = 1$; $\hat{a} = \frac{\vec{a}}{|\vec{a}|}$ Specifies pure spatial direction without altering scale.
Co-initial Vectors Two or more vectors having the identical initial point $\vec{OA}, \vec{OB}, \vec{OC}$ Emanate from the same source (e.g., concurrent forces).
Collinear / Parallel Vectors Two or more vectors parallel to the same straight line, irrespective of magnitudes and senses $\vec{b} = \lambda \vec{a}$ for some scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$ Slopes/lines of action are parallel.
Equal Vectors Vectors having identical magnitude AND identical direction, regardless of initial points $\vec{a} = \vec{b} \iff |\vec{a}| = |\vec{b}|$ and $\hat{a} = \hat{b}$ Free vectors that can be translated parallel to themselves.
Negative of a Vector A vector having the same magnitude as $\vec{a}$ but opposite direction $\vec{BA} = -\vec{AB}$ $|\vec{BA}| = |\vec{AB}|$ and $\vec{BA} + \vec{AB} = \vec{0}$.
Like & Unlike Vectors Collinear vectors having the same sense of direction (Like, $\lambda > 0$) or opposite sense (Unlike, $\lambda < 0$) $\vec{b} = \lambda \vec{a}$ Like: $\theta = 0^\circ$; Unlike: $\theta = 180^\circ$.
Coplanar Vectors Three or more vectors lying in the same plane or parallel to the same plane Scaled linear combination: $\vec{c} = x\vec{a} + y\vec{b}$ Box product / determinant condition $= 0$.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: Fundamental Relation of Direction Cosines ($l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$)

Board Weightage: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Given: A point $P(x, y, z)$ in space with position vector $\vec{r} = \vec{OP} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$ such that $|\vec{r}| = r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$. The vector $\vec{OP}$ makes angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ with the positive $X, Y, Z$ axes respectively.
  • To Prove:

$$ \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 \quad \text{i.e.,} \quad l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

  • Construction: From point $P(x, y, z)$, drop a perpendicular $PA$ onto the $x$-axis. Then $A$ is the point $(x, 0, 0)$ on the $x$-axis.
          Z
          |     . P(x, y, z)
          |    /|
          |   / |
          |  /  |
          | / r |
          |/ α  |
          O-----A-------- X
         /     (x,0,0)
        /
       Y
  • Step-by-Step Proof: In the right-angled triangle $\Delta OAP$, the angle at vertex $A$ is $\angle OAP = 90^\circ$. The adjacent side along the $x$-axis has length $OA = x$. The hypotenuse is $OP = |\vec{r}| = r$.

Therefore, by basic trigonometry:

$$ \cos\alpha = \frac{OA}{OP} = \frac{x}{r} \implies x = lr $$

Similarly, dropping perpendiculars from $P$ onto the $y$-axis and $z$-axis yields:

$$ \cos\beta = \frac{y}{r} \implies y = mr $$

$$ \cos\gamma = \frac{z}{r} \implies z = nr $$

Now, compute the Euclidean magnitude of vector $\vec{OP}$:

$$ r^2 = |\vec{OP}|^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 $$

Substituting $x = lr$, $y = mr$, and $z = nr$ into the distance equation:

$$ r^2 = (lr)^2 + (mr)^2 + (nr)^2 $$

$$ r^2 = r^2(l^2 + m^2 + n^2) $$

Since $P$ is not the origin, $r \neq 0$, dividing both sides by $r^2$:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

$$ \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: Relation Between Direction Ratios and Direction Cosines

Board Weightage: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Given: Let $a, b, c$ be the direction ratios of a vector $\vec{r}$, and $l, m, n$ be its direction cosines.
  • To Prove:

$$ l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad m = \pm \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad n = \pm \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

  • Step-by-Step Proof: By the definition of direction ratios, there exists a non-zero proportionality constant $k \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = k $$

$$ \implies l = ak, \quad m = bk, \quad n = ck $$

From Theorem 1, we know that:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

Substitute $l, m, n$ into the relation:

$$ (ak)^2 + (bk)^2 + (ck)^2 = 1 $$

$$ k^2(a^2 + b^2 + c^2) = 1 $$

$$ k^2 = \frac{1}{a^2 + b^2 + c^2} $$

$$ k = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

Substituting this value of $k$ back into $l = ak, m = bk, n = ck$:

$$ l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad m = \pm \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad n = \pm \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: Vector Joining Two Points

Board Weightage: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • Given: Two points $P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $P_2(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ with position vectors $\vec{OP_1} = x_1\hat{i} + y_1\hat{j} + z_1\hat{k}$ and $\vec{OP_2} = x_2\hat{i} + y_2\hat{j} + z_2\hat{k}$.
  • To Prove:

$$ \vec{P_1P_2} = (x_2 - x_1)\hat{i} + (y_2 - y_1)\hat{j} + (z_2 - z_1)\hat{k} $$

and

$$ |\vec{P_1P_2}| = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

  • Step-by-Step Proof: In triangle $\Delta OP_1P_2$, by the Triangle Law of Vector Addition:

$$ \vec{OP_1} + \vec{P_1P_2} = \vec{OP_2} $$

Rearranging the vector equation:

$$ \vec{P_1P_2} = \vec{OP_2} - \vec{OP_1} $$

Substitute the component expressions of $\vec{OP_1}$ and $\vec{OP_2}$:

$$ \vec{P_1P_2} = (x_2\hat{i} + y_2\hat{j} + z_2\hat{k}) - (x_1\hat{i} + y_1\hat{j} + z_1\hat{k}) $$

$$ \vec{P_1P_2} = (x_2 - x_1)\hat{i} + (y_2 - y_1)\hat{j} + (z_2 - z_1)\hat{k} $$

The magnitude follows from the Euclidean 3D norm:

$$ |\vec{P_1P_2}| = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

1. IN-DEPTH PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & MATHEMATICAL INTUITION

1.1 Vector Addition: Geometric Foundations (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Pedagogical Definition

Let $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ be any two given vectors. If the terminal point of $\vec{a}$ coincides with the initial point of $\vec{b}$, then the vector represented by the directed line segment joining the initial point of $\vec{a}$ to the terminal point of $\vec{b}$ is defined as the sum (or resultant) of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, denoted by $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$.

Triangle Law of Vector Addition

Formal Statement: If two vectors are represented both in magnitude and direction by the two sides of a triangle taken in the same order, then their resultant is represented both in magnitude and direction by the third side of the triangle taken in the reverse order.

Let $\vec{a} = \vec{AB}$ and $\vec{b} = \vec{BC}$. The closing side $\vec{AC}$ taken in reverse order represents $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$:

$$ \vec{AB} + \vec{BC} = \vec{AC} $$

Using directed line segments, since $\vec{CA} = -\vec{AC}$, we obtain the fundamental cyclic relation:

$$ \vec{AB} + \vec{BC} + \vec{CA} = \vec{AA} = \vec{0} $$

Parallelogram Law of Vector Addition

Formal Statement: If two vectors are represented both in magnitude and direction by two adjacent sides of a parallelogram drawn from a common point, then their resultant is represented both in magnitude and direction by the diagonal of the parallelogram passing through that common point.

Let $\vec{OA} = \vec{a}$ and $\vec{OB} = \vec{b}$ be two co-initial vectors. Complete parallelogram $OACB$ such that $\vec{BC} = \vec{OA} = \vec{a}$ and $\vec{AC} = \vec{OB} = \vec{b}$. Then by the triangle law in $\triangle OAC$:

$$ \vec{OC} = \vec{OA} + \vec{AC} = \vec{a} + \vec{b} $$

Difference (Subtraction) of Two Vectors

Vector subtraction is defined as the addition of the negative of a vector:

$$ \vec{a} - \vec{b} = \vec{a} + (-\vec{b}) $$

Geometrically, if $\vec{OA} = \vec{a}$ and $\vec{OB} = \vec{b}$, construct $\vec{OB'} = -\vec{b}$ along the line of $OB$ in the opposite direction. Then the diagonal $\vec{OC'}$ of parallelogram $OAC'B'$ gives $\vec{a} - \vec{b}$. Notice that in parallelogram $OACB$, the second diagonal $\vec{BA} = \vec{OA} - \vec{OB} = \vec{a} - \vec{b}$.

1.2 Algebraic Properties of Vector Addition (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

  1. Closure Property: For any two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, their sum $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$ is uniquely defined and is also a vector.
  2. Commutative Law:

$$ \vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} + \vec{a} $$

  1. Associative Law:

$$ (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) + \vec{c} = \vec{a} + (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) $$

  1. Existence of Additive Identity: The zero vector $\vec{0}$ satisfies:

$$ \vec{a} + \vec{0} = \vec{0} + \vec{a} = \vec{a} \quad \forall \vec{a} $$

  1. Existence of Additive Inverse: For every vector $\vec{a}$, there exists a negative vector $-\vec{a}$ such that:

$$ \vec{a} + (-\vec{a}) = (-\vec{a}) + \vec{a} = \vec{0} $$

1.3 Multiplication of a Vector by a Scalar (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Definition

Let $\vec{a}$ be a given vector and $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ be a scalar. The product of $\vec{a}$ by $\lambda$, denoted as $\lambda\vec{a}$, is a vector whose: 1. Magnitude: $|\lambda\vec{a}| = |\lambda| |\vec{a}|$ 2. Direction: - Same as $\vec{a}$ if $\lambda > 0$ - Opposite to $\vec{a}$ if $\lambda < 0$ - Null vector $\vec{0}$ if $\lambda = 0$ or $\vec{a} = \vec{0}$

Collinearity (Parallelism) Criterion

Two non-zero vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are collinear if and only if there exists a non-zero scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$ \vec{b} = \lambda \vec{a} $$

Unit Vector in the Direction of $\vec{a}$

For any non-zero vector $\vec{a}$, setting $\lambda = \frac{1}{|\vec{a}|}$ yields a vector having length $1$:

$$ \hat{a} = \frac{\vec{a}}{|\vec{a}|} \implies |\hat{a}| = \left|\frac{1}{|\vec{a}|}\right| |\vec{a}| = 1 $$

1.4 Rectangular Component Form in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $O(0,0,0)$ be the origin in a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system. Let $\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$ denote unit vectors along the positive $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ axes respectively:

$$ |\hat{i}| = |\hat{j}| = |\hat{k}| = 1 $$

Position Vector of a Point

Let $P(x, y, z)$ be any point in space. The position vector $\vec{r} = \vec{OP}$ is expressed uniquely as:

$$ \vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k} $$

  • $x, y, z$ are called the scalar components (or rectangular Cartesian components) of $\vec{r}$.
  • $x\hat{i}, y\hat{j}, z\hat{k}$ are called the vector components of $\vec{r}$ along the axes.

Magnitude of a Vector in Component Form

By successive applications of Pythagoras' theorem in three dimensions:

$$ |\vec{r}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

Component Operations

Let $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$, with $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{R}$: 1. Sum:

$$ \vec{a} + \vec{b} = (a_1 + b_1)\hat{i} + (a_2 + b_2)\hat{j} + (a_3 + b_3)\hat{k} $$

  1. Difference:

$$ \vec{a} - \vec{b} = (a_1 - b_1)\hat{i} + (a_2 - b_2)\hat{j} + (a_3 - b_3)\hat{k} $$

  1. Scalar Multiplication:

$$ \lambda\vec{a} = (\lambda a_1)\hat{i} + (\lambda a_2)\hat{j} + (\lambda a_3)\hat{k} $$

  1. Vector Joining Two Points $P_1(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $P_2(x_2, y_2, z_2)$:

$$ \vec{P_1P_2} = \vec{OP_2} - \vec{OP_1} = (x_2 - x_1)\hat{i} + (y_2 - y_1)\hat{j} + (z_2 - z_1)\hat{k} $$

$$ |\vec{P_1P_2}| = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

  1. Collinearity Condition in Components:

$$ \frac{b_1}{a_1} = \frac{b_2}{a_2} = \frac{b_3}{a_3} = \lambda $$

Direction Cosines and Direction Ratios (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $\vec{r} = a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}$ make angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ with the positive $X, Y, Z$ axes respectively ($0 \le \alpha, \beta, \gamma \le \pi$). - $\cos\alpha = l$, $\cos\beta = m$, $\cos\gamma = n$ are the direction cosines (d.c.s). - $a, b, c$ are proportional to $l, m, n$ and are called direction ratios (d.r.s).

$$ l = \frac{a}{|\vec{r}|} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad m = \frac{b}{|\vec{r}|} = \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad n = \frac{c}{|\vec{r}|} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 $$

$$ \hat{r} = \frac{\vec{r}}{|\vec{r}|} = l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k} $$

2. COMPLETE STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL PROOFS & DERIVATIONS

Proof 2.1: Commutative Law of Vector Addition (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ \vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} + \vec{a} $$

Given: Two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$.
To Prove: $\vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} + \vec{a}$.
Construction: Let $\vec{OA} = \vec{a}$. From terminal point $A$, draw $\vec{AB} = \vec{b}$. Complete parallelogram $OABC$ by drawing $OC$ parallel and equal to $AB$, and $CB$ parallel and equal to $OA$.

       B --------- C
      /           /
     /           /
    /           /
   O --------- A

Step-by-Step Proof: 1. In $\triangle OAB$, by the Triangle Law of Addition:

$$ \vec{OB} = \vec{OA} + \vec{AB} = \vec{a} + \vec{b} $$

  1. Since opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal and parallel:

$$ \vec{OC} = \vec{AB} = \vec{b} \quad \text{and} \quad \vec{CB} = \vec{OA} = \vec{a} $$

  1. In $\triangle OCB$, by the Triangle Law of Addition:

$$ \vec{OB} = \vec{OC} + \vec{CB} = \vec{b} + \vec{a} $$

  1. Equating the two expressions for the diagonal vector $\vec{OB}$:

$$ \vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} + \vec{a} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Associative Law of Vector Addition (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) + \vec{c} = \vec{a} + (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) $$

Given: Three vectors $\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$.
To Prove: $(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) + \vec{c} = \vec{a} + (\vec{b} + \vec{c})$.
Construction: Choose origin $O$. Set $\vec{OP} = \vec{a}$, $\vec{PQ} = \vec{b}$, and $\vec{QR} = \vec{c}$. Join $O$ to $Q$, $P$ to $R$, and $O$ to $R$.

Step-by-Step Proof: 1. In $\triangle OPQ$, by the Triangle Law:

$$ \vec{OQ} = \vec{OP} + \vec{PQ} = \vec{a} + \vec{b} $$

  1. In $\triangle OQR$, by the Triangle Law:

$$ \vec{OR} = \vec{OQ} + \vec{QR} = (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) + \vec{c} $$

  1. In $\triangle PQR$, by the Triangle Law:

$$ \vec{PR} = \vec{PQ} + \vec{QR} = \vec{b} + \vec{c} $$

  1. In $\triangle OPR$, by the Triangle Law:

$$ \vec{OR} = \vec{OP} + \vec{PR} = \vec{a} + (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) $$

  1. Equating both expressions for the closing vector $\vec{OR}$:

$$ (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) + \vec{c} = \vec{a} + (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: Distributive Law of Scalar Multiplication Over Vector Addition (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ \lambda(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = \lambda\vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b} \quad (\lambda > 0) $$

Given: Two vectors $\vec{a}, \vec{b}$ and a positive scalar $\lambda > 0$.
To Prove: $\lambda(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = \lambda\vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b}$.
Construction: Let $\vec{OA} = \vec{a}$ and $\vec{AB} = \vec{b}$, so $\vec{OB} = \vec{a} + \vec{b}$. Along rays $OA$ and $OB$, choose points $A'$ and $B'$ such that:

$$ OA' = \lambda \cdot OA \implies \vec{OA'} = \lambda\vec{a} $$

$$ OB' = \lambda \cdot OB \implies \vec{OB'} = \lambda(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) $$

Step-by-Step Proof: 1. In $\triangle OAB$ and $\triangle OA'B'$:

$$ \frac{OA'}{OA} = \lambda = \frac{OB'}{OB} $$

The angle $\angle AOB$ is common to both triangles. 2. By SAS similarity criterion:

$$ \triangle OA'B' \sim \triangle OAB $$

  1. Therefore, the corresponding sides are proportional:

$$ \frac{A'B'}{AB} = \frac{OA'}{OA} = \lambda \implies A'B' = \lambda \cdot AB $$

  1. Furthermore, $\angle OA'B' = \angle OAB$, which proves that the line segment $A'B'$ is parallel to $AB$.
  2. Because $A'B'$ has $\lambda$ times the magnitude of $AB$ and identical orientation:

$$ \vec{A'B'} = \lambda\vec{AB} = \lambda\vec{b} $$

  1. Applying the Triangle Law in $\triangle OA'B'$:

$$ \vec{OB'} = \vec{OA'} + \vec{A'B'} $$

  1. Substituting the vector values:

$$ \lambda(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = \lambda\vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b} $$

(Note: If $\lambda < 0$, let $\lambda = -k$ where $k > 0$; reversing directions yields the identical equality. If $\lambda = 0$, both sides reduce to $\vec{0}$.)

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.4: Triangle Inequality (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}| $$

Given: Two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$.
To Prove: $|\vec{a} + \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}|$.

Step-by-Step Proof: 1. Case I: Either $\vec{a} = \vec{0}$ or $\vec{b} = \vec{0}$.
If $\vec{a} = \vec{0}$, then $|\vec{0} + \vec{b}| = |\vec{b}| = 0 + |\vec{b}| = |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}|$. The equality holds trivially.

  1. Case II: Both $\vec{a} \neq \vec{0}$ and $\vec{b} \neq \vec{0}$ are non-collinear.
    Let $\vec{OA} = \vec{a}$ and $\vec{AB} = \vec{b}$. Then $\vec{OB} = \vec{a} + \vec{b}$.
    Since $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are non-collinear, points $O, A, B$ form a non-degenerate triangle $\triangle OAB$.
    In any triangle, the length of any side is strictly less than the sum of the lengths of the other two sides:

$$ OB < OA + AB $$

Substituting side lengths with vector magnitudes:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}| < |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}| $$

  1. Case III: Vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are collinear.
  2. If $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ have the same direction, then $\vec{b} = k\vec{a}$ where $k > 0$:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}| = |\vec{a} + k\vec{a}| = |(1+k)\vec{a}| = (1+k)|\vec{a}| = |\vec{a}| + k|\vec{a}| = |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}| $$

  • If $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ have opposite directions, then $\vec{b} = -k\vec{a}$ where $k > 0$:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}| = |(1-k)\vec{a}| = |1-k||\vec{a}| < (1+k)|\vec{a}| = |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}| $$

  1. Combining all exhaustive cases:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.5: Internal and External Section Formula for Position Vectors (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Theorem: Let $A$ and $B$ be two points with position vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ relative to origin $O$. If point $R$ divides the line segment $AB$ in the ratio $m : n$, then the position vector $\vec{r} = \vec{OR}$ is given by:

$$ \text{Internal Division: } \vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m + n} $$

$$ \text{External Division: } \vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} - n\vec{a}}{m - n} \quad (m \neq n) $$

Proof of Internal Division:

   O
  /|\
 / | \
A--R--B

Given: $R$ lies on $AB$ such that $\frac{AR}{RB} = \frac{m}{n}$, where $m, n > 0$.
To Prove: $\vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m + n}$.

Step-by-Step Proof: 1. Since $R$ divides $AB$ internally, the directed segments $\vec{AR}$ and $\vec{RB}$ have the same direction:

$$ \frac{|\vec{AR}|}{|\vec{RB}|} = \frac{m}{n} \implies n|\vec{AR}| = m|\vec{RB}| $$

  1. Because they point along the same straight line in the identical sense:

$$ n\vec{AR} = m\vec{RB} $$

  1. Express vectors in terms of position vectors of endpoints:

$$ \vec{AR} = \vec{OR} - \vec{OA} = \vec{r} - \vec{a} $$

$$ \vec{RB} = \vec{OB} - \vec{OR} = \vec{b} - \vec{r} $$

  1. Substitute these into the vector equality:

$$ n(\vec{r} - \vec{a}) = m(\vec{b} - \vec{r}) $$

  1. Expand using distributive properties:

$$ n\vec{r} - n\vec{a} = m\vec{b} - m\vec{r} $$

  1. Collect terms involving $\vec{r}$ on the left-hand side:

$$ m\vec{r} + n\vec{r} = m\vec{b} + n\vec{a} $$

$$ (m + n)\vec{r} = m\vec{b} + n\vec{a} $$

  1. Since $m + n \neq 0$, scalar multiplication by $\frac{1}{m+n}$ gives:

$$ \vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m + n} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Corollary: Midpoint Formula

If $R$ is the midpoint of $AB$, $m : n = 1 : 1$:

$$ \vec{r} = \frac{\vec{a} + \vec{b}}{2} $$

Section Formula, Direction Ratios & Direction Cosines

Class 12 Mathematics: Vectors

Master Teaching Notes: Section Formula, Direction Angles, Direction Cosines & Direction Ratios

Module 1: Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1. Position Vector (Foundational Building Block)

  • Definition: Let $O(0,0,0)$ be the fixed reference origin in 3D Euclidean space. For any point $P(x, y, z)$, the vector $\vec{r} = \vec{OP}$ is defined as the position vector of point $P$ with respect to $O$:

$$ \vec{r} = \vec{OP} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k} $$

  • Magnitude:

$$ |\vec{r}| = |\vec{OP}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} $$

  • Vector joining two points: If $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ have position vectors $\vec{a} = \vec{OA}$ and $\vec{b} = \vec{OB}$, by triangle law of vector addition in $\Delta OAB$:

$$ \vec{OA} + \vec{AB} = \vec{OB} \implies \vec{AB} = \vec{OB} - \vec{OA} = \vec{b} - \vec{a} $$

$$ \vec{AB} = (x_2 - x_1)\hat{i} + (y_2 - y_1)\hat{j} + (z_2 - z_1)\hat{k} $$

2. The Section Formula ⭐⭐⭐⭐

The section formula determines the position vector of a point $R(\vec{r})$ lying on the line passing through points $A(\vec{a})$ and $B(\vec{b})$ such that it divides the directed line segment $AB$ in a specified scalar ratio $m : n$.

Types of Division:

  1. Internal Division: Point $R$ lies strictly between $A$ and $B$. The directed displacement vectors $\vec{AR}$ and $\vec{RB}$ point in the same direction:

$$ \frac{|\vec{AR}|}{|\vec{RB}|} = \frac{m}{n} \implies n\,\vec{AR} = m\,\vec{RB} $$

  1. External Division: Point $R$ lies outside the segment $AB$ on the line extending past $A$ or $B$. The directed vectors $\vec{AR}$ and $\vec{BR}$ have opposite senses or $\frac{\vec{AR}}{\vec{RB}} = -\frac{m}{n}$ when viewed with directed collinear scaling.
  2. Midpoint Formula (Special Case of Internal Division $1:1$):

$$ \vec{r} = \frac{\vec{a} + \vec{b}}{2} $$

  1. Centroid of a Triangle: If $A(\vec{a}), B(\vec{b}), C(\vec{c})$ form a triangle, the centroid $G(\vec{g})$ divides median $AD$ in ratio $2:1$, yielding:

$$ \vec{g} = \frac{\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}}{3} $$

3. Direction Angles, Direction Cosines (DCos), and Direction Ratios (DRs) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

3.1 Direction Angles ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma$)

Let a directed non-zero vector $\vec{r} = \vec{OP} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$ make positive angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ with the positive directions of coordinate axes $OX, OY, OZ$ respectively. * Domain: $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in [0, \pi]$

3.2 Direction Cosines ($l, m, n$)

The cosines of these direction angles are termed the Direction Cosines (DCos) of the vector $\vec{r}$:

$$ l = \cos\alpha, \quad m = \cos\beta, \quad n = \cos\gamma $$

Geometric Projection: * Drop a perpendicular from $P(x, y, z)$ onto the positive $x$-axis meeting at $A(x, 0, 0)$. In right-angled $\Delta OAP$:

$$ \cos\alpha = \frac{OA}{OP} = \frac{x}{|\vec{r}|} \implies x = l|\vec{r}| $$

$$ \cos\beta = \frac{OB}{OP} = \frac{y}{|\vec{r}|} \implies y = m|\vec{r}| $$

$$ \cos\gamma = \frac{OC}{OP} = \frac{z}{|\vec{r}|} \implies z = n|\vec{r}| $$

3.3 Direction Ratios ($a, b, c$)

  • Any three real numbers $a, b, c$ that are proportional to the direction cosines $l, m, n$ are called Direction Ratios (DRs) of the vector:

$$ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = \lambda \quad (\lambda \neq 0) $$

  • Key Distinction:
  • Direction cosines are unique for a directed line segment and satisfy $l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$.
  • Direction ratios are never unique; any non-zero scalar multiple $ka, kb, kc$ represents the same set of direction ratios.
  • For a vector $\vec{r} = a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}$, the scalar scalar components $a, b, c$ are directly its direction ratios.
  • The components of its unit vector $\hat{r} = \frac{\vec{r}}{|\vec{r}|} = l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k}$ are its direction cosines.

Module 2: Complete Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 1: Section Formula for Internal Division ⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$ \begin{array}{l} Given: \text{Two points } A \text{ and } B \text{ with position vectors } \vec{OA} = \vec{a} \text{ and } \vec{OB} = \vec{b} \text{ relative to origin } O.\\ \text{Point } R(\vec{r}) \text{ lies on segment } AB \text{ such that it divides } AB \text{ internally in the ratio } m : n.\\ To Prove: \vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m + n}\\ Derivation: \end{array} $$

$$ \text{Since } R \text{ divides } AB \text{ internally in the ratio } m : n \text{, the directed segments satisfy:} $$

$$ \frac{|\vec{AR}|}{|\vec{RB}|} = \frac{m}{n} $$

$$ \text{Since vectors } \vec{AR} \text{ and } \vec{RB} \text{ are collinear and have the identical direction:} $$

$$ n\,\vec{AR} = m\,\vec{RB} $$

$$ \text{Expressing } \vec{AR} \text{ and } \vec{RB} \text{ in terms of position vectors:} $$

$$ \vec{AR} = \vec{OR} - \vec{OA} = \vec{r} - \vec{a} $$

$$ \vec{RB} = \vec{OB} - \vec{OR} = \vec{b} - \vec{r} $$

$$ \text{Substitute into the vector relation:} $$

$$ n(\vec{r} - \vec{a}) = m(\vec{b} - \vec{r}) $$

$$ n\vec{r} - n\vec{a} = m\vec{b} - m\vec{r} $$

$$ \text{Collecting terms containing } \vec{r} \text{ onto the left-hand side:} $$

$$ m\vec{r} + n\vec{r} = m\vec{b} + n\vec{a} $$

$$ (m + n)\vec{r} = m\vec{b} + n\vec{a} $$

$$ \text{Since } m + n \neq 0 \text{ (both } m, n > 0 \text{):} $$

$$ \vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m + n} \quad \blacksquare $$

Proof 2: Section Formula for External Division ⭐⭐⭐

$$ \begin{array}{l} Given: \text{Points } A(\vec{a}) \text{ and } B(\vec{b})\text{. Point } R(\vec{r}) \text{ divides the line segment } AB \text{ externally in the ratio } m : n \ (m \neq n).\\ To Prove: \vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} - n\vec{a}}{m - n}\\ Derivation: \end{array} $$

$$ \text{By definition of external division, } \frac{|\vec{AR}|}{|\vec{BR}|} = \frac{m}{n}\text{. In vector form, noting collinearity along the ray:} $$

$$ n\,\vec{AR} = m\,\vec{BR} $$

$$ \text{Expressing vectors via position vectors:} $$

$$ \vec{AR} = \vec{r} - \vec{a}, \quad \vec{BR} = \vec{r} - \vec{b} $$

$$ n(\vec{r} - \vec{a}) = m(\vec{r} - \vec{b}) $$

$$ n\vec{r} - n\vec{a} = m\vec{r} - m\vec{b} $$

$$ \text{Rearranging terms:} $$

$$ m\vec{b} - n\vec{a} = m\vec{r} - n\vec{r} $$

$$ (m - n)\vec{r} = m\vec{b} - n\vec{a} $$

$$ \text{Since } m \neq n \implies m - n \neq 0\text{:} $$

$$ \vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} - n\vec{a}}{m - n} \quad \blacksquare $$

Proof 3: Fundamental Identity of Direction Cosines ($l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$ \begin{array}{l} Given: \text{A vector } \vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k} \text{ with direction angles } \alpha, \beta, \gamma \text{ and DCos } l = \cos\alpha, m = \cos\beta, n = \cos\gamma.\\ To Prove: l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1\\ Derivation: \end{array} $$

$$ \text{By definition of magnitude:} $$

$$ |\vec{r}|^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 $$

$$ \text{From orthogonal projections on coordinate axes:} $$

$$ x = |\vec{r}|\cos\alpha = l|\vec{r}| $$

$$ y = |\vec{r}|\cos\beta = m|\vec{r}| $$

$$ z = |\vec{r}|\cos\gamma = n|\vec{r}| $$

$$ \text{Squaring and adding these scalar components:} $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = (l|\vec{r}|)^2 + (m|\vec{r}|)^2 + (n|\vec{r}|)^2 $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = |\vec{r}|^2 (l^2 + m^2 + n^2) $$

$$ \text{Substitute } x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = |\vec{r}|^2\text{:} $$

$$ |\vec{r}|^2 = |\vec{r}|^2 (l^2 + m^2 + n^2) $$

$$ \text{Since } \vec{r} \neq \vec{0} \implies |\vec{r}| \neq 0\text{, dividing both sides by } |\vec{r}|^2\text{:} $$

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 \quad \blacksquare $$

$$ Corollaries: $$

$$ \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma = (1 - \cos^2\alpha) + (1 - \cos^2\beta) + (1 - \cos^2\gamma) = 3 - (l^2 + m^2 + n^2) = 3 - 1 = 2 $$

$$ \cos 2\alpha + \cos 2\beta + \cos 2\gamma = (2\cos^2\alpha - 1) + (2\cos^2\beta - 1) + (2\cos^2\gamma - 1) = 2(1) - 3 = -1 $$

Proof 4: Relationship Between Direction Cosines and Direction Ratios ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

$$ \begin{array}{l} Given: a, b, c \text{ are direction ratios of a directed line/vector with DCos } l, m, n.\\ To Prove: l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad m = \pm \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad n = \pm \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}\\ Derivation: \end{array} $$

$$ \text{By definition of direction ratios:} $$

$$ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = k \quad (k \text{ is a non-zero scalar}) $$

$$ \implies l = ka, \quad m = kb, \quad n = kc $$

$$ \text{Substitute these into the fundamental identity } l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1\text{:} $$

$$ (ka)^2 + (kb)^2 + (kc)^2 = 1 $$

$$ k^2 (a^2 + b^2 + c^2) = 1 $$

$$ k^2 = \frac{1}{a^2 + b^2 + c^2} \implies k = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

$$ \text{Substituting } k \text{ back gives:} $$

$$ l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad m = \pm \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad n = \pm \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} \quad \blacksquare $$

Scalar (Dot) Product of Two Vectors & Projection of a Vector

Class 12 Mathematics: Vector Algebra

Topic: Scalar (Dot) Product of Two Vectors & Projection of a Vector

Course Level: CBSE Class 12 Senior Secondary / JEE Main Foundation
Pedagogical Blueprint: Comprehensive Classroom Teaching Note & Derivation Compendium

Part 1: Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Definition of Scalar (Dot) Product $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

Let $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ be two non-zero vectors inclined at an angle $\theta$, where $0 \le \theta \le \pi$. The scalar product or dot product of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, denoted by $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$, is defined as the real scalar:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| \cos\theta $$

Critical Boundary Conditions:

  1. If $\vec{a} = \vec{0}$ or $\vec{b} = \vec{0}$, $\theta$ is not defined. In this scenario, we define:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0 $$

  1. Since $|\vec{a}| \ge 0$, $|\vec{b}| \ge 0$, the sign of $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$ depends entirely on $\cos\theta$:
  2. $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} > 0 \iff 0 \le \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$ ($\theta$ is acute)
  3. $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0 \iff \theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$ ($\vec{a} \perp \vec{b}$, for non-zero vectors)
  4. $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} < 0 \iff \frac{\pi}{2} < \theta \le \pi$ ($\theta$ is obtuse)

1.2 Geometric Interpretation $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

Let $\vec{a} = \vec{OA}$ and $\vec{b} = \vec{OB}$ with an angle $\theta$ between them ($0 \le \theta \le \pi$). Drop a perpendicular from point $B$ onto the line support containing $\vec{a}$, meeting it at point $M$.

  • The directed length $OM$ is the magnitude of the projection of $\vec{b}$ along $\vec{a}$:

$$ OM = |\vec{b}| \cos\theta $$

  • Therefore:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| \big(|\vec{b}| \cos\theta\big) = (\text{Magnitude of } \vec{a}) \times (\text{Projection of } \vec{b} \text{ on } \vec{a}) $$

  • Symmetrically, dropping a perpendicular from $A$ to the line of $\vec{b}$ gives:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{b}| \big(|\vec{a}| \cos\theta\big) = (\text{Magnitude of } \vec{b}) \times (\text{Projection of } \vec{a} \text{ on } \vec{b}) $$

Pedagogical Intuition: The dot product measures how much one vector "travels" along the direction of another vector, weighted by the length of the base vector.

1.3 Projection of a Vector $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

(a) Scalar Projection (Magnitude of Projection)

The scalar projection of a vector $\vec{a}$ on a non-zero vector $\vec{b}$ is the scalar component of $\vec{a}$ along the line of direction of $\vec{b}$:

$$ \text{Projection of } \vec{a} \text{ on } \vec{b} = \vec{a} \cdot \hat{b} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|} $$

Similarly:

$$ \text{Projection of } \vec{b} \text{ on } \vec{a} = \vec{b} \cdot \hat{a} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{a}|} $$

(b) Vector Projection

The actual vector component of $\vec{a}$ directed along $\vec{b}$ is obtained by scaling the unit vector $\hat{b}$ by the scalar projection:

$$ \text{Vector projection of } \vec{a} \text{ on } \vec{b} = (\vec{a} \cdot \hat{b})\hat{b} = \left(\frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|^2}\right)\vec{b} $$

(c) Vector Component Perpendicular to $\vec{b}$

Any vector $\vec{a}$ can be uniquely resolved into two components: one parallel ($\vec{a}_\parallel$) and one perpendicular ($\vec{a}_\perp$) to a given vector $\vec{b}$:

$$ \vec{a} = \vec{a}_\parallel + \vec{a}_\perp $$

$$ \vec{a}_\parallel = \left(\frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|^2}\right)\vec{b} $$

$$ \vec{a}_\perp = \vec{a} - \left(\frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|^2}\right)\vec{b} $$

1.4 Fundamental Properties of Scalar Product $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

  1. Commutativity:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = \vec{b} \cdot \vec{a} $$

  1. Self-Product (Square of Magnitude):

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{a} = |\vec{a}||\vec{a}|\cos 0 = |\vec{a}|^2 \implies |\vec{a}| = \sqrt{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{a}} $$

(Standard notation: $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{a} = \vec{a}^2 = |\vec{a}|^2$) 3. Condition for Orthogonality: For two non-zero vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$ \vec{a} \perp \vec{b} \iff \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0 $$

  1. Distributive Law over Vector Addition:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} + \vec{a} \cdot \vec{c} $$

$$ (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) \cdot \vec{c} = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{c} + \vec{b} \cdot \vec{c} $$

  1. Scalar Multiplication Compatibility: For any real scalar $\lambda$:

$$ (\lambda \vec{a}) \cdot \vec{b} = \lambda(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}) = \vec{a} \cdot (\lambda \vec{b}) $$

  1. Orthonormal Basis System ($\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$):

$$ \hat{i} \cdot \hat{i} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{j} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{k} = 1 $$

$$ \hat{i} \cdot \hat{j} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{k} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{i} = 0 $$

Part 2: Complete Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 2.1: Component Form of Scalar Product $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

Statement:
Let $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$. Then:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 $$

Proof:

Given: Two vectors in Cartesian coordinates:
$\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$.

To Prove:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 $$

Derivation:
By direct substitution and applying the distributive property of the dot product over vector addition:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = (a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}) \cdot (b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}) $$

Expanding term-by-term:

$$ \begin{aligned} \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} &= a_1\hat{i} \cdot (b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}) \\ &+ a_2\hat{j} \cdot (b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}) \\ &+ a_3\hat{k} \cdot (b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}) \end{aligned} $$

Distributing scalars and using compatibility with scalar multiplication:

$$ \begin{aligned} \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} &= a_1 b_1 (\hat{i} \cdot \hat{i}) + a_1 b_2 (\hat{i} \cdot \hat{j}) + a_1 b_3 (\hat{i} \cdot \hat{k}) \\ &+ a_2 b_1 (\hat{j} \cdot \hat{i}) + a_2 b_2 (\hat{j} \cdot \hat{j}) + a_2 b_3 (\hat{j} \cdot \hat{k}) \\ &+ a_3 b_1 (\hat{k} \cdot \hat{i}) + a_3 b_2 (\hat{k} \cdot \hat{j}) + a_3 b_3 (\hat{k} \cdot \hat{k}) \end{aligned} $$

Using standard orthonormal relationships:

$$ \hat{i} \cdot \hat{i} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{j} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{k} = 1 $$

$$ \hat{i} \cdot \hat{j} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{i} = \hat{i} \cdot \hat{k} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{i} = \hat{j} \cdot \hat{k} = \hat{k} \cdot \hat{j} = 0 $$

All cross-terms vanish ($0$):

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_1 b_1(1) + a_2 b_2(1) + a_3 b_3(1) = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.2: Angle Between Two Vectors $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

Statement:
The angle $\theta$ between two non-zero vectors $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$ is given by:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}\sqrt{b_1^2 + b_2^2 + b_3^2}} $$

Proof:

By definition:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| \cos\theta \implies \cos\theta = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{a}| |\vec{b}|} $$

Substituting the Cartesian forms: 1. $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3$ 2. $|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}$ 3. $|\vec{b}| = \sqrt{b_1^2 + b_2^2 + b_3^2}$

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}\sqrt{b_1^2 + b_2^2 + b_3^2}} $$

$$ \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}\sqrt{b_1^2 + b_2^2 + b_3^2}}\right) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.3: Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

Statement:
For any two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$ |\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| $$

Proof:

Case 1: If $\vec{a} = \vec{0}$ or $\vec{b} = \vec{0}$.
Then $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0$ and $|\vec{a}||\vec{b}| = 0$. The equality holds ($0 = 0$).

Case 2: If $\vec{a} \ne \vec{0}$ and $\vec{b} \ne \vec{0}$.
By definition:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| \cos\theta $$

Taking the absolute value on both sides:

$$ |\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}| = \big||\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| \cos\theta\big| = |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| |\cos\theta| $$

Since for all real $\theta \in [0, \pi]$, $|\cos\theta| \le 1$:

$$ |\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| (1) = |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.4: Triangle Inequality $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

Statement:
For any two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}| $$

Proof:

Consider $|\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2$:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 = (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) \cdot (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) $$

Expanding by distributivity:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{a} + \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} + \vec{b} \cdot \vec{a} + \vec{b} \cdot \vec{b} $$

Since $\vec{b} \cdot \vec{a} = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$ and $\vec{x} \cdot \vec{x} = |\vec{x}|^2$:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 + 2(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}) + |\vec{b}|^2 $$

Using the property that any real number $x \le |x|$:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} \le |\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}| $$

Therefore:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 \le |\vec{a}|^2 + 2|\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}| + |\vec{b}|^2 $$

Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality ($|\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}|$):

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 \le |\vec{a}|^2 + 2|\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| + |\vec{b}|^2 = (|\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}|)^2 $$

Taking the positive square root on both sides (magnitudes are non-negative):

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.5: Parallelogram Law Identity $\big[\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\big]$

Statement:
The sum of the squares of the diagonals of a parallelogram equals the sum of the squares of all its four sides:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 + |\vec{a} - \vec{b}|^2 = 2(|\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2) $$

Proof:

Expand the first term:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 = (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) \cdot (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = |\vec{a}|^2 + 2(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}) + |\vec{b}|^2 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Expand the second term:

$$ |\vec{a} - \vec{b}|^2 = (\vec{a} - \vec{b}) \cdot (\vec{a} - \vec{b}) = |\vec{a}|^2 - 2(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}) + |\vec{b}|^2 \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Adding equations (1) and (2):

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 + |\vec{a} - \vec{b}|^2 = 2|\vec{a}|^2 + 2|\vec{b}|^2 = 2(|\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Definitions

1.1 Core Definition of Vector (Cross) Product ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ be two non-zero, non-parallel vectors inclined at an angle $\theta$, where $0 \le \theta \le \pi$.

The Vector Product (or Cross Product) of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, denoted by $\vec{a} \times \vec{b}$, is defined as a vector:

$$ \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| \sin\theta \, \hat{n} $$

Where: - $|\vec{a}|$ and $|\vec{b}|$ are the magnitudes of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$. - $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ is the angle between $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ when placed co-initial. - $\hat{n}$ is a unit vector perpendicular to both $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ such that the vectors $\vec{a}$, $\vec{b}$, and $\hat{n}$ form a right-handed system (Right-Hand Thumb Rule).

          ^ n̂  (Perpendicular to plane of a and b)
          |
          |       / b
          |      /
          |  θ  / 
          |--->/___________> a

Right-Hand Thumb Rule: If the fingers of the right hand are curled from $\vec{a}$ towards $\vec{b}$ through the smaller angle $\theta$, the outstretched thumb points in the direction of $\hat{n}$.

Boundary & Special Cases:

  1. If $\vec{a} = \vec{0}$ or $\vec{b} = \vec{0}$, then $\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \vec{0}$.
  2. If $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are collinear / parallel ($\theta = 0$ or $\theta = \pi$):

$$ \sin\theta = 0 \implies \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \vec{0} $$

Conversely, for non-zero vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$, $\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \vec{0} \iff \vec{a} \parallel \vec{b}$. 3. In particular, for any vector $\vec{a}$:

$$ \vec{a} \times \vec{a} = \vec{0}, \quad \vec{a} \times (-\vec{a}) = \vec{0} $$

1.2 Fundamental Properties ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  1. Non-Commutativity (Anti-Commutative Property):

$$ \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = -(\vec{b} \times \vec{a}) $$

(Reversing the rotation from $\vec{b}$ to $\vec{a}$ reverses the thumb's direction via the right-hand rule, giving $-\hat{n}$).

  1. Scalar Multiplication: For any scalar $\lambda$:

$$ \lambda(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) = (\lambda\vec{a}) \times \vec{b} = \vec{a} \times (\lambda\vec{b}) $$

  1. Distributivity over Addition:
  2. Left Distributive: $\vec{a} \times (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) = (\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) + (\vec{a} \times \vec{c})$

  3. Right Distributive: $(\vec{b} + \vec{c}) \times \vec{a} = (\vec{b} \times \vec{a}) + (\vec{c} \times \vec{a})$

  4. Orthogonal Unit Vectors ($\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$):

$$ \hat{i} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k} \times \hat{k} = \vec{0} $$

Cyclic order (Clockwise cycle $\hat{i} \to \hat{j} \to \hat{k} \to \hat{i}$):

$$ \hat{i} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k}, \quad \hat{j} \times \hat{k} = \hat{i}, \quad \hat{k} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j} $$

$$ \hat{j} \times \hat{i} = -\hat{k}, \quad \hat{k} \times \hat{j} = -\hat{i}, \quad \hat{i} \times \hat{k} = -\hat{j} $$

  1. Unit Vector Perpendicular to Plane of $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$ \hat{n} = \pm \frac{\vec{a} \times \vec{b}}{|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|} $$

  1. Angle Between Two Vectors via Sine:

$$ \sin\theta = \frac{|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|}{|\vec{a}| |\vec{b}|} $$

1.3 Geometric Interpretations ⭐⭐⭐⭐

(A) Area of a Parallelogram

  1. Given adjacent sides $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$ \text{Area} = |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| $$

  1. Given diagonals $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$:

$$ \text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2| $$

(B) Area of a Triangle

  1. Given adjacent sides $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$ \Delta = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| $$

  1. Given position vectors of vertices $A(\vec{a})$, $B(\vec{b})$, $C(\vec{c})$:

$$ \Delta = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC}| = \frac{1}{2} |(\vec{b} - \vec{a}) \times (\vec{c} - \vec{a})| = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{a} \times \vec{b} + \vec{b} \times \vec{c} + \vec{c} \times \vec{a}| $$

(C) Collinearity of Three Points

Points $A(\vec{a})$, $B(\vec{b})$, $C(\vec{c})$ are collinear if and only if:

$$ \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = \vec{0} \iff \vec{a} \times \vec{b} + \vec{b} \times \vec{c} + \vec{c} \times \vec{a} = \vec{0} $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 1: Determinant Form of Vector Product ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem: If $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$, then:

$$ \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} $$

Given: Vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ in Cartesian component form.
To Prove: $\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2)\hat{i} - (a_1 b_3 - a_3 b_1)\hat{j} + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)\hat{k}$.

Proof: Using the distributive law of cross product over addition:

$$ \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = (a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}) \times (b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}) $$

Expanding term-by-term:

$$ \begin{aligned} \vec{a} \times \vec{b} &= a_1 b_1(\hat{i} \times \hat{i}) + a_1 b_2(\hat{i} \times \hat{j}) + a_1 b_3(\hat{i} \times \hat{k}) \\ &\quad + a_2 b_1(\hat{j} \times \hat{i}) + a_2 b_2(\hat{j} \times \hat{j}) + a_2 b_3(\hat{j} \times \hat{k}) \\ &\quad + a_3 b_1(\hat{k} \times \hat{i}) + a_3 b_2(\hat{k} \times \hat{j}) + a_3 b_3(\hat{k} \times \hat{k}) \end{aligned} $$

Substitute standard unit vector products: - $\hat{i} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k} \times \hat{k} = \vec{0}$ - $\hat{i} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k}, \quad \hat{i} \times \hat{k} = -\hat{j}$ - $\hat{j} \times \hat{i} = -\hat{k}, \quad \hat{j} \times \hat{k} = \hat{i}$ - $\hat{k} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j}, \quad \hat{k} \times \hat{j} = -\hat{i}$

$$ \begin{aligned} \vec{a} \times \vec{b} &= a_1 b_2\hat{k} - a_1 b_3\hat{j} - a_2 b_1\hat{k} + a_2 b_3\hat{i} + a_3 b_1\hat{j} - a_3 b_2\hat{i} \\ &= (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2)\hat{i} - (a_1 b_3 - a_3 b_1)\hat{j} + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)\hat{k} \end{aligned} $$

By the definition of the expansion of a $3 \times 3$ determinant along the first row:

$$ \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i}(a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2) - \hat{j}(a_1 b_3 - a_3 b_1) + \hat{k}(a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1) $$

Thus:

$$ \vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2: Lagrange's Identity ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem: For any two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 - (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 $$

Given: Two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ with angle $\theta$ between them.
To Prove: $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 + (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2$.

Proof: By definition of vector product and dot product:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| \sin\theta \quad (\text{since } \sin\theta \ge 0 \text{ for } \theta \in [0, \pi] \text{ and } |\hat{n}| = 1) $$

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}| \cos\theta $$

Squaring both equations:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 \sin^2\theta $$

$$ (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 \cos^2\theta $$

Adding the two equations:

$$ \begin{aligned} |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 + (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 &= |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 \sin^2\theta + |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 \cos^2\theta \\ &= |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 (\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta) \end{aligned} $$

Since $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 + (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 $$

Rearranging terms yields:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 - (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 3: Area of a Parallelogram in Terms of Diagonals ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem: If the diagonals of a parallelogram are represented by vectors $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$, then the area of the parallelogram is $\frac{1}{2} |\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2|$.

Given: Parallelogram $ABCD$ with adjacent sides $\vec{AB} = \vec{a}$ and $\vec{AD} = \vec{b}$.
Diagonals $\vec{AC} = \vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{BD} = \vec{d}_2$.
To Prove: $\text{Area}(ABCD) = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2|$.

Proof: Using vector triangle law:

$$ \vec{d}_1 = \vec{AC} = \vec{AB} + \vec{BC} = \vec{a} + \vec{b} $$

$$ \vec{d}_2 = \vec{BD} = \vec{BA} + \vec{AD} = -\vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} - \vec{a} $$

Compute the cross product of the diagonal vectors $\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2$:

$$ \vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2 = (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) \times (\vec{b} - \vec{a}) $$

Distributing across terms:

$$ \vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2 = (\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) - (\vec{a} \times \vec{a}) + (\vec{b} \times \vec{b}) - (\vec{b} \times \vec{a}) $$

Since $\vec{a} \times \vec{a} = \vec{0}$, $\vec{b} \times \vec{b} = \vec{0}$, and $-(\vec{b} \times \vec{a}) = \vec{a} \times \vec{b}$:

$$ \vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2 = (\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) - \vec{0} + \vec{0} + (\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) = 2(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) $$

Taking magnitude on both sides:

$$ |\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2| = |2(\vec{a} \times \vec{b})| = 2 |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| $$

$$ \implies |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2| $$

Since the area of parallelogram with adjacent sides $\vec{a}, \vec{b}$ is $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$:

$$ \text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 4: Vector Expression for Area of Triangle Given 3 Vertices ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem: If $\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$ are the position vectors of the vertices $A, B, C$ of $\triangle ABC$, then:

$$ \text{Area}(\triangle ABC) = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{a} \times \vec{b} + \vec{b} \times \vec{c} + \vec{c} \times \vec{a}| $$

Proof: Sides $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{AC}$ are given by:

$$ \vec{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a} $$

$$ \vec{AC} = \vec{c} - \vec{a} $$

Area of $\triangle ABC$ is:

$$ \Delta = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC}| $$

Evaluate $\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC}$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} &= (\vec{b} - \vec{a}) \times (\vec{c} - \vec{a}) \\ &= \vec{b} \times \vec{c} - \vec{b} \times \vec{a} - \vec{a} \times \vec{c} + \vec{a} \times \vec{a} \end{aligned} $$

Using $\vec{a} \times \vec{a} = \vec{0}$, $-\vec{b} \times \vec{a} = \vec{a} \times \vec{b}$, and $-\vec{a} \times \vec{c} = \vec{c} \times \vec{a}$:

$$ \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = \vec{b} \times \vec{c} + \vec{a} \times \vec{b} + \vec{c} \times \vec{a} = \vec{a} \times \vec{b} + \vec{b} \times \vec{c} + \vec{c} \times \vec{a} $$

Taking magnitudes and multiplying by $\frac{1}{2}$:

$$ \text{Area}(\triangle ABC) = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{a} \times \vec{b} + \vec{b} \times \vec{c} + \vec{c} \times \vec{a}| $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

💡 Example 1: Identity Evaluation with Sine Angles [CBSE 2024, 2 Marks]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: If $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are the direction angles of a directed vector $\vec{r}$, find the value of:

$$ \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma $$

Solution: 1. Let $l = \cos\alpha$, $m = \cos\beta$, $n = \cos\gamma$ be the direction cosines of $\vec{r}$. 2. We know the fundamental identity:

$$ \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 $$

  1. Using the standard trigonometric identity $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$:

$$ \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma = (1 - \cos^2\alpha) + (1 - \cos^2\beta) + (1 - \cos^2\gamma) $$

  1. Grouping the constant terms:

$$ = 3 - (\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma) $$

  1. Substituting $\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1$:

$$ = 3 - 1 = 2 $$

Final Answer: $\mathbf{2}$

💡 Example 2: Determining Unknown Direction Angle [CBSE 2023, 2 Marks]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: A vector $\vec{r}$ makes angles $\frac{\pi}{3}$ with the $x$-axis and $\frac{\pi}{4}$ with the $y$-axis. If it makes an acute angle $\theta$ with the $z$-axis, find the value of $\theta$ and write the unit vector along $\vec{r}$.

Solution: 1. Given $\alpha = \frac{\pi}{3}$, $\beta = \frac{\pi}{4}$, and $\gamma = \theta$ (where $\theta \in [0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$). 2. Calculate the known direction cosines:

$$ l = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{2} $$

$$ m = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} $$

$$ n = \cos\theta $$

  1. Apply the DC relation $l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$:

$$ \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 + \cos^2\theta = 1 $$

$$ \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{2} + \cos^2\theta = 1 $$

$$ \frac{3}{4} + \cos^2\theta = 1 \implies \cos^2\theta = 1 - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{1}{4} $$

  1. Taking the square root:

$$ \cos\theta = \pm \frac{1}{2} $$

Since $\theta$ is specified to be acute, $\cos\theta > 0$:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{3} $$

  1. The unit vector along $\vec{r}$ is $\hat{r} = l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k}$:

$$ \hat{r} = \frac{1}{2}\hat{i} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{j} + \frac{1}{2}\hat{k} $$

💡 Example 3: Collinearity & Linear Combination [CBSE 2022 Term-II, 3 Marks]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: Show that the points $A(2, 3, -4)$, $B(1, -2, 3)$, and $C(3, 8, -11)$ are collinear, and find the ratio in which $B$ divides $AC$.

Solution: 1. Construct the vectors $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{BC}$:

$$ \vec{AB} = (1 - 2)\hat{i} + (-2 - 3)\hat{j} + (3 - (-4))\hat{k} = -\hat{i} - 5\hat{j} + 7\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{BC} = (3 - 1)\hat{i} + (8 - (-2))\hat{j} + (-11 - 3)\hat{k} = 2\hat{i} + 10\hat{j} - 14\hat{k} $$

  1. Check for scalar proportionality $\vec{BC} = \lambda \vec{AB}$:

$$ \vec{BC} = -2(-\hat{i} - 5\hat{j} + 7\hat{k}) = -2\vec{AB} $$

Since $\vec{BC} = -2\vec{AB}$, $\vec{BC}$ and $\vec{AB}$ are collinear (parallel). 3. Both vectors share the common point $B$. Therefore, points $A, B$, and $C$ must lie on the same straight line, proving collinearity. 4. To find the ratio in which $B$ divides $AC$, let the ratio be $k : 1$. By the section formula for the $x$-coordinate:

$$ x_B = \frac{k(x_C) + 1(x_A)}{k + 1} \implies 1 = \frac{k(3) + 1(2)}{k + 1} $$

$$ k + 1 = 3k + 2 \implies 2k = -1 \implies k = -\frac{1}{2} $$

The negative sign indicates external division. Final Answer: Points are collinear; $B$ divides $AC$ externally in the ratio $1 : 2$.

💡 Example 5: Direction Cosines of Equally Inclined Vector [CBSE 2020, 1 Mark]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem: A vector $\vec{r}$ is equally inclined to the coordinate axes $OX, OY$, and $OZ$. Find its direction cosines.

Solution: 1. Since $\vec{r}$ is equally inclined to the axes:

$$ \alpha = \beta = \gamma $$

$$ \implies \cos\alpha = \cos\beta = \cos\gamma \implies l = m = n $$

  1. Using $l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$:

$$ l^2 + l^2 + l^2 = 1 \implies 3l^2 = 1 \implies l = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} $$

  1. Therefore:

$$ l = m = n = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

  1. Confusing Direction Cosines with Direction Ratios:
  2. Trap: Students treat any set of numbers $(a, b, c)$ as $(l, m, n)$ without checking if $a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = 1$.

  3. Fix: The components of a vector $\vec{v} = a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}$ are its direction ratios. The components of its unit vector $\hat{v} = l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k}$ are its direction cosines.

  4. Writing False Identities for Angle Ratios:

  5. Trap: Writing $\tan^2\alpha + \tan^2\beta + \tan^2\gamma = 1$ or $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 180^\circ$.

  6. Fix: There is no linear relationship between the direction angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$. The sum $(\alpha + \beta + \gamma)$ is not constant. The relationship holds strictly for their squared cosines: $\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1$.

  7. Ignoring the Sign when Taking Square Roots for Direction Angles:

  8. Trap: If $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1}{4}$, students automatically write $\cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}$ without checking whether the problem specifies an acute or obtuse angle. If obtuse, $\cos\theta = -\frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$.

  9. Collinearity vs. Co-planarity Confusion:

  10. Trap: Proving $\vec{AB} = \lambda \vec{CD}$ and concluding that $A, B, C, D$ are collinear.
  11. Fix: $\vec{AB} = \lambda \vec{CD}$ only proves that the lines $AB$ and $CD$ are parallel. To prove points $A, B, C$ are collinear, they must share a common point (e.g., $\vec{AB} = \lambda \vec{BC}$).

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

  • Unit Vector Quick Hack: To convert any vector $\vec{v} = a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}$ into its direction cosines, simply divide each coefficient by $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}$:

$$ (l, m, n) = \left(\frac{a}{|\vec{v}|}, \frac{b}{|\vec{v}|}, \frac{c}{|\vec{v}|}\right) $$

  • The Double-Angle Cosine Identity: If a question asks for $\cos 2\alpha + \cos 2\beta + \cos 2\gamma$:

$$ \cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1 $$

$$ \implies \cos 2\alpha + \cos 2\beta + \cos 2\gamma = 2(\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma) - 3 = 2(1) - 3 = \mathbf{-1} $$

(Direct 1-mark objective question shortcut!)

  • Equally Inclined Sign Rule: If a vector is equally inclined to the axes, its unit vector is always:

  • Collinearity Verification via Ratios: For vectors $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$:

$$ \vec{a} \parallel \vec{b} \iff \frac{a_1}{b_1} = \frac{a_2}{b_2} = \frac{a_3}{b_3} = \lambda $$

Check this ratio first before writing down algebraic expansions.

[CBSE 2022 Term-II, 3 Marks]

$$ \vec{a} = \hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 7\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{b} = 2\hat{i} + 6\hat{j} + 3\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{c} = 3\hat{i} + 10\hat{j} - \hat{k} $$

  1. Form the directed vectors $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{BC}$:

$$ \vec{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a} = (2-1)\hat{i} + (6-2)\hat{j} + (3-7)\hat{k} = \hat{i} + 4\hat{j} - 4\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{BC} = \vec{c} - \vec{b} = (3-2)\hat{i} + (10-6)\hat{j} + (-1-3)\hat{k} = \hat{i} + 4\hat{j} - 4\hat{k} $$

  1. Establish the scalar proportional relationship:

$$ \vec{AB} = 1 \cdot \vec{BC} $$

  1. Divide by the denominator $(-1)$:

$$ \vec{OR} = \frac{-3\vec{a} - 5\vec{b}}{-1} = 3\vec{a} + 5\vec{b} $$

  1. Verify that $P$ is the midpoint of segment $RQ$:

💡 Example 3.5: Direction Cosines & Unit Vector Involving Angular Constraints

5.1 The "Cross-Check" Method for Section Formula

To avoid mixing up internal and external division formulas, assign signs directly to the ratio:

$$ \text{Internal } (m : n) \implies \text{Use } +n \quad \mid \quad \text{External } (m : n) \implies \text{Use } -n $$

$$ \vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} + (-n)\vec{a}}{m + (-n)} = \frac{m\vec{b} - n\vec{a}}{m - n} $$

A single formula handles both cases.

5.2 The Direction Cosine Squared-Trig Identities

CBSE frequently sets 1-mark MCQs on angular relations with the axes:

$$ \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 $$

Using $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$:

$$ (1 - \sin^2\alpha) + (1 - \sin^2\beta) + (1 - \sin^2\gamma) = 1 \implies \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma = 2 $$

Using double-angle identities $\cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$:

$$ \cos 2\alpha + \cos 2\beta + \cos 2\gamma = -1 $$

5.3 Instant Collinearity Inspection

To check collinearity of two vectors in under 5 seconds:

$$ \vec{a} = 4\hat{i} - 6\hat{j} + 10\hat{k}, \quad \vec{b} = -6\hat{i} + 9\hat{j} - 15\hat{k} $$

Check ratios pairwise:

$$ \frac{-6}{4} = -\frac{3}{2}, \quad \frac{9}{-6} = -\frac{3}{2}, \quad \frac{-15}{10} = -\frac{3}{2} $$

Constant ratio $\lambda = -\frac{3}{2} \implies$ collinear and antiparallel immediately.

5.4 Centroid of a Triangle Shortcut

For a triangle with vertices $\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$, the centroid $G$ has position vector:

$$ \vec{g} = \frac{\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}}{3} $$

Golden Property: For any origin or reference point $O$:

$$ \vec{GA} + \vec{GB} + \vec{GC} = \vec{0} $$

5.5 Fast Unit Vector Normalization

When finding unit vectors, factor out any common scalar integer first:

$$ \vec{v} = 12\hat{i} - 16\hat{j} + 15\hat{k} $$

$$ |\vec{v}| = \sqrt{144 + 256 + 225} = \sqrt{625} = 25 \implies \hat{v} = \frac{12}{25}\hat{i} - \frac{16}{25}\hat{j} + \frac{3}{5}\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{v} = |\vec{v}|\hat{v} = |\vec{v}|(l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k}) $$

  • Rule of Thumb: To extract DCos instantaneously, simply compute the unit vector $\hat{v}$. The coefficients of $\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$ in $\hat{v}$ are $l, m, n$.

  • Sum of Squared Trigonometric Identities Cheat Sheet:

$$ \sum \cos^2\alpha = l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

$$ \sum \sin^2\alpha = \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma = 2 $$

$$ \sum \cos 2\alpha = \cos 2\alpha + \cos 2\beta + \cos 2\gamma = -1 $$

  1. External Division via Negative Ratio Trick:
  2. Treat external division in ratio $m : n$ simply as internal division in ratio $m : (-n)$ or $(-m) : n$.
  3. Using the internal formula with ratio $k : 1$ where $k = -\frac{m}{n}$ guarantees algebraic consistency and avoids having to memorize two separate formulas:

$$ \vec{r} = \frac{k\vec{b} + \vec{a}}{k + 1} $$

  1. Ratio Finding Strategy:
  2. When asked to find the ratio in which a point/plane divides a segment $AB$, never assume $m : n$ (2 unknowns).
  3. Always assume the ratio to be $k : 1$. If $k > 0$, the division is internal; if $k < 0$, the division is external.
💡 Example 3.1: Finding Vector Projection & Resolution $\big[\text{CBSE 2024, 3 Marks}\big]$
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem:
If $\vec{a} = 2\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = -\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + \hat{k}$, find: 1. The scalar projection of $\vec{a}$ on $\vec{b}$. 2. The vector projection of $\vec{a}$ along $\vec{b}$. 3. Express $\vec{a}$ as the sum of a vector $\vec{a}_1$ parallel to $\vec{b}$ and a vector $\vec{a}_2$ perpendicular to $\vec{b}$.

Solution:

Step 1: Compute $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$ and $|\vec{b}|$

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = (2)( -1) + (2)(2) + (3)(1) = -2 + 4 + 3 = 5 $$

$$ |\vec{b}| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 2^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt{6} $$

Step 2: Scalar Projection

$$ \text{Projection of } \vec{a} \text{ on } \vec{b} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{6}} = \frac{5\sqrt{6}}{6} $$

Step 3: Vector Projection ($\vec{a}_1$)

$$ \vec{a}_1 = \left(\frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|^2}\right)\vec{b} = \frac{5}{6}(-\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + \hat{k}) = -\frac{5}{6}\hat{i} + \frac{5}{3}\hat{j} + \frac{5}{6}\hat{k} $$

Step 4: Perpendicular Component ($\vec{a}_2$)

$$ \vec{a}_2 = \vec{a} - \vec{a}_1 = (2\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}) - \left(-\frac{5}{6}\hat{i} + \frac{5}{3}\hat{j} + \frac{5}{6}\hat{k}\right) $$

$$ \vec{a}_2 = \left(2 + \frac{5}{6}\right)\hat{i} + \left(2 - \frac{5}{3}\right)\hat{j} + \left(3 - \frac{5}{6}\right)\hat{k} = \frac{17}{6}\hat{i} + \frac{1}{3}\hat{j} + \frac{13}{6}\hat{k} $$

(Self-check: $\vec{a}_2 \cdot \vec{b} = \frac{17}{6}(-1) + \frac{1}{3}(2) + \frac{13}{6}(1) = -\frac{17}{6} + \frac{4}{6} + \frac{13}{6} = 0 \implies \vec{a}_2 \perp \vec{b}$)

💡 Example 3.2: Dot Product with Zero Sum $\big[\text{CBSE 2023, 2020, 4 Marks}\big]$
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem:
Three vectors $\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$ satisfy the condition $\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c} = \vec{0}$. If $|\vec{a}| = 3$, $|\vec{b}| = 4$, and $|\vec{c}| = 2\sqrt{7}$ (or $5$), evaluate the quantity:

$$ \mu = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} + \vec{b} \cdot \vec{c} + \vec{c} \cdot \vec{a} $$

(Take $|\vec{a}| = 3, |\vec{b}| = 4, |\vec{c}| = 5$)

Solution:

Step 1: Use Self-Product on the Given Condition

$$ \vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c} = \vec{0} $$

Taking the dot product of both sides with $(\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c})$:

$$ (\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}) \cdot (\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}) = \vec{0} \cdot \vec{0} = 0 $$

Step 2: Expand using Distributivity and Commutativity

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2 + |\vec{c}|^2 + 2(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} + \vec{b} \cdot \vec{c} + \vec{c} \cdot \vec{a}) = 0 $$

Step 3: Substitute the Known Magnitudes

$$ 3^2 + 4^2 + 5^2 + 2\mu = 0 $$

$$ 9 + 16 + 25 + 2\mu = 0 $$

$$ 50 + 2\mu = 0 \implies 2\mu = -50 \implies \mu = -25 $$

$$ \mathbf{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} + \vec{b} \cdot \vec{c} + \vec{c} \cdot \vec{a} = -25} $$

💡 Example 3.3: Mutually Perpendicular Unit Vectors $\big[\text{CBSE 2022 Term-2, 2 Marks}\big]$
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem:
If $\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$ are three mutually perpendicular unit vectors, find the value of:

$$ |2\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}| $$

Solution:

Step 1: Identify Given Properties - Unit vectors: $|\vec{a}| = |\vec{b}| = |\vec{c}| = 1$ - Mutually perpendicular: $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = \vec{b} \cdot \vec{c} = \vec{c} \cdot \vec{a} = 0$

Step 2: Consider the Square of the Magnitude

$$ |2\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}|^2 = (2\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}) \cdot (2\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}) $$

Expanding:

$$ |2\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}|^2 = 4|\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2 + |\vec{c}|^2 + 4(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}) + 2(\vec{b} \cdot \vec{c}) + 4(\vec{c} \cdot \vec{a}) $$

Step 3: Substitute the Values

$$ |2\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}|^2 = 4(1)^2 + (1)^2 + (1)^2 + 4(0) + 2(0) + 4(0) $$

$$ |2\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}|^2 = 4 + 1 + 1 = 6 $$

Taking the positive square root:

$$ |2\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}| = \sqrt{6} $$

💡 Example 3.4: Finding an Unknown Parameter via Orthogonality $\big[\text{CBSE 2021 / Sample Paper, 2 Marks}\big]$
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Problem:
Find the value of $\lambda$ such that the vectors $\vec{a} = 2\hat{i} - \hat{j} + 2\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = 3\hat{i} + \lambda\hat{j} + \hat{k}$ are perpendicular to each other. Hence, find the projection of $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$ on $\vec{a} - \vec{b}$.

Solution:

Part 1: Condition of Orthogonality

$$ \vec{a} \perp \vec{b} \iff \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0 $$

$$ (2)(3) + (-1)(\lambda) + (2)(1) = 0 $$

$$ 6 - \lambda + 2 = 0 \implies \lambda = 8 $$

Part 2: Calculate Projection of $(\vec{a} + \vec{b})$ on $(\vec{a} - \vec{b})$ Let $\vec{u} = \vec{a} + \vec{b}$ and $\vec{v} = \vec{a} - \vec{b}$.

$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) \cdot (\vec{a} - \vec{b}) = |\vec{a}|^2 - |\vec{b}|^2 $$

Computing magnitudes: - $|\vec{a}|^2 = 2^2 + (-1)^2 + 2^2 = 4 + 1 + 4 = 9$ - Since $\lambda = 8$: $\vec{b} = 3\hat{i} + 8\hat{j} + \hat{k}$ - $|\vec{b}|^2 = 3^2 + 8^2 + 1^2 = 9 + 64 + 1 = 74$

Numerator:

$$ \vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 9 - 74 = -65 $$

Denominator:

$$ |\vec{v}| = |\vec{a} - \vec{b}| $$

$$ \vec{a} - \vec{b} = (2-3)\hat{i} + (-1-8)\hat{j} + (2-1)\hat{k} = -\hat{i} - 9\hat{j} + \hat{k} $$

$$ |\vec{a} - \vec{b}| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + (-9)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{1 + 81 + 1} = \sqrt{83} $$

$$ \text{Projection} = \frac{\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v}}{|\vec{v}|} = \frac{-65}{\sqrt{83}} = -\frac{65\sqrt{83}}{83} $$

(Note: If the board asks for "magnitude of projection", write $\frac{65}{\sqrt{83}}$ and mention that scalar projection with sign indicates opposite orientation).

Part 5: Speed Hacks & Golden Points for Board Exams

1. The Symmetric Expansion Hack

Whenever a problem states:

$$ \vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c} = \vec{0} $$

Immediately write:

$$ \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} + \vec{b} \cdot \vec{c} + \vec{c} \cdot \vec{a} = -\frac{1}{2}\left(|\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2 + |\vec{c}|^2\right) $$

Saves 2 minutes of derivation during the exam.

2. Difference of Squares for Orthogonal Diagonals

$$ (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) \cdot (\vec{a} - \vec{b}) = |\vec{a}|^2 - |\vec{b}|^2 $$

  • If $(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) \perp (\vec{a} - \vec{b})$, then $|\vec{a}|^2 - |\vec{b}|^2 = 0 \implies |\vec{a}| = |\vec{b}|$.
  • Geometric Deduction: The diagonals of a parallelogram are perpendicular if and only if the parallelogram is a rhombus.

3. Angle Bisector Golden Rule

The vector along the internal angle bisector of two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ is:

$$ \vec{v}_{\text{bisector}} = \lambda (\hat{a} + \hat{b}) = \lambda \left(\frac{\vec{a}}{|\vec{a}|} + \frac{\vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|}\right) $$

The vector along the external angle bisector is:

$$ \vec{v}_{\text{ext-bisector}} = \mu (\hat{a} - \hat{b}) $$

4. Direct Coordinates of a Unit Vector with Direction Angles

If a unit vector $\hat{r}$ makes angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ with the positive coordinate axes:

$$ \hat{r} = (\cos\alpha)\hat{i} + (\cos\beta)\hat{j} + (\cos\gamma)\hat{k} $$

and the dot product ensures:

$$ \hat{r} \cdot \hat{i} = \cos\alpha, \quad \hat{r} \cdot \hat{j} = \cos\beta, \quad \hat{r} \cdot \hat{k} = \cos\gamma $$

$$ \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = |\hat{r}|^2 = 1 $$

💡 Example 3: Application of Lagrange's Identity
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2022 Term-II, 2 Marks]
If $|\vec{a}| = 10$, $|\vec{b}| = 2$, and $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 12$, find the value of $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$.

Solution: Step 1: Apply Lagrange’s Identity

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 + (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 $$

Step 2: Substitute known numerical values

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 + (12)^2 = (10)^2 \times (2)^2 $$

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 + 144 = 100 \times 4 = 400 $$

Step 3: Solve for $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = 400 - 144 = 256 $$

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = \sqrt{256} = 16 $$

(Since magnitude is non-negative, $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = 16$.)

💡 Example 4: Condition of Collinearity of Three Position Vectors
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2021 Compartment / 2020, 3 Marks]
Show that the points $A, B, C$ with position vectors $\vec{a} = -2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 5\hat{k}$, $\vec{b} = \hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}$, and $\vec{c} = 7\hat{i} - \hat{k}$ are collinear.

Solution: Step 1: Compute vectors $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{AC}$

$$ \vec{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a} = (1 - (-2))\hat{i} + (2 - 3)\hat{j} + (3 - 5)\hat{k} = 3\hat{i} - \hat{j} - 2\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{AC} = \vec{c} - \vec{a} = (7 - (-2))\hat{i} + (0 - 3)\hat{j} + (-1 - 5)\hat{k} = 9\hat{i} - 3\hat{j} - 6\hat{k} $$

Step 2: Check proportional relationship or evaluate cross product Method A (Proportionality):

$$ \vec{AC} = 9\hat{i} - 3\hat{j} - 6\hat{k} = 3(3\hat{i} - \hat{j} - 2\hat{k}) = 3\vec{AB} $$

Since $\vec{AC} = \lambda \vec{AB}$ with $\lambda = 3$, $\vec{AB} \parallel \vec{AC}$.

$$ \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 3 & -1 & -2 \\ 9 & -3 & -6 \end{vmatrix} $$

Since $\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = \vec{0}$ and both vectors share a common point $A$, the points $A, B,$ and $C$ are collinear.

💡 Example 5: Solving Vector Equations Using Cross Product
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2025 Sample Question Paper, 5 Marks]
Find a vector $\vec{r}$ of magnitude $3\sqrt{2}$ units which makes an angle of $\frac{\pi}{4}$ and $\frac{\pi}{2}$ with $y$-axis and $z$-axis respectively, and satisfies $(\vec{r} \times \vec{a}) = \vec{b}$, where $\vec{a} = \hat{i} + \hat{j} + \hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = 2\hat{i} - \hat{j} - \hat{k}$ or determine whether such a vector exists.

Solution: Step 1: Check consistency of the equation $\vec{r} \times \vec{a} = \vec{b}$ For any vector cross product $\vec{r} \times \vec{a} = \vec{b}$, the resultant vector $\vec{b}$ must be orthogonal to both $\vec{r}$ and $\vec{a}$:

$$ \vec{b} \cdot \vec{a} = 0 $$

Check dot product:

$$ \vec{b} \cdot \vec{a} = (2\hat{i} - \hat{j} - \hat{k}) \cdot (\hat{i} + \hat{j} + \hat{k}) = 2(1) + (-1)(1) + (-1)(1) = 2 - 1 - 1 = 0 $$

The orthogonality condition $\vec{b} \perp \vec{a}$ holds.

Step 2: Direction cosines of $\vec{r}$ Let $\vec{r} = |\vec{r}|(l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k})$.
Given: - $\beta = \frac{\pi}{4} \implies m = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ - $\gamma = \frac{\pi}{2} \implies n = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0$

Using $l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$:

$$ l^2 + \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^2 + 0^2 = 1 \implies l^2 + \frac{1}{2} = 1 \implies l^2 = \frac{1}{2} \implies l = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} $$

Step 3: Construct candidate vector(s) $\vec{r}$ Since $|\vec{r}| = 3\sqrt{2}$:

$$ \vec{r} = 3\sqrt{2}\left(\pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{i} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\hat{j} + 0\hat{k}\right) = \pm 3\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} $$

Step 4: Verify which candidate satisfies $\vec{r} \times \vec{a} = \vec{b}$ - Case 1: $\vec{r}_1 = 3\hat{i} + 3\hat{j}$

$$ \vec{r}_1 \times \vec{a} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 3 & 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i}(3 - 0) - \hat{j}(3 - 0) + \hat{k}(3 - 3) = 3\hat{i} - 3\hat{j} \ne 2\hat{i} - \hat{j} - \hat{k} $$

  • Case 2: $\vec{r}_2 = -3\hat{i} + 3\hat{j}$

$$ \vec{r}_2 \times \vec{a} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ -3 & 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i}(3 - 0) - \hat{j}(-3 - 0) + \hat{k}(-3 - 3) = 3\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 6\hat{k} \ne \vec{b} $$

Conclusion:
No such vector $\vec{r}$ exists that simultaneously satisfies the given direction angles, magnitude, and the cross-product relation.

  • Counter-clockwise traversal yields negative:

$$ \hat{j} \times \hat{i} = -\hat{k}, \quad \hat{k} \times \hat{j} = -\hat{i}, \quad \hat{i} \times \hat{k} = -\hat{j} $$

5.2 Determinant Zero Shortcut for Parallel Vectors

If two vectors have proportional coefficients:

$$ \frac{a_1}{b_1} = \frac{a_2}{b_2} = \frac{a_3}{b_3} = k $$

Rows 2 and 3 of the cross-product determinant are linearly dependent $\implies$ the determinant is identically $\vec{0}$ without manual cofactor expansion.

5.3 Lagrange’s Identity as an Angle Calculator

When finding $\sin\theta$, avoiding the full determinant calculation of $\vec{a} \times \vec{b}$ can save time if $|\vec{a}|$, $|\vec{b}|$, and $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$ are readily available: 1. Compute scalar dot product: $k = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$ 2. Compute $\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}}{|\vec{a}| |\vec{b}|}$ 3. Evaluate $\sin\theta = \sqrt{1 - \cos^2\theta}$

5.4 Area Invariance Principle

For any triangle $ABC$:

$$ \vec{AB} \times \vec{AC} = \vec{BC} \times \vec{BA} = \vec{CA} \times \vec{CB} $$

$$ \implies |\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC}| = |\vec{BC} \times \vec{BA}| = |\vec{CA} \times \vec{CB}| $$

Any pair of co-initial vectors formed by the three vertices produces the identical scalar area.

  1. Rearrange for angle between $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$: Given $\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c} = \vec{0} \implies \vec{a} + \vec{b} = -\vec{c}$ (Marking: $[\frac{1}{2}\text{ mark}]$ for isolating $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$)

  2. Take dot product of both sides with itself:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b}|^2 = |-\vec{c}|^2 $$

$$ |\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2 + 2(\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}) = |\vec{c}|^2 $$

(Marking: $[1\text{ mark}]$ for expansion formula)

$$ 3^2 + 5^2 + 2|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta = 7^2 $$

$$ 9 + 25 + 2(3)(5)\cos\theta = 49 $$

$$ 34 + 30\cos\theta = 49 $$

$$ 30\cos\theta = 15 \implies \cos\theta = \frac{15}{30} = \frac{1}{2} $$

(Marking: $[1\text{ mark}]$ for finding $\cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}$)

  1. Determine angle $\theta$:

$$ \theta = \frac{\pi}{3} \quad (60^\circ) $$

(Marking: $[\frac{1}{2}\text{ mark}]$ for the angle value)

  1. Evaluate $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$: Using vector cross product magnitude definition:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta $$

Since $\theta = \frac{\pi}{3} \implies \sin\theta = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = (3)(5)\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = \frac{15\sqrt{3}}{2} $$

Question 14

[CBSE 2020 / 2022 Term-2 / 2025 Compartment, 5 Marks] | ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

(a) Prove that for any two vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2|\vec{b}|^2 - (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 \quad \text{(Lagrange's Identity)} $$

(b) If $|\vec{a}| = 10$, $|\vec{b}| = 2$ and $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 12$, find $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$. (c) Show that $(\vec{a} - \vec{b}) \times (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = 2(\vec{a} \times \vec{b})$ and give its geometric interpretation.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution & Marking Scheme:

Part (a): Proof of Lagrange's Identity

  1. Let $\theta$ be the angle between $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$.

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta $$

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2|\vec{b}|^2\sin^2\theta = |\vec{a}|^2|\vec{b}|^2(1 - \cos^2\theta) $$

$$ = |\vec{a}|^2|\vec{b}|^2 - (|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta)^2 $$

Since $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta$:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2|\vec{b}|^2 - (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 $$

(Marking: $[1\frac{1}{2}\text{ marks}]$ for algebraic proof)

Part (b): Numerical Evaluation

  1. Substitute values into Lagrange's Identity:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = (10)^2(2)^2 - (12)^2 $$

$$ = (100)(4) - 144 = 400 - 144 = 256 $$

  1. Take square root:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = \sqrt{256} = 16 $$

(Marking: $[1\frac{1}{2}\text{ marks}]$ for substitution and calculating 16)

Part (c): Expansion and Geometric Interpretation

  1. Expand using distributive property of vector product:

$$ (\vec{a} - \vec{b}) \times (\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = \vec{a} \times \vec{a} + \vec{a} \times \vec{b} - \vec{b} \times \vec{a} - \vec{b} \times \vec{b} $$

  1. Since $\vec{a} \times \vec{a} = \vec{0}$, $\vec{b} \times \vec{b} = \vec{0}$, and $-\vec{b} \times \vec{a} = \vec{a} \times \vec{b}$:

$$ = \vec{0} + \vec{a} \times \vec{b} + \vec{a} \times \vec{b} - \vec{0} = 2(\vec{a} \times \vec{b}) $$

(Marking: $[1\text{ mark}]$ for step-by-step vector simplification)

  1. Geometric Interpretation: If $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ represent the adjacent sides of a parallelogram, then $(\vec{a} + \vec{b})$ and $(\vec{a} - \vec{b})$ represent its diagonals. The equality demonstrates that the area of a parallelogram formed by the diagonals is twice the area of the parallelogram formed by the adjacent sides $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$. (Marking: $[1\text{ mark}]$ for clear geometric explanation)

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 Vector Product Shortcut: The "2312" Mental Determinant Method

When computing $\vec{a} \times \vec{b}$ for $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$, skip drawing the $3 \times 3$ determinant: 1. Write column coefficients for indices $2, 3, 1, 2$:

$$ \begin{matrix} a_2 & a_3 & a_1 & a_2 \\ b_2 & b_3 & b_1 & b_2 \end{matrix} $$

  1. Compute 2x2 criss-cross products directly:
  2. $\hat{i}\text{-component}: (a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2)$
  3. $\hat{j}\text{-component}: (a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3)$
  4. $\hat{k}\text{-component}: (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)$ Time saved: ~45 seconds; eliminates the classic sign slip on the middle $(-\hat{j})$ cofactor.

1.2 Lagrange’s Identity for Rapid Modulus Calculations

Avoid expanding cross-product components when only magnitudes are given or asked:

$$ |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 + (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 |\vec{b}|^2 $$

  • Trigonometric Form: $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2|\vec{b}|^2 - (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 = |\vec{a}|^2|\vec{b}|^2 (1 - \cos^2\theta) = |\vec{a}|^2|\vec{b}|^2\sin^2\theta$.
  • Instant MCQ Shortcut: If $|\vec{a}| = 2$, $|\vec{b}| = 5$, and $|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}| = 8$, find $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$:

$$ (\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b})^2 = 2^2 \cdot 5^2 - 8^2 = 100 - 64 = 36 \implies \vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = \pm 6 $$

(Never write just $+6$; examiner traps will test both signs unless angle is stated acute).

1.3 Rapid Symmetric Modulus Expansions

For questions involving conditions like $\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c} = \vec{0}$:

$$ |\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}|^2 = |\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2 + |\vec{c}|^2 + 2(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} + \vec{b}\cdot\vec{c} + \vec{c}\cdot\vec{a}) $$

  • If $\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c} = \vec{0}$, then:

$$ \vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} + \vec{b}\cdot\vec{c} + \vec{c}\cdot\vec{a} = -\frac{1}{2}\left(|\vec{a}|^2 + |\vec{b}|^2 + |\vec{c}|^2\right) $$

  • If $\vec{a}, \vec{b}, \vec{c}$ are unit vectors summing to $\vec{0}$, the dot-product sum is unconditionally $-\frac{3}{2}$.

1.4 Area of Quadrilaterals via Diagonals

For any plane quadrilateral with diagonal vectors $\vec{d}_1$ and $\vec{d}_2$:

$$ \text{Area} = \frac{1}{2}|\vec{d}_1 \times \vec{d}_2| $$

  • Trap Avoidance: Do NOT use $\frac{1}{2}|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$ for a parallelogram if the vectors given are adjacent sides; for adjacent sides $\vec{a}, \vec{b}$, $\text{Area} = |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|$. The factor of $\frac{1}{2}$ is strictly reserved for diagonals or triangles.

1.5 Collinearity Fast Check

To check if three points $A(\vec{a}), B(\vec{b}), C(\vec{c})$ are collinear: - Form $\vec{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a}$ and $\vec{BC} = \vec{c} - \vec{b}$. - Check proportionality of direction ratios:

$$ \frac{x_2 - x_1}{x_3 - x_2} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{y_3 - y_2} = \frac{z_2 - z_1}{z_3 - z_2} = \lambda $$

  • If constant ratio $\lambda$ holds across all three components, they are collinear. No need to compute $|\vec{AB}| + |\vec{BC}| = |\vec{AC}|$ (avoids radical calculations).

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT High-Yield Axioms)

  1. Direction Cosines vs Direction Ratios:
  2. Direction cosines MUST satisfy:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 \quad \text{or} \quad \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 $$

  • A critical deduction tested in boards:

$$ \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma = (1-\cos^2\alpha) + (1-\cos^2\beta) + (1-\cos^2\gamma) = 3 - 1 = 2 $$

  • Direction ratios $a, b, c$ do NOT sum to 1; they are any proportional triplet $(ka, kb, kc)$.

  • Self Cross-Product:

$$ \vec{a} \times \vec{a} = \vec{0} \quad (\text{zero vector, NOT scalar } 0) $$

Always write the arrow over $\vec{0}$ in vector equations like $\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \vec{0}$.

  1. Cauchy-Schwarz & Triangle Inequalities for Vectors:
  2. $|\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}|$ (Equality holds iff $\vec{a}, \vec{b}$ are collinear).

  3. $|\vec{a} + \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}|$ (Triangle inequality; equality holds iff vectors are collinear and have the same direction).

  4. Distributive Law Validity:

  5. Dot product over addition: $\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) = \vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} + \vec{a}\cdot\vec{c}$
  6. Cross product over addition: $\vec{a} \times (\vec{b} + \vec{c}) = \vec{a}\times\vec{b} + \vec{a}\times\vec{c}$

  7. Cross product is anti-commutative: $\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = -(\vec{b} \times \vec{a})$.

  8. Right-Handed Triad Condition:

  9. $\hat{i} \times \hat{j} = \hat{k}$, $\hat{j} \times \hat{k} = \hat{i}$, $\hat{k} \times \hat{i} = \hat{j}$
  10. Reversing the order introduces a negative sign: $\hat{j} \times \hat{i} = -\hat{k}$.
