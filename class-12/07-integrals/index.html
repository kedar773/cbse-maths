Indefinite Integrals as Anti-derivatives & Integration by Substitution

Class 12 Mathematics: Indefinite Integrals

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 The Concept of Anti-Derivative (Primitive) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In differential calculus, given a function $F(x)$, we compute its derivative $F'(x) = f(x)$. Indefinite integration is the inverse operation: given $f(x)$, find a function $F(x)$ such that:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\big[F(x)\big] = f(x) $$

  • Definition: A function $F(x)$ is called an anti-derivative (or primitive, or indefinite integral) of $f(x)$ on an interval $I$ if:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\big[F(x)\big] = f(x), \quad \forall x \in I $$

  • Indefinite Integral Notation:

$$ \int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C $$

  • $\int$: Integral sign (an elongated 'S' for summa).
  • $f(x)$: Integrand.
  • $x$: Variable of integration.
  • $dx$: Differential of $x$, indicating the variable of integration.
  • $C$: Constant of Integration (an arbitrary real number).
  • $F(x) + C$: The indefinite integral of $f(x)$.

1.2 Geometric Interpretation of $\int f(x)\,dx$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $y = F(x)$ be a primitive of $f(x)$. Then:

$$ y = F(x) + C $$

represents a one-parameter family of curves, where each value of $C \in \mathbb{R}$ corresponds to a distinct curve.

Geometric Invariants:

  1. Vertical Translation: For any two distinct constants $C_1$ and $C_2$, the curve $y = F(x) + C_2$ is obtained by shifting $y = F(x) + C_1$ vertically along the $y$-axis by $|C_2 - C_1|$ units.
  2. Parallel Tangents along any Line $x = x_0$: Take the vertical ordinate line $x = x_0$. The slope of the tangent to any curve in the family at $(x_0, y)$ is given by:

$$ \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x = x_0} = \left.\frac{d}{dx}[F(x) + C]\right|_{x = x_0} = F'(x_0) = f(x_0) $$

Because $f(x_0)$ depends solely on $x_0$ and is completely independent of $C$, the tangents to all curves of the family at their respective points of intersection with the line $x = x_0$ are mutually parallel.

1.3 Fundamental Algebraic Properties of Indefinite Integrals ⭐⭐⭐

Let $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ be integrable functions and $k \in \mathbb{R}$ be any scalar constant.

  1. Derivative of an Integral:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int f(x)\,dx\right] = f(x) $$

  1. Integral of a Derivative:

$$ \int \left[\frac{d}{dx}f(x)\right] dx = f(x) + C $$

  1. Homogeneity (Constant Multiple Rule):

$$ \int k f(x)\,dx = k \int f(x)\,dx \quad (k \neq 0) $$

  1. Additivity (Sum / Difference Rule):

$$ \int \big[f(x) \pm g(x)\big]\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx \pm \int g(x)\,dx $$

  1. Linearity Combination:

$$ \int \left[\sum_{i=1}^n k_i f_i(x)\right] dx = \sum_{i=1}^n k_i \int f_i(x)\,dx $$

1.4 Integration by Substitution: Theoretical Basis ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

When an integrand is not in standard elementary form, we introduce a new independent variable $t$ through a differentiable transformation $x = g(t)$ or $t = \phi(x)$.

This technique directly inverts the Chain Rule of Differentiation:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\Big[F(\phi(x))\Big] = F'(\phi(x))\cdot \phi'(x) = f(\phi(x))\phi'(x) $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: The Principle of Indefinite Integrals Differing by a Constant

Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

If $F(x)$ and $G(x)$ are two anti-derivatives of the same function $f(x)$ defined on an open interval $I$, then $F(x)$ and $G(x)$ differ by an arbitrary constant; that is, $F(x) - G(x) = C$.

Formal Proof

  • Given:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\big[F(x)\big] = f(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{d}{dx}\big[G(x)\big] = f(x), \quad \forall x \in I $$

  • To Prove:

$$ F(x) - G(x) = C \quad (\text{where } C \text{ is a constant}) $$

  • Construction / Auxiliary Function: Define a new function $H(x)$ on $I$ by:

$$ H(x) = F(x) - G(x) $$

  • Step-by-Step Derivation:
  • Differentiate $H(x)$ with respect to $x$ using the linearity of derivatives:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\big[H(x)\big] = \frac{d}{dx}\big[F(x) - G(x)\big] = \frac{d}{dx}\big[F(x)\big] - \frac{d}{dx}\big[G(x)\big] $$

  1. Substitute the given hypotheses:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\big[H(x)\big] = f(x) - f(x) = 0, \quad \forall x \in I $$

  1. By the Mean Value Theorem (or its corollary: a function with zero derivative everywhere on an interval is constant): Let $x_1, x_2 \in I$ with $x_1 < x_2$. By Lagrange's Mean Value Theorem, there exists $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ such that:

$$ \frac{H(x_2) - H(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} = H'(c) $$

 Since $H'(c) = 0$, we have:

$$ H(x_2) - H(x_1) = 0 \implies H(x_2) = H(x_1) $$

  1. Hence, $H(x)$ is a constant function on $I$. Let $H(x) = C$.

$$ F(x) - G(x) = C \implies F(x) = G(x) + C $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: The Substitution Theorem for Indefinite Integrals

Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Let $I$ and $J$ be intervals in $\mathbb{R}$. If $\phi: J \to I$ is a differentiable function with continuous derivative $\phi'(t)$, and $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ has an anti-derivative $F$, then for $x = \phi(t)$:

$$ \int f(x)\,dx = \int f(\phi(t))\cdot \phi'(t)\,dt $$

Formal Proof

  • Given:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\big[F(x)\big] = f(x) $$

and $x = \phi(t)$, where $\phi(t)$ is differentiable with respect to $t$. - To Prove:

$$ \int f(x)\,dx = \int f(\phi(t))\phi'(t)\,dt $$

  • Step-by-Step Derivation:
  • Consider the composite function $w(t) = F(\phi(t))$.
  • By the Chain Rule of differential calculus:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\big[F(\phi(t))\big] = F'(\phi(t))\cdot \frac{d}{dt}[\phi(t)] $$

  1. Since $F'(u) = f(u)$, setting $u = \phi(t)$ gives:

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\big[F(\phi(t))\big] = f(\phi(t))\cdot \phi'(t) $$

  1. By the definition of the indefinite integral (anti-derivative):

$$ \int f(\phi(t))\phi'(t)\,dt = F(\phi(t)) + C $$

  1. But since $F(x) = \int f(x)\,dx$, and $x = \phi(t)$:

$$ F(\phi(t)) + C = \left[\int f(x)\,dx\right]_{x = \phi(t)} $$

  1. Expressed in differential notation, $dx = \phi'(t)\,dt$, yielding:

$$ \int f(x)\,dx = \int f(\phi(t))\phi'(t)\,dt $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 3: Core General Substitution Forms

Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Form (A): $\int [f(x)]^n f'(x)\,dx$ where $n \neq -1$

  • Substitution: Let $t = f(x) \implies dt = f'(x)\,dx$.
  • Derivation:

$$ \int [f(x)]^n f'(x)\,dx = \int t^n\,dt = \frac{t^{n+1}}{n+1} + C = \frac{[f(x)]^{n+1}}{n+1} + C $$

Form (B): The Logarithmic Derivative Form $\int \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx$

  • Substitution: Let $t = f(x) \implies dt = f'(x)\,dx$.
  • Derivation:

$$ \int \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx = \int \frac{1}{t}\,dt = \ln|t| + C = \ln|f(x)| + C $$

Theorem 4: Rigorous Derivation of Standard Trigonometric Integrals

Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

(i) Derivation of $\int \tan x\,dx = \ln|\sec x| + C$

$$ \int \tan x\,dx = \int \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx $$

  • Let $t = \cos x \implies dt = -\sin x\,dx \implies \sin x\,dx = -dt$.

$$ \int \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx = \int \frac{-dt}{t} = -\ln|t| + C_1 = -\ln|\cos x| + C_1 $$

Using logarithm laws: $-\ln|\cos x| = \ln\left|\frac{1}{\cos x}\right| = \ln|\sec x|$.

$$ \int \tan x\,dx = \ln|\sec x| + C \quad \text{or} \quad -\ln|\cos x| + C $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

(ii) Derivation of $\int \cot x\,dx = \ln|\sin x| + C$

$$ \int \cot x\,dx = \int \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\,dx $$

  • Let $t = \sin x \implies dt = \cos x\,dx$.

$$ \int \frac{\cos x}{\sin x}\,dx = \int \frac{dt}{t} = \ln|t| + C = \ln|\sin x| + C $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

(iii) Derivation of $\int \sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C$

Multiply and divide numerator and denominator by $(\sec x + \tan x)$:

$$ \int \sec x\,dx = \int \frac{\sec x(\sec x + \tan x)}{\sec x + \tan x}\,dx = \int \frac{\sec^2 x + \sec x \tan x}{\sec x + \tan x}\,dx $$

  • Let $t = \sec x + \tan x$.
  • Differentiating with respect to $x$:

$$ dt = (\sec x \tan x + \sec^2 x)\,dx $$

Substitute into the integral:

$$ \int \frac{dt}{t} = \ln|t| + C = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + C $$

Alternative Half-Angle Form:

$$ \sec x + \tan x = \frac{1 + \sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{\left(\cos\frac{x}{2} + \sin\frac{x}{2}\right)^2}{\cos^2\frac{x}{2} - \sin^2\frac{x}{2}} = \frac{\cos\frac{x}{2} + \sin\frac{x}{2}}{\cos\frac{x}{2} - \sin\frac{x}{2}} = \frac{1 + \tan\frac{x}{2}}{1 - \tan\frac{x}{2}} = \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{x}{2}\right) $$

$$ \therefore \int \sec x\,dx = \ln\left|\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{x}{2}\right)\right| + C $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

(iv) Derivation of $\int \csc x\,dx = \ln|\csc x - \cot x| + C$

Multiply and divide numerator and denominator by $(\csc x - \cot x)$:

$$ \int \csc x\,dx = \int \frac{\csc x(\csc x - \cot x)}{\csc x - \cot x}\,dx = \int \frac{\csc^2 x - \csc x \cot x}{\csc x - \cot x}\,dx $$

  • Let $t = \csc x - \cot x$.
  • Differentiating with respect to $x$:

$$ dt = \big(-\csc x \cot x - (-\csc^2 x)\big)\,dx = (\csc^2 x - \csc x \cot x)\,dx $$

Substitute into the integral:

$$ \int \frac{dt}{t} = \ln|t| + C = \ln|\csc x - \cot x| + C $$

Alternative Half-Angle Form:

$$ \csc x - \cot x = \frac{1 - \cos x}{\sin x} = \frac{2\sin^2(x/2)}{2\sin(x/2)\cos(x/2)} = \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

$$ \therefore \int \csc x\,dx = \ln\left|\tan\frac{x}{2}\right| + C $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Class 12 Mathematics: Integrals (Teaching Master Note)

Unit: Calculus

Target Audience: Class 12 CBSE Board & Competitive Aspirants

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Intuition & Star Priority

1.1 Integration Using Trigonometric Identities (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Core Concept & Intuition

Standard elementary integration techniques fail when encountering products or powers of trigonometric functions like $\sin^2 x$, $\cos^3 x$, or $\sin mx \cos nx$ because the integral operator is linear over addition, not over multiplication:

$$ \int [f(x) \cdot g(x)]\,dx \neq \int f(x)\,dx \cdot \int g(x)\,dx $$

The objective of this method is linearization: decomposing nonlinear powers ($\sin^n x, \cos^n x$) and products ($\sin mx \cos nx$) into first-degree sums and differences of cosines and sines using classical trigonometric identities. Once linearized, standard antiderivatives can be applied directly.

Essential Trigonometric Identities Required

  1. Power-Reduction (Half-Angle) Formulas:

$$ \sin^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos 2x}{2}, \quad \cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2} $$

  1. Triple-Angle Decompositions:

$$ \sin 3x = 3\sin x - 4\sin^3 x \implies \sin^3 x = \frac{3\sin x - \sin 3x}{4} $$

$$ \cos 3x = 4\cos^3 x - 3\cos x \implies \cos^3 x = \frac{3\cos x + \cos 3x}{4} $$

  1. Product-to-Sum Formulas (AB Formulas):

$$ 2\sin A \cos B = \sin(A+B) + \sin(A-B) $$

$$ 2\cos A \sin B = \sin(A+B) - \sin(A-B) $$

$$ 2\cos A \cos B = \cos(A+B) + \cos(A-B) $$

$$ 2\sin A \sin B = \cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B) $$

  1. Higher Tangent/Secant Relations:

$$ \tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1, \quad \cot^2 x = \csc^2 x - 1 $$

1.2 Standard Integrals of Particular Functions (Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Core Framework

When integrating rational or irrational algebraic fractions where the denominator is a quadratic expression $ax^2 + bx + c$ or its radical $\sqrt{ax^2 + bx + c}$, the function cannot be directly integrated using ordinary polynomial rules. By completing the square, every quadratic expression can be reduced to one of three fundamental forms:

$$ x^2 - a^2, \quad x^2 + a^2, \quad a^2 - x^2 $$

The Canonical Set of 6 Rational/Radical Integrals

  1. $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a} \ln \left| \frac{x-a}{x+a} \right| + C$ (for $|x| > |a|$)
  2. $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{a^2 - x^2} = \frac{1}{2a} \ln \left| \frac{a+x}{a-x} \right| + C$ (for $|x| < |a|$)
  3. $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{x^2 + a^2} = \frac{1}{a} \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C$
  4. $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}} = \ln \left| x + \sqrt{x^2 - a^2} \right| + C$
  5. $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}} = \ln \left| x + \sqrt{x^2 + a^2} \right| + C$
  6. $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C$

2. Rigorous Step-by-Step Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 2.1: $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a} \ln \left| \frac{x-a}{x+a} \right| + C$

  • Given: Integrand $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2 - a^2}$, with $x^2 \neq a^2$.
  • To Prove: $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a} \ln \left| \frac{x-a}{x+a} \right| + C$.
  • Algebraic Derivation (Partial Fractions): Factor the denominator into linear factors:

$$ x^2 - a^2 = (x-a)(x+a) $$

Decompose the numerator using identity manipulation:

$$ \frac{1}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{(x-a)(x+a)} = \frac{1}{2a} \left[ \frac{(x+a) - (x-a)}{(x-a)(x+a)} \right] $$

Split the fraction:

$$ \frac{1}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a} \left[ \frac{1}{x-a} - \frac{1}{x+a} \right] $$

Apply the linearity of integration:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a} \left[ \int \frac{dx}{x-a} - \int \frac{dx}{x+a} \right] $$

Recall that $\int \frac{1}{u}\,du = \ln|u| + C_0$:

$$ = \frac{1}{2a} \left[ \ln|x-a| - \ln|x+a| \right] + C $$

Using logarithm properties $\ln|u| - \ln|v| = \ln\left|\frac{u}{v}\right|$:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a} \ln \left| \frac{x-a}{x+a} \right| + C \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 2.2: $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{x^2 + a^2} = \frac{1}{a} \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C$

  • Given: Integrand $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2 + a^2}$.
  • To Prove: $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{x^2 + a^2} = \frac{1}{a} \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C$.
  • Proof by Trigonometric Substitution: Let $x = a\tan\theta \implies \theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)$.
    Differentiating both sides with respect to $\theta$:

$$ dx = a\sec^2 \theta \, d\theta $$

Substitute into the integral:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{x^2 + a^2} = \int \frac{a\sec^2\theta \, d\theta}{a^2\tan^2\theta + a^2} = \int \frac{a\sec^2\theta}{a^2(\tan^2\theta + 1)} \, d\theta $$

Using fundamental Pythagorean identity $1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta$:

$$ = \int \frac{a\sec^2\theta}{a^2\sec^2\theta} \, d\theta = \frac{1}{a} \int 1 \, d\theta = \frac{1}{a}\theta + C $$

Back-substituting $\theta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)$:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{x^2 + a^2} = \frac{1}{a} \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 2.3: $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}} = \ln \left| x + \sqrt{x^2 + a^2} \right| + C$

  • Given: Integrand $f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}}$.
  • To Prove: $\displaystyle \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}} = \ln \left| x + \sqrt{x^2 + a^2} \right| + C$.
  • Proof: Let $x = a\tan\theta \implies dx = a\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$.
    The radical transforms as:

$$ \sqrt{x^2 + a^2} = \sqrt{a^2\tan^2\theta + a^2} = a\sqrt{\tan^2\theta + 1} = a\sec\theta $$

Substituting into the integral:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}} = \int \frac{a\sec^2\theta \, d\theta}{a\sec\theta} = \int \sec\theta \, d\theta $$

Multiply numerator and denominator by $(\sec\theta + \tan\theta)$:

$$ \int \sec\theta \, d\theta = \int \frac{\sec\theta(\sec\theta + \tan\theta)}{\sec\theta + \tan\theta} \, d\theta = \ln|\sec\theta + \tan\theta| + C_1 $$

From the triangle geometry of substitution:

$$ \tan\theta = \frac{x}{a}, \quad \sec\theta = \sqrt{1 + \tan^2\theta} = \frac{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}}{a} $$

Substituting back:

$$ = \ln \left| \frac{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}}{a} + \frac{x}{a} \right| + C_1 = \ln \left| \frac{x + \sqrt{x^2 + a^2}}{a} \right| + C_1 $$

$$ = \ln \left| x + \sqrt{x^2 + a^2} \right| - \ln a + C_1 $$

Absorb the constant $-\ln a + C_1 = C$:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + a^2}} = \ln \left| x + \sqrt{x^2 + a^2} \right| + C \quad \blacksquare $$

Integration by Partial Fractions and Rational Algebraic Functions

Integration by Partial Fractions & Rational Algebraic Functions

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Rational Function and Its Classification (NCERT Framework)

A function of the form:

$$ f(x) = \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

where $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are polynomials in $x$ with real coefficients and $Q(x) \neq 0$, is called a rational algebraic function.

Classification by Polynomial Degree:

  1. Proper Rational Function $\left(\deg(P) < \deg(Q)\right)$: The degree of numerator polynomial $P(x)$ is strictly less than the degree of denominator polynomial $Q(x)$.

  2. Examples: $\dfrac{2x + 1}{x^2 + 5x + 6}$, $\dfrac{1}{x(x^2 + 1)}$.

  3. Improper Rational Function $\left(\deg(P) \ge \deg(Q)\right)$: The degree of numerator polynomial $P(x)$ is greater than or equal to the degree of denominator polynomial $Q(x)$.

  4. Examples: $\dfrac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 5x + 6}$, $\dfrac{x^3 + x}{x - 1}$.

[!IMPORTANT] Fundamental Directive for Evaluation: A rational function cannot be resolved into partial fractions directly if it is improper. You must first perform polynomial long division to express it as:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} = T(x) + \frac{R(x)}{Q(x)} $$

where $T(x)$ is the polynomial quotient and $R(x)$ is the remainder polynomial such that $\deg(R) < \deg(Q)$. The proper rational fraction $\frac{R(x)}{Q(x)}$ is then split into partial fractions.

1.2 Mathematical Intuition Behind Partial Fractions

In basic algebra, two or more elementary algebraic fractions are combined into a single compound fraction by finding their common denominator:

$$ \frac{2}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{x + 2} = \frac{2(x + 2) - 1(x - 1)}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{x + 5}{x^2 + x - 2} $$

Integration reverses this process. Integrating $\dfrac{x+5}{x^2 + x - 2}$ directly requires non-obvious manipulations, whereas integrating $\dfrac{2}{x - 1} - \dfrac{1}{x + 2}$ is immediate:

$$ \int \frac{x+5}{x^2+x-2}\,dx = 2\ln|x-1| - \ln|x+2| + C $$

Hence, Partial Fraction Decomposition is an invertible linear projection from the space of compound rational fractions onto a direct sum of elementary rational function subspaces whose primitives are standard logarithmic, rational, or inverse trigonometric forms.

Proof 2.1: Fundamental Integral of $\frac{1}{x^2 - a^2}$

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{dx}{x^2 - a^2}, \quad |x| \neq a $$

To Prove:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\frac{x - a}{x + a}\right| + C $$

Step-by-Step Derivation:

Factorize the denominator using difference of squares:

$$ x^2 - a^2 = (x - a)(x + a) $$

Express the integrand as a proper rational fraction:

$$ \frac{1}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{(x - a)(x + a)} $$

Set up partial fraction decomposition:

$$ \frac{1}{(x - a)(x + a)} = \frac{A}{x - a} + \frac{B}{x + a} $$

Clearing denominators:

$$ 1 = A(x + a) + B(x - a) $$

To find $A$ and $B$, evaluate at roots: - Set $x = a$:

$$ 1 = A(a + a) + B(0) \implies 1 = 2aA \implies A = \frac{1}{2a} $$

  • Set $x = -a$:

$$ 1 = A(0) + B(-a - a) \implies 1 = -2aB \implies B = -\frac{1}{2a} $$

Substitute $A$ and $B$ back into the integrand:

$$ \frac{1}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a}\left[\frac{1}{x - a} - \frac{1}{x + a}\right] $$

Now, integrating both sides with respect to $x$:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{dx}{x^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{2a}\left[\int \frac{dx}{x - a} - \int \frac{dx}{x + a}\right] $$

$$ = \frac{1}{2a} \left[ \ln|x - a| - \ln|x + a| \right] + C $$

Applying logarithm quotient law $\ln m - \ln n = \ln\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)$:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \frac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\frac{x - a}{x + a}\right| + C $$

Q.E.D.

Proof 2.2: Fundamental Integral of $\frac{1}{a^2 - x^2}$

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given:

$$ \mathcal{J} = \int \frac{dx}{a^2 - x^2}, \quad |x| \neq a $$

To Prove:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{a^2 - x^2} = \frac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\frac{a + x}{a - x}\right| + C $$

Step-by-Step Derivation:

Factor the denominator:

$$ a^2 - x^2 = (a - x)(a + x) $$

Set up decomposition:

$$ \frac{1}{(a - x)(a + x)} = \frac{A}{a - x} + \frac{B}{a + x} $$

$$ 1 = A(a + x) + B(a - x) $$

Evaluating at critical points: - Set $x = a$:

$$ 1 = A(2a) + 0 \implies A = \frac{1}{2a} $$

  • Set $x = -a$:

$$ 1 = 0 + B(2a) \implies B = \frac{1}{2a} $$

Substituting into the integral:

$$ \mathcal{J} = \frac{1}{2a}\int \frac{dx}{a - x} + \frac{1}{2a}\int \frac{dx}{a + x} $$

Recall that $\int \frac{dx}{a - x} = -\ln|a - x|$ due to the chain rule derivative of $(-x)$:

$$ \mathcal{J} = \frac{1}{2a} \left[ -\ln|a - x| + \ln|a + x| \right] + C $$

$$ \mathcal{J} = \frac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\frac{a + x}{a - x}\right| + C $$

Q.E.D.

Proof 2.3: General Linear-Over-Irreducible Quadratic Decomposition

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given:

$$ \mathcal{K} = \int \frac{Ax + B}{x^2 + px + q}\,dx \quad \text{where } p^2 - 4q < 0 $$

To Prove:

$$ \mathcal{K} = \frac{A}{2}\ln|x^2 + px + q| + \frac{2B - Ap}{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2x + p}{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}\right) + C $$

Step-by-Step Derivation:

Express the numerator as a linear combination of the derivative of the denominator and a constant:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(x^2 + px + q) = 2x + p $$

Set:

$$ Ax + B = \lambda(2x + p) + \mu = 2\lambda x + (\lambda p + \mu) $$

Equating coefficients: - Coefficient of $x$: $2\lambda = A \implies \lambda = \frac{A}{2}$ - Constant term: $\lambda p + \mu = B \implies \mu = B - \frac{Ap}{2} = \frac{2B - Ap}{2}$

Split the original integral into two sub-integrals:

$$ \mathcal{K} = \lambda \int \frac{2x + p}{x^2 + px + q}\,dx + \mu \int \frac{dx}{x^2 + px + q} = \lambda \mathcal{K}_1 + \mu \mathcal{K}_2 $$

Evaluating $\mathcal{K}_1$: Let $t = x^2 + px + q \implies dt = (2x + p)\,dx$:

$$ \mathcal{K}_1 = \int \frac{dt}{t} = \ln|t| = \ln|x^2 + px + q| $$

Evaluating $\mathcal{K}_2$: Complete the square on the quadratic denominator:

$$ x^2 + px + q = \left(x + \frac{p}{2}\right)^2 + \left(q - \frac{p^2}{4}\right) = \left(x + \frac{p}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}{2}\right)^2 $$

Let $k = \frac{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}{2}$. Standard form:

$$ \mathcal{K}_2 = \int \frac{dx}{\left(x + \frac{p}{2}\right)^2 + k^2} = \frac{1}{k}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x + \frac{p}{2}}{k}\right) $$

$$ = \frac{2}{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2x + p}{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}\right) $$

Combine $\lambda \mathcal{K}_1 + \mu \mathcal{K}_2$:

$$ \mathcal{K} = \frac{A}{2}\ln|x^2 + px + q| + \left(\frac{2B - Ap}{2}\right)\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}\right)\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2x + p}{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}\right) + C $$

$$ = \frac{A}{2}\ln|x^2 + px + q| + \frac{2B - Ap}{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2x + p}{\sqrt{4q - p^2}}\right) + C $$

Q.E.D.

1. Pedagogical Foundations & Mathematical Theory

Integration by Parts is the integral calculus counterpart of the differential calculus Product Rule. When an integrand presents itself as a product of two distinct families of functions where substitution fails, integration by parts is the primary analytical method.

1.1 Theorem 1: Integration by Parts Formula

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Highest Board Frequency)

NCERT Statement

Let $u$ and $v$ be two differentiable functions of a single real variable $x$ defined on a common interval $I$. Then, the integral of the product of these two functions is given by:

$$ \int u(x) \cdot v(x) \, dx = u(x) \int v(x) \, dx - \int \left[ \frac{d}{dx}[u(x)] \cdot \left(\int v(x) \, dx\right) \right] dx $$

In classical classroom nomenclature: - $u(x)$ is designated as the First Function ($f(x)$ or $\text{I}$). - $v(x)$ is designated as the Second Function ($g(x)$ or $\text{II}$).

$$ \int (\text{I} \cdot \text{II}) \, dx = \text{I} \int \text{II} \, dx - \int \left[ \frac{d}{dx}(\text{I}) \cdot \int \text{II} \, dx \right] dx $$

1.2 Geometric & Analytical Intuition

Differential Form & Area Interpretation

Consider the differential identity for two variables $u$ and $v$:

$$ d(uv) = u \, dv + v \, du $$

Integrating both sides across appropriate limits from $(u_1, v_1)$ to $(u_2, v_2)$:

$$ \int_{(u_1, v_1)}^{(u_2, v_2)} d(uv) = \int_{v_1}^{v_2} u \, dv + \int_{u_1}^{u_2} v \, du $$

$$ [uv]_{(u_1, v_1)}^{(u_2, v_2)} = \int_{v_1}^{v_2} u \, dv + \int_{u_1}^{u_2} v \, du $$

Rearranging gives the standard differential formulation:

$$ \int u \, dv = uv - \int v \, du $$

Geometric Meaning: In the $u$-$v$ coordinate plane, the total area of the bounding rectangle formed by the coordinates $(u_2, v_2)$ relative to $(u_1, v_1)$ is partitioned into two complementary orthogonal regions: 1. $\int u \, dv$ represents the area integrated along the vertical axis ($v$). 2. $\int v \, du$ represents the area integrated along the horizontal axis ($u$).

The sum of these two areas equals the difference of the corner rectangular areas: $\Delta(uv) = u_2 v_2 - u_1 v_1$.

1.3 The Selection Hierarchy: The ILATE Rule

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Integration by Parts succeeds or fails based on the choice of the First Function $\text{I}$. The second function $\text{II}$ must be readily integrable, while the first function $\text{I}$ should simplify upon differentiation.

The acronym ILATE establishes the decreasing order of priority for choosing the First Function ($u$):

Letter Function Category Standard Representatives Typical Derivatives $\frac{d}{dx}[u]$
I Inverse Trigonometric $\sin^{-1} x, \cos^{-1} x, \tan^{-1} x, \sec^{-1} x$ Algebraic fractions: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}, \frac{1}{1+x^2}$
L Logarithmic $\ln x, \log_{10} x, \log_a(bx+c)$ Rational forms: $\frac{1}{x}$
A Algebraic $x^n, ax+b, \sqrt{x}, x^2+3x+1$ Degree-reduced polynomials: $n x^{n-1}$
T Trigonometric $\sin x, \cos x, \sec^2 x, \csc x \cot x$ Alternating trigonometric forms
E Exponential $e^x, a^x, e^{kx}$ Invariant/scaled exponentials: $k e^{kx}$

Key Heuristics

  1. The "Single Function" Exception: When integrating solitary Inverse Trigonometric or Logarithmic functions (e.g., $\int \ln x \, dx$, $\int \tan^{-1} x \, dx$), introduce the algebraic identity factor $\mathbf{1} = x^0$ as the second function.
  2. Order Matters: Functions appearing earlier in ILATE are differentiated ($u$); functions appearing later are integrated ($dv$).

2. Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

2.1 Formal Proof of Integration by Parts

Statement

$$ \int u(x) v(x) \, dx = u(x) \int v(x) \, dx - \int \left( \frac{du}{dx} \int v(x) \, dx \right) dx $$

Direct Proof

Step 1: Let $u(x)$ and $w(x)$ be two continuously differentiable functions of $x$. By the Product Rule of Differential Calculus:

$$ \frac{d}{dx} [u(x) \cdot w(x)] = u(x) \frac{dw}{dx} + w(x) \frac{du}{dx} $$

Step 2: Integrate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$ \int \frac{d}{dx} [u(x) \cdot w(x)] \, dx = \int \left( u(x) \frac{dw}{dx} \right) dx + \int \left( w(x) \frac{du}{dx} \right) dx $$

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (indefinite case):

$$ u(x) \cdot w(x) = \int \left( u(x) \frac{dw}{dx} \right) dx + \int \left( w(x) \frac{du}{dx} \right) dx $$

Step 3: Rearrange the equality to isolate $\int \left( u(x) \frac{dw}{dx} \right) dx$:

$$ \int \left( u(x) \frac{dw}{dx} \right) dx = u(x) \cdot w(x) - \int \left( \frac{du}{dx} \cdot w(x) \right) dx $$

Step 4: Define $v(x) = \frac{dw}{dx}$. Integrating both sides with respect to $x$ gives:

$$ w(x) = \int v(x) \, dx $$

(The constant of integration can be omitted here without loss of generality, as it cancels out later in the derivation).

Step 5: Substitute $w(x)$ and $\frac{dw}{dx}$ back into the equation:

$$ \int u(x) v(x) \, dx = u(x) \left( \int v(x) \, dx \right) - \int \left[ \frac{du}{dx} \cdot \left( \int v(x) \, dx \right) \right] dx $$

$$ \blacksquare \quad \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.2 Proof of the Exponential Integral $\int e^x [f(x) + f'(x)] \, dx$

Statement

To prove:

$$ \int e^x \left[ f(x) + f'(x) \right] dx = e^x f(x) + C $$

Direct Proof

Step 1: Expand the integral into two distinct linear terms:

$$ I = \int e^x f(x) \, dx + \int e^x f'(x) \, dx $$

Step 2: Apply Integration by Parts only to the first integral, $I_1 = \int e^x f(x) \, dx$: - First function: $u(x) = f(x)$ - Second function: $v(x) = e^x$

Using the formula:

$$ I_1 = f(x) \int e^x \, dx - \int \left( \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)] \int e^x \, dx \right) dx $$

Since $\int e^x \, dx = e^x$:

$$ I_1 = f(x) e^x - \int f'(x) e^x \, dx $$

Step 3: Substitute $I_1$ back into the complete expression for $I$:

$$ I = \left( e^x f(x) - \int e^x f'(x) \, dx \right) + \int e^x f'(x) \, dx $$

Step 4: The integral $\int e^x f'(x) \, dx$ cancels with $-\int e^x f'(x) \, dx$:

$$ I = e^x f(x) + C $$

$$ \blacksquare \quad \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.3 Proof of Standard Special Integrals Using Integration by Parts

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem: Derivation of $\int \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} \, dx$

$$ \int \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} \, dx = \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} + \frac{a^2}{2} \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C $$

Direct Proof

Step 1: Write the integrand as a product with $1$:

$$ I = \int \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} \cdot 1 \, dx $$

Assign functions via ILATE: - $\text{I} = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$ - $\text{II} = 1$

Step 2: Apply Integration by Parts:

$$ I = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} \cdot x - \int \left( \frac{d}{dx}\left[\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\right] \cdot x \right) dx $$

Calculate the derivative:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\right] = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \cdot (-2x) = \frac{-x}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} $$

Substitute back:

$$ I = x\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} - \int \frac{-x^2}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx $$

$$ I = x\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} - \int \frac{(a^2 - x^2) - a^2}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx $$

Step 3: Split the integral:

$$ I = x\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} - \left[ \int \frac{a^2 - x^2}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx - a^2 \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx \right] $$

$$ I = x\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} - \int \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} \, dx + a^2 \int \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}} \, dx $$

Step 4: Recognize $I = \int \sqrt{a^2 - x^2} \, dx$:

$$ I = x\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} - I + a^2 \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) $$

Step 5: Solve algebraically for $I$:

$$ 2I = x\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} + a^2 \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) $$

$$ I = \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{a^2 - x^2} + \frac{a^2}{2}\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + C $$

$$ \blacksquare \quad \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Definite Integrals & Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Comprehensive Master Teaching Note: Definite Integrals & Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

1. Pedagogical Theory, Mathematical Rigour & Geometric Intuition

1.1 Conceptual Definition of a Definite Integral (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $f$ be a continuous real-valued function defined on a closed, bounded interval $[a, b]$, where $a \le b$.

The definite integral of $f$ from $a$ to $b$ is denoted by:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx $$

where: * $a$ is the lower limit of integration. * $b$ is the upper limit of integration. * $[a, b]$ is the interval of integration. * $f(x)$ is the integrand. * $x$ is the dummy variable (i.e., $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(t) \, dt = \int_{a}^{b} f(u) \, du$).

Unlike an indefinite integral—which represents an infinite family of antiderivatives parametrized by an arbitrary constant of integration $C$—a definite integral evaluates to a unique real number.

1.2 Geometric Interpretation & Signed Area (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

  1. Non-negative Integrand ($f(x) \ge 0$ for all $x \in [a, b]$): The definite integral $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$ denotes the exact measure of the planar region bounded by:
  2. The curve $y = f(x)$,
  3. The $x$-axis ($y = 0$),
  4. The vertical ordinates $x = a$ and $x = b$.

  1. General Continuous Integrand (Sign-changing on $[a, b]$): The definite integral represents the net signed area:

To compute the geometric (total physical) area, we integrate the absolute value:

$$ \text{Total Area} = \int_{a}^{b} |f(x)| \, dx = \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx + \int_{c}^{b} (-f(x)) \, dx $$

where $c \in (a, b)$ is a root such that $f(x) \le 0$ on $[c, b]$.

1.3 Area Function (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $f$ be a continuous function defined on $[a, b]$. For any $x \in [a, b]$, the definite integral $\int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt$ defines a function of the upper limit $x$.

We define the Area Function $A(x)$ by:

$$ A(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt \quad \text{for all } x \in [a, b] $$

  • At $x = a$: $A(a) = \int_{a}^{a} f(t) \, dt = 0$.
  • At $x = b$: $A(b) = \int_{a}^{b} f(t) \, dt$.

1.4 The First Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC-1) (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Formal Statement (NCERT):

Let $f$ be a continuous function on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and let $A(x)$ be the area function defined by:

$$ A(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt \quad \forall \, x \in [a, b] $$

Then, $A(x)$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$ and continuous on $[a, b]$, and:

$$ A'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt\right] = f(x) \quad \forall \, x \in (a, b) $$

Pedagogical Insight: Differentiation and integration are inverse operations. Rate of change of accumulated area with respect to the right boundary equals the instantaneous height of the curve at that boundary.

1.5 The Second Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC-2) (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Formal Statement (NCERT):

Let $f$ be a continuous function defined on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and let $F$ be any antiderivative (primitive) of $f$ on $[a, b]$, such that $F'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$. Then:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \Big[ F(x) \Big]_{a}^{b} = F(b) - F(a) $$

Note on the Arbitrary Constant: If we pick an arbitrary primitive $F(x) + C$:

$$ \Big[ F(x) + C \Big]_{a}^{b} = \big(F(b) + C\big) - \big(F(a) + C\big) = F(b) - F(a) $$

The constant $C$ cancels identically. Hence, an explicit constant of integration is omitted when evaluating definite integrals.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: The First Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC-1)

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt\right] = f(x) $$

Given: * A function $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ continuous on $[a, b]$. * Area function $A(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt$ for $x \in [a, b]$.

To Prove:

$$ A'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{A(x+h) - A(x)}{h} = f(x) \quad \forall \, x \in (a, b) $$

Derivation: 1. Let $x \in (a, b)$ and choose an increment $h \ne 0$ such that $(x + h) \in [a, b]$. 2. By the additive property of intervals:

$$ A(x+h) = \int_{a}^{x+h} f(t) \, dt = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt + \int_{x}^{x+h} f(t) \, dt = A(x) + \int_{x}^{x+h} f(t) \, dt $$

  1. Therefore:

$$ A(x+h) - A(x) = \int_{x}^{x+h} f(t) \, dt $$

  1. Since $f$ is continuous on the closed subinterval $I_h$ with endpoints $x$ and $x+h$, by the Extreme Value Theorem, $f$ attains its absolute minimum value $m_h$ and absolute maximum value $M_h$ on $I_h$:

$$ m_h \le f(t) \le M_h \quad \forall \, t \in I_h $$

  1. For $h > 0$, integrate this inequality with respect to $t$ over $[x, x+h]$:

$$ \int_{x}^{x+h} m_h \, dt \le \int_{x}^{x+h} f(t) \, dt \le \int_{x}^{x+h} M_h \, dt $$

$$ m_h \cdot h \le A(x+h) - A(x) \le M_h \cdot h $$

  1. Dividing throughout by $h > 0$:

$$ m_h \le \frac{A(x+h) - A(x)}{h} \le M_h $$

(An identical inequality holds when $h < 0$, with inequalities flipping upon dividing by negative $h$ and re-orienting). 7. Take the limit as $h \to 0$. Because $f$ is continuous at $x$:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} m_h = f(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{h \to 0} M_h = f(x) $$

  1. Applying the Squeeze (Sandwich) Theorem:

$$ A'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{A(x+h) - A(x)}{h} = f(x) $$

Q.E.D.

Proof 2.2: The Second Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC-2)

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a) $$

Given: * $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$. * $F$ is an antiderivative of $f$ on $[a, b]$, meaning $F'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$.

To Prove:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a) $$

Derivation: 1. Define the area function $A(x) = \int_{a}^{x} f(t) \, dt$. 2. By FTC-1, $A'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$. 3. Since $F'(x) = f(x)$ as well, we have:

$$ A'(x) - F'(x) = 0 \implies \frac{d}{dx}\Big(A(x) - F(x)\Big) = 0 $$

  1. By the Mean Value Theorem deduction, any function whose derivative vanishes everywhere on an interval is constant. Therefore:

$$ A(x) - F(x) = C \implies A(x) = F(x) + C $$

for some real constant $C$. 5. Evaluate at $x = a$:

$$ A(a) = \int_{a}^{a} f(t) \, dt = 0 $$

$$ 0 = F(a) + C \implies C = -F(a) $$

  1. Substitute $C$ back into the relation:

$$ A(x) = F(x) - F(a) $$

  1. Now evaluate this identity at $x = b$:

$$ A(b) = \int_{a}^{b} f(t) \, dt = F(b) - F(a) $$

  1. Replacing the dummy variable $t$ with $x$ yields:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a) $$

Q.E.D.

Proof 2.3: Definite Integration by Substitution (Change of Variable Theorem)

Theorem: Let $g: [a, b] \to [c, d]$ have a continuous derivative $g'$ on $[a, b]$, and let $f$ be continuous on the range of $g$. If $u = g(x)$, then:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(g(x)) g'(x) \, dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u) \, du $$

Proof: 1. Let $F$ be an antiderivative of $f$, so that $F'(u) = f(u)$. 2. Define a composite function $\Phi(x) = F(g(x))$ on $[a, b]$. 3. By the Chain Rule of Differential Calculus:

$$ \Phi'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}[F(g(x))] = F'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) = f(g(x)) g'(x) $$

  1. By FTC-2, the definite integral of $\Phi'(x)$ over $[a, b]$ is:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(g(x)) g'(x) \, dx = \Big[ \Phi(x) \Big]_{a}^{b} = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a) $$

  1. Evaluating $\Phi$ at the boundaries:

$$ \Phi(b) - \Phi(a) = F(g(b)) - F(g(a)) $$

  1. But by FTC-2 applied directly to the function $f(u)$ over the interval $[g(a), g(b)]$:

$$ \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u) \, du = \Big[ F(u) \Big]_{g(a)}^{g(b)} = F(g(b)) - F(g(a)) $$

  1. Equating the two expressions:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(g(x)) g'(x) \, dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u) \, du $$

Q.E.D.

Proof 2.4: Definite Integration by Parts

Theorem: If $u(x)$ and $v(x)$ are continuously differentiable on $[a, b]$, then:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} u(x) v'(x) \, dx = \Big[ u(x) v(x) \Big]_{a}^{b} - \int_{a}^{b} v(x) u'(x) \, dx $$

Proof: 1. By the Product Rule of Differentiation:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[u(x) v(x)] = u(x) v'(x) + v(x) u'(x) $$

  1. Integrating both sides over $[a, b]$ with respect to $x$:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} \frac{d}{dx}[u(x) v(x)] \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} u(x) v'(x) \, dx + \int_{a}^{b} v(x) u'(x) \, dx $$

  1. By FTC-2, the left-hand side simplifies to:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} \frac{d}{dx}[u(x) v(x)] \, dx = \Big[ u(x) v(x) \Big]_{a}^{b} $$

  1. Rearranging terms:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} u(x) v'(x) \, dx = \Big[ u(x) v(x) \Big]_{a}^{b} - \int_{a}^{b} v(x) u'(x) \, dx $$

Q.E.D.

1. Pedagogical Foundation & Geometric Intuition

1.1 The Concept of a Definite Integral

Let $f(x)$ be a continuous real-valued function defined on a closed interval $[a, b]$.

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Part 2):

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = [F(x)]_a^b = F(b) - F(a) $$

where $F(x)$ is an anti-derivative (primitive) of $f(x)$, meaning $F'(x) = f(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$.

1.2 Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$ represents the net signed area bounded by the curve $y = f(x)$, the $x$-axis, and the vertical ordinates $x = a$ and $x = b$: - Regions where $f(x) \ge 0$ contribute positive area. - Regions where $f(x) \le 0$ contribute negative area.

When computing definite integrals, standard indefinite integration followed by evaluation at endpoints can become computationally unwieldy or impossible (e.g., non-elementary primitives like $\frac{\sin x}{x}$). The Properties of Definite Integrals exploit geometric symmetries, translations, and reflections to bypass anti-differentiation entirely or reduce complex problems to elementary algebra.

2. Exhaustive Properties, Proofs & Theoretical Derivations

In NCERT terminology, these standard properties are conventionally designated $P_0$ through $P_7$.

Property $P_0$: Dummy Variable Invariance ⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Changing the variable of integration does not affect the numerical value of the definite integral.

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(t) \, dt $$

Proof:

  • Given: $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$. Let $\int f(x) \, dx = F(x) + C$.
  • To Prove: $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(t) \, dt$

Step 1: Evaluate the Left-Hand Side (L.H.S.):

$$ \text{L.H.S.} = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = [F(x)]_a^b = F(b) - F(a) $$

Step 2: Evaluate the Right-Hand Side (R.H.S.):

$$ \text{R.H.S.} = \int_{a}^{b} f(t) \, dt = [F(t)]_a^b = F(b) - F(a) $$

Conclusion:

$$ \text{L.H.S.} = \text{R.H.S.} \quad \blacksquare $$

Property $P_1$: Reversal of Limits ⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Interchanging the upper and lower limits reverses the sign of the integral.

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = -\int_{b}^{a} f(x) \, dx $$

In particular, when $a = b$: $\int_{a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = 0$.

Proof:

  • Given: Let $F(x)$ be an anti-derivative of $f(x)$.
  • To Prove: $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = -\int_{b}^{a} f(x) \, dx$

Step 1: By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(a) $$

Step 2: Factor out $-1$:

$$ F(b) - F(a) = -[F(a) - F(b)] $$

Step 3: Express the bracketed quantity as an integral from $b$ to $a$:

$$ F(a) - F(b) = \int_{b}^{a} f(x) \, dx $$

Therefore:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = -\int_{b}^{a} f(x) \, dx \quad \blacksquare $$

Property $P_2$: Interval Additivity (Splitting Property) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: For any real numbers $a, b, c$ where $f$ is integrable on the encompassing interval:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx + \int_{c}^{b} f(x) \, dx $$

(Most commonly used when $c \in [a, b]$ where the integrand changes analytical definition, e.g., piecewise, absolute value, or greatest integer functions).

Proof:

  • Given: $F'(x) = f(x)$.
  • To Prove: $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx + \int_{c}^{b} f(x) \, dx$

Step 1: Evaluate the components on the R.H.S.:

$$ \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx = F(c) - F(a) $$

$$ \int_{c}^{b} f(x) \, dx = F(b) - F(c) $$

Step 2: Sum the two integrals:

$$ \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx + \int_{c}^{b} f(x) \, dx = [F(c) - F(a)] + [F(b) - F(c)] = F(b) - F(a) $$

Step 3: Identify with L.H.S.:

$$ F(b) - F(a) = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx $$

$$ \therefore \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{c} f(x) \, dx + \int_{c}^{b} f(x) \, dx \quad \blacksquare $$

Property $P_3$: General Interval Reflection ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(a + b - x) \, dx $$

Proof:

  • Given: $f$ is integrable on $[a, b]$.
  • To Prove: $\int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(a + b - x) \, dx$

Step 1: Consider the R.H.S. integral:

$$ I_{\text{RHS}} = \int_{a}^{b} f(a + b - x) \, dx $$

Step 2: Substitute:

$$ t = a + b - x \implies dt = -dx \implies dx = -dt $$

Step 3: Transform limits of integration: - When $x = a \implies t = a + b - a = b$ - When $x = b \implies t = a + b - b = a$

Step 4: Substitute variables and evaluate:

$$ I_{\text{RHS}} = \int_{b}^{a} f(t) (-dt) = -\int_{b}^{a} f(t) \, dt $$

Step 5: Apply $P_1$ (reverse limits) and $P_0$ (change dummy variable):

$$ -\int_{b}^{a} f(t) \, dt = \int_{a}^{b} f(t) \, dt = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \text{L.H.S.} $$

$$ \therefore \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{b} f(a + b - x) \, dx \quad \blacksquare $$

Property $P_4$: "King's Rule" (Special Case of $P_3$ where $a = 0$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

$$ \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(a - x) \, dx $$

Proof:

Follows directly from $P_3$ by setting lower limit $a \to 0$ and upper limit $b \to a$:

$$ t = a - x \implies dx = -dt $$

  • When $x = 0 \implies t = a$
  • When $x = a \implies t = 0$

$$ \int_{0}^{a} f(a - x) \, dx = \int_{a}^{0} f(t)(-dt) = \int_{0}^{a} f(t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx \quad \blacksquare $$

Property $P_5$: Interval Bisecting / Folding ⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

$$ \int_{0}^{2a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{a} f(2a - x) \, dx $$

Proof:

Step 1: By Property $P_2$, split the integral at $c = a$:

$$ \int_{0}^{2a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx + \int_{a}^{2a} f(x) \, dx \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Step 2: Let $I_2 = \int_{a}^{2a} f(x) \, dx$. Apply the substitution:

$$ t = 2a - x \implies x = 2a - t \implies dx = -dt $$

Step 3: Change limits for $I_2$: - When $x = a \implies t = 2a - a = a$ - When $x = 2a \implies t = 2a - 2a = 0$

Step 4: Simplify $I_2$:

$$ I_2 = \int_{a}^{0} f(2a - t)(-dt) = \int_{0}^{a} f(2a - t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{a} f(2a - x) \, dx \quad (\text{by } P_0) $$

Step 5: Substitute $I_2$ back into (1):

$$ \int_{0}^{2a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{a} f(2a - x) \, dx \quad \blacksquare $$

Property $P_6$: "Queen's Rule" ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

$$ \int_{0}^{2a} f(x) \, dx = \begin{cases} 2\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx, & \text{if } f(2a - x) = f(x) \\[12pt] 0, & \text{if } f(2a - x) = -f(x) \end{cases} $$

Proof:

From Property $P_5$:

$$ \int_{0}^{2a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{a} f(2a - x) \, dx $$

  • Case 1: If $f(2a - x) = f(x)$:

$$ \int_{0}^{2a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx = 2\int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx $$

  • Case 2: If $f(2a - x) = -f(x)$:

$$ \int_{0}^{2a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{a} [-f(x)] \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx - \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx = 0 \quad \blacksquare $$

Property $P_7$: Symmetric Interval Property (Even/Odd Functions) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:

$$ \int_{-a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \begin{cases} 2\displaystyle\int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx, & \text{if } f(x) \text{ is an even function, i.e., } f(-x) = f(x) \\[12pt] 0, & \text{if } f(x) \text{ is an odd function, i.e., } f(-x) = -f(x) \end{cases} $$

Proof:

Step 1: Split the integral at $0$ using Property $P_2$:

$$ \int_{-a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{-a}^{0} f(x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Step 2: In the first integral $I_1 = \int_{-a}^{0} f(x) \, dx$, substitute:

$$ t = -x \implies dx = -dt $$

  • When $x = -a \implies t = a$
  • When $x = 0 \implies t = 0$

$$ I_1 = \int_{a}^{0} f(-t)(-dt) = -\int_{a}^{0} f(-t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{a} f(-t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{a} f(-x) \, dx \quad (\text{by } P_0) $$

Step 3: Substitute back into (1):

$$ \int_{-a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(-x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} [f(x) + f(-x)] \, dx $$

  • Case 1: If $f$ is even ($f(-x) = f(x)$):

$$ \int_{-a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} [f(x) + f(x)] \, dx = 2\int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx $$

  • Case 2: If $f$ is odd ($f(-x) = -f(x)$):

$$ \int_{-a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} [f(x) - f(x)] \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} 0 \, dx = 0 \quad \blacksquare $$

3. High-Yield Examples & 5-Year Board PYQs (2020–2025)

Question 1: Piecewise & Absolute Value Evaluation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

[CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
Evaluate:

$$ I = \int_{-1}^{2} |x^3 - x| \, dx $$

Solution:

Step 1: Determine critical transition points.
Set the integrand's inner argument to zero:

$$ x^3 - x = 0 \implies x(x^2 - 1) = 0 \implies x(x - 1)(x + 1) = 0 \implies x = -1, 0, 1 $$

All three roots lie within or on the boundary of $[-1, 2]$.

Step 2: Sign analysis of $g(x) = x^3 - x$ across sub-intervals: 1. For $x \in (-1, 0)$: Test $x = -0.5 \implies (-0.5)^3 - (-0.5) = -0.125 + 0.5 = +0.375 > 0$.

$$ \therefore |x^3 - x| = x^3 - x $$

  1. For $x \in (0, 1)$: Test $x = 0.5 \implies (0.5)^3 - (0.5) = 0.125 - 0.5 = -0.375 < 0$.

$$ \therefore |x^3 - x| = -(x^3 - x) = x - x^3 $$

  1. For $x \in (1, 2)$: Test $x = 1.5 \implies (1.5)^3 - 1.5 = 3.375 - 1.5 = +1.875 > 0$.

$$ \therefore |x^3 - x| = x^3 - x $$

Step 3: Split the integral using Property $P_2$:

$$ I = \int_{-1}^{0} (x^3 - x) \, dx + \int_{0}^{1} (x - x^3) \, dx + \int_{1}^{2} (x^3 - x) \, dx $$

Step 4: Integrate each component:

$$ \int (x^3 - x) \, dx = \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{x^2}{2} \right] $$

  • Component 1:

$$ \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_{-1}^{0} = 0 - \left( \frac{(-1)^4}{4} - \frac{(-1)^2}{2} \right) = - \left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{2}\right) = -\left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{4} $$

  • Component 2:

$$ \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4} \right]_{0}^{1} = \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{4}\right) - 0 = \frac{1}{4} $$

  • Component 3:

$$ \left[ \frac{x^4}{4} - \frac{x^2}{2} \right]_{1}^{2} = \left(\frac{16}{4} - \frac{4}{2}\right) - \left(\frac{1}{4} - \frac{1}{2}\right) = (4 - 2) - \left(-\frac{1}{4}\right) = 2 + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{9}{4} $$

Step 5: Aggregate the partial areas:

$$ I = \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{9}{4} = \frac{11}{4} $$

Question 2: Standard Complementary Symmetry ($P_4$) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

[CBSE 2023, 3 Marks]
Evaluate:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{\sqrt{\sin x}}{\sqrt{\sin x} + \sqrt{\cos x}} \, dx \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Solution:

Step 1: Apply King's Rule ($P_4$):

$$ \int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(a - x) \, dx $$

Here $a = \frac{\pi}{2}$. Replace $x$ with $\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)$:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{\sqrt{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)}}{\sqrt{\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)} + \sqrt{\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)}} \, dx $$

Since $\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \cos x$ and $\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right) = \sin x$:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{\sqrt{\cos x}}{\sqrt{\cos x} + \sqrt{\sin x}} \, dx \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Step 2: Add equations (1) and (2):

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \frac{\sqrt{\sin x} + \sqrt{\cos x}}{\sqrt{\sin x} + \sqrt{\cos x}} \, dx $$

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} 1 \, dx $$

$$ 2I = [x]_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} = \frac{\pi}{2} - 0 = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{4} $$

Question 3: Elimination of Linear Factor $x$ via $P_4$ ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

[CBSE 2022 Term-2 / 2020, 5 Marks]
Evaluate:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{x \sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Solution:

Step 1: Apply Property $P_4$: Replace $x$ with $(\pi - x)$:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{(\pi - x) \sin(\pi - x)}{1 + \cos^2(\pi - x)} \, dx $$

Since $\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x$ and $\cos(\pi - x) = -\cos x \implies \cos^2(\pi - x) = \cos^2 x$:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{(\pi - x) \sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Step 2: Add equations (1) and (2):

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{x \sin x + (\pi - x)\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx $$

$$ 2I = \pi \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx $$

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx $$

Step 3: Evaluate by substitution: Let $u = \cos x \implies du = -\sin x \, dx \implies \sin x \, dx = -du$.
Limits: - $x = 0 \implies u = \cos 0 = 1$ - $x = \pi \implies u = \cos \pi = -1$

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_{1}^{-1} \frac{-du}{1 + u^2} = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{du}{1 + u^2} \quad (\text{by } P_1) $$

Step 4: Integrate:

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} [\tan^{-1}(u)]_{-1}^{1} = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \tan^{-1}(1) - \tan^{-1}(-1) \right] = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \frac{\pi}{4} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \right] = \frac{\pi}{2} \left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{\pi^2}{4} $$

Question 4: Logarithmic Trigonometric Integral (Classic Proof-cum-Evaluation) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

[CBSE 2025 Sample Paper / CBSE 2020 5M]
Prove that:

$$ \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin x) \, dx = -\frac{\pi}{2} \log 2 $$

Solution:

Let:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin x) \, dx \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Step 1: Apply Property $P_4$:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log\left(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)\right) \, dx = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\cos x) \, dx \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Step 2: Add (1) and (2):

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} [\log(\sin x) + \log(\cos x)] \, dx = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin x \cos x) \, dx $$

Multiply and divide the argument by $2$:

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log\left(\frac{2\sin x \cos x}{2}\right) \, dx = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin 2x) \, dx - \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log 2 \, dx $$

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin 2x) \, dx - (\log 2)[x]_0^{\frac{\pi}{2}} $$

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin 2x) \, dx - \frac{\pi}{2} \log 2 \quad \text{--- (3)} $$

Step 3: Evaluate $I_1 = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin 2x) \, dx$:
Substitute $2x = t \implies dx = \frac{dt}{2}$.
Limits: $x = 0 \implies t = 0$; $x = \frac{\pi}{2} \implies t = \pi$.

$$ I_1 = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} \log(\sin t) \, dt $$

Apply Property $P_6$ to $\int_{0}^{\pi} \log(\sin t) \, dt$:
Here $2a = \pi \implies a = \frac{\pi}{2}$.
Check condition: $f(2a - t) = \sin(\pi - t) = \sin t = f(t)$.

$$ \therefore \int_{0}^{\pi} \log(\sin t) \, dt = 2 \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin t) \, dt $$

Substitute this back into $I_1$:

$$ I_1 = \frac{1}{2} \left[ 2 \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin t) \, dt \right] = \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \log(\sin t) \, dt = I \quad (\text{by } P_0) $$

Step 4: Substitute $I_1 = I$ into (3):

$$ 2I = I - \frac{\pi}{2}\log 2 \implies I = -\frac{\pi}{2} \log 2 = \frac{\pi}{2} \log\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \quad \blacksquare $$

Question 5: Modulus & Interval Splitting ⭐⭐⭐⭐

[CBSE 2023, 2 Marks]
Evaluate:

$$ \int_{0}^{2} |x^2 + 2x - 3| \, dx $$

Solution:

Step 1: Factor the polynomial:

$$ x^2 + 2x - 3 = (x + 3)(x - 1) $$

Step 2: Sign determination within $[0, 2]$: - For $x \in [0, 1)$: $(x + 3) > 0$ and $(x - 1) < 0 \implies (x^2 + 2x - 3) \le 0$.

$$ |x^2 + 2x - 3| = -(x^2 + 2x - 3) = 3 - 2x - x^2 $$

  • For $x \in (1, 2]$: $(x + 3) > 0$ and $(x - 1) > 0 \implies (x^2 + 2x - 3) \ge 0$.

$$ |x^2 + 2x - 3| = x^2 + 2x - 3 $$

Step 3: Split the integral ($P_2$):

$$ I = \int_{0}^{1} (3 - 2x - x^2) \, dx + \int_{1}^{2} (x^2 + 2x - 3) \, dx $$

Step 4: Evaluate each part: - Part 1:

$$ \left[ 3x - x^2 - \frac{x^3}{3} \right]_0^1 = \left(3(1) - 1^2 - \frac{1}{3}\right) - 0 = 2 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{5}{3} $$

  • Part 2:

$$ \left[ \frac{x^3}{3} + x^2 - 3x \right]_1^2 = \left(\frac{8}{3} + 4 - 6\right) - \left(\frac{1}{3} + 1 - 3\right) = \left(\frac{8}{3} - 2\right) - \left(\frac{1}{3} - 2\right) = \frac{2}{3} - \left(-\frac{5}{3}\right) = \frac{7}{3} $$

Step 5: Total sum:

$$ I = \frac{5}{3} + \frac{7}{3} = \frac{12}{3} = 4 $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Pitfalls

Trap Scenario Error Committed by Students Correct Board Methodology
1. Forgetting $2I$ on L.H.S. Students add (1) and (2) after applying King's rule ($P_4$) and write $I = \int 1 \, dx = \frac{\pi}{2}$, forgetting to divide by 2. Explicitly write: $I + I = 2I$. Circle $2I$ in your draft to ensure you divide: $I = \frac{\text{R.H.S.}}{2}$.
2. Blindly assuming symmetry implies $0$ Seeing limits $[-a, a]$ and immediately writing answer $= 0$ without verifying whether $f(-x) = -f(x)$. Always test: compute $f(-x)$. If $f(-x) \ne -f(x)$, state clearly whether it is even, neither even nor odd, or can be split into an odd part and an even part.
3. Non-monotonic substitutions Substituting $t = \tan x$ directly across $[0, \pi]$ where $\tan x$ has an infinite discontinuity at $x = \frac{\pi}{2}$. Check continuity! If a discontinuity exists within $[a, b]$, split the integral via $P_2$ around the discontinuity or apply $P_6$ first to reduce the upper limit to $\frac{\pi}{2}$.
4. Unchanged limits during substitution Writing $u = g(x)$ and using $du = g'(x)dx$, but keeping original limits $a$ and $b$. Mark limits clearly: Write $x = a \implies u = g(a)$ and $x = b \implies u = g(b)$ alongside your substitution.
5. Modulus critical points outside range Splitting $|x - 3|$ on $[-1, 2]$ at $x = 3$. Only split at roots that lie strictly inside the open interval $(a, b)$. Since $3 \notin (-1, 2)$, $|x - 3| = -(x - 3) = 3 - x$ throughout $[-1, 2]$.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

5.1 The General King's Symmetry Result

If $f(x) + f(a + b - x) = K$ (a constant), then:

$$ \int_{a}^{b} \frac{f(x)}{f(x) + f(a + b - x)} \, dx = \frac{b - a}{2} $$

Instant Applications: - $\displaystyle\int_{\frac{\pi}{6}}^{\frac{\pi}{3}} \frac{dx}{1 + \sqrt{\tan x}} = \frac{\frac{\pi}{3} - \frac{\pi}{6}}{2} = \frac{\pi}{12}$ - $\displaystyle\int_{2}^{7} \frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{9 - x}} \, dx = \frac{7 - 2}{2} = \frac{5}{2}$

5.2 Linear Factor Elimination Shortcut

If an integral has the form:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi} x \cdot g(\sin x) \, dx $$

where $g(\sin(\pi - x)) = g(\sin x)$, King's Rule eliminates $x$ directly:

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} g(\sin x) \, dx $$

5.3 Quick Odd/Even Decomposition

Any arbitrary function $f(x)$ integrated on a symmetric interval $[-a, a]$ can be decomposed into an even part and an odd part:

$$ f(x) = \underbrace{\frac{f(x) + f(-x)}{2}}_{\text{Even Part } f_e(x)} + \underbrace{\frac{f(x) - f(-x)}{2}}_{\text{Odd Part } f_o(x)} $$

Since $\int_{-a}^{a} f_o(x) \, dx = 0$, only the even component survives:

$$ \int_{-a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = 2 \int_{0}^{a} \left(\frac{f(x) + f(-x)}{2}\right) dx $$

Classic Exam Application:

$$ \int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \left( \sin^7 x + x^3 \cos x + \cos^2 x \right) dx $$

  • $\sin^7(-x) = -\sin^7 x$ (Odd $\implies 0$)
  • $(-x)^3 \cos(-x) = -x^3 \cos x$ (Odd $\implies 0$)
  • $\cos^2(-x) = \cos^2 x$ (Even $\implies 2 \int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}} \cos^2 x \, dx = 2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}$)

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

5.1 The Instant Logarithmic Derivative Recognition

Whenever an integrand displays a quotient where the numerator is the derivative of the denominator:

$$ \int \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx = \ln|f(x)| + C $$

  • $\int \frac{2x + 5}{x^2 + 5x + 7}\,dx = \ln|x^2 + 5x + 7| + C$ (Mental check: $D[x^2+5x+7] = 2x+5$).
  • $\int \frac{\sec^2 x}{\tan x}\,dx = \ln|\tan x| + C$.

5.2 The "Power-Chain" Quick-Rule

$$ \int [f(x)]^n \cdot f'(x)\,dx = \frac{[f(x)]^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \quad (n \neq -1) $$

  • $\int \sin^5 x \cos x\,dx = \frac{\sin^6 x}{6} + C$.
  • $\int \frac{(\tan^{-1}x)^4}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \frac{(\tan^{-1}x)^5}{5} + C$.

5.3 Symmetric Shift for $\int \frac{dx}{x(x^n + 1)}$

When integrating $\int \frac{dx}{x(x^n + 1)}$ or $\int \frac{dx}{x(x^n - 1)}$: - Hack: Factor out $x^n$ from denominator, or multiply numerator and denominator by $x^{n-1}$.

$$ \int \frac{dx}{x(x^n + 1)} = \int \frac{x^{n-1}}{x^n(x^n + 1)}\,dx $$

Let $t = x^n \implies dt = n x^{n-1}\,dx$:

$$ = \frac{1}{n}\int \frac{dt}{t(t + 1)} = \frac{1}{n} \ln\left|\frac{t}{t + 1}\right| + C = \frac{1}{n}\ln\left|\frac{x^n}{x^n + 1}\right| + C $$

5.4 Algebraic Rationalizations: $\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x + a} + \sqrt{x + b}}$

Never attempt trigonometric substitutions when linear roots appear in sums/differences in the denominator. - Hack: Rationalize by multiplying numerator and denominator by $(\sqrt{x + a} - \sqrt{x + b})$:

$$ \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+a} + \sqrt{x+b}} = \frac{\sqrt{x+a} - \sqrt{x+b}}{(x+a) - (x+b)} = \frac{(x+a)^{1/2} - (x+b)^{1/2}}{a - b} $$

Integrate directly via power rule in under 15 seconds.

5.1 Golden Parity Rules for $\int \sin^m x \cos^n x \, dx$

  • If the power of $\sin x$ is odd ($m = 2k+1$): Save one $\sin x \, dx$, convert remaining sines to cosines using $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$, and substitute $t = \cos x$.
  • If the power of $\cos x$ is odd ($n = 2k+1$): Save one $\cos x \, dx$, convert remaining cosines to sines using $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$, and substitute $t = \sin x$.
  • If both powers are even: Must use half-angle formulas:

$$ \sin^2 x = \frac{1-\cos 2x}{2}, \quad \cos^2 x = \frac{1+\cos 2x}{2} $$

  • If $m + n$ is a negative even integer: Divide numerator and denominator by $\cos^k x$ to express the entire integrand in $\tan x$ and $\sec^2 x$, then substitute $t = \tan x$.

5.2 Mnemonic for the Standard Quadratic Radicals

  • For roots containing quadratic terms:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 \pm a^2}} = \ln \left| x + \sqrt{\text{exact same radical}} \right| + C $$

The radical in the argument of the logarithm is always identical to the radical in the denominator of the question.

5.3 Completing the Square Formulaic Direct Expression

For any expression $x^2 + bx + c$:

$$ x^2 + bx + c = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + \left(c - \frac{b^2}{4}\right) $$

This eliminates algebraic hesitation when finding the constant term under exam conditions.

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 5x + 6}\,dx $$

Solution:

Step 1: Degree inspection & Division - Degree of numerator = 2. - Degree of denominator = 2. Since $\deg(P) = \deg(Q)$, the fraction is improper. Dividing numerator by denominator:

$$ \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 5x + 6} = \frac{(x^2 - 5x + 6) + (5x - 5)}{x^2 - 5x + 6} = 1 + \frac{5x - 5}{x^2 - 5x + 6} $$

Hence:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int 1\,dx + \int \frac{5x - 5}{(x - 2)(x - 3)}\,dx = x + \mathcal{I}_1 $$

Step 2: Partial Fraction Decomposition on $\mathcal{I}_1$ Let:

$$ \frac{5x - 5}{(x - 2)(x - 3)} = \frac{A}{x - 2} + \frac{B}{x - 3} $$

$$ 5x - 5 = A(x - 3) + B(x - 2) $$

  • Put $x = 2$:

$$ 5(2) - 5 = A(2 - 3) + 0 \implies 5 = -A \implies A = -5 $$

  • Put $x = 3$:

$$ 5(3) - 5 = 0 + B(3 - 2) \implies 10 = B(1) \implies B = 10 $$

Step 3: Integration

$$ \mathcal{I}_1 = \int \left(\frac{-5}{x - 2} + \frac{10}{x - 3}\right)\,dx = -5\ln|x - 2| + 10\ln|x - 3| $$

Final Answer:

$$ \mathcal{I} = x - 5\ln|x - 2| + 10\ln|x - 3| + C $$

💡 Example 3.2: Repeated Linear Factors
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2023, 4 Marks] Evaluate:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{3x - 2}{(x + 1)^2(x + 3)}\,dx $$

Solution:

Step 1: Setup Decomposition

$$ \frac{3x - 2}{(x + 1)^2(x + 3)} = \frac{A}{x + 1} + \frac{B}{(x + 1)^2} + \frac{C}{x + 3} $$

Multiplying both sides by $(x + 1)^2(x + 3)$:

$$ 3x - 2 = A(x + 1)(x + 3) + B(x + 3) + C(x + 1)^2 $$

Step 2: Solve for Unknowns - Set $x = -1$:

$$ 3(-1) - 2 = 0 + B(-1 + 3) + 0 \implies -5 = 2B \implies B = -\frac{5}{2} $$

  • Set $x = -3$:

$$ 3(-3) - 2 = 0 + 0 + C(-3 + 1)^2 \implies -11 = 4C \implies C = -\frac{11}{4} $$

  • Equate coefficients of $x^2$ on both sides:

$$ 0 = A + C \implies A = -C = \frac{11}{4} $$

Step 3: Integrate each component

$$ \mathcal{I} = \frac{11}{4}\int \frac{dx}{x + 1} - \frac{5}{2}\int (x + 1)^{-2}\,dx - \frac{11}{4}\int \frac{dx}{x + 3} $$

$$ = \frac{11}{4}\ln|x + 1| - \frac{5}{2}\left[\frac{(x + 1)^{-1}}{-1}\right] - \frac{11}{4}\ln|x + 3| + C $$

$$ = \frac{11}{4}\ln\left|\frac{x + 1}{x + 3}\right| + \frac{5}{2(x + 1)} + C $$

Final Answer:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \frac{11}{4}\ln\left|\frac{x + 1}{x + 3}\right| + \frac{5}{2(x + 1)} + C $$

💡 Example 3.3: Pure Even Powers via Algebraic Substitution
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2022, Term-II, 3 Marks] Evaluate:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{x^2}{(x^2 + 1)(x^2 + 4)}\,dx $$

Solution:

[!WARNING] Do NOT set $x^2 = t$ and replace $dx$ by $\frac{dt}{2x}$. The differential $dx$ contains no factor of $x$. Instead, treat $x^2 = y$ as a temporary algebraic device for decomposition only.

Step 1: Algebraic Decomposition Consider the integrand as a function of $y = x^2$:

$$ \frac{y}{(y + 1)(y + 4)} = \frac{A}{y + 1} + \frac{B}{y + 4} $$

$$ y = A(y + 4) + B(y + 1) $$

  • Put $y = -1$:

$$ -1 = A(3) \implies A = -\frac{1}{3} $$

  • Put $y = -4$:

$$ -4 = B(-3) \implies B = \frac{4}{3} $$

Therefore:

$$ \frac{y}{(y + 1)(y + 4)} = -\frac{1}{3(y + 1)} + \frac{4}{3(y + 4)} $$

Step 2: Restore $y = x^2$ for Integration

$$ \frac{x^2}{(x^2 + 1)(x^2 + 4)} = -\frac{1}{3}\cdot \frac{1}{x^2 + 1} + \frac{4}{3}\cdot \frac{1}{x^2 + 2^2} $$

Now integrate with respect to $x$:

$$ \mathcal{I} = -\frac{1}{3}\int \frac{dx}{x^2 + 1} + \frac{4}{3}\int \frac{dx}{x^2 + 2^2} $$

$$ = -\frac{1}{3}\tan^{-1}(x) + \frac{4}{3}\cdot \frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + C $$

Final Answer:

$$ \mathcal{I} = -\frac{1}{3}\tan^{-1}(x) + \frac{2}{3}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + C $$

💡 Example 3.4: Mixed Linear & Irreducible Quadratic Factor
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2021, 5 Marks] Evaluate:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{2x}{(x^2 + 1)(x^2 + 3)}\,dx $$

Solution:

Notice the numerator has $2x\,dx = d(x^2)$. This is a standard variable substitution: Let $x^2 = t \implies 2x\,dx = dt$.

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{dt}{(t + 1)(t + 3)} $$

By inspection (or partial fractions):

$$ \frac{1}{(t + 1)(t + 3)} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{t + 1} - \frac{1}{t + 3}\right] $$

Integrating:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\ln|t + 1| - \ln|t + 3|\right] + C = \frac{1}{2}\ln\left|\frac{t + 1}{t + 3}\right| + C $$

Back-substitute $t = x^2$:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \frac{1}{2}\ln\left|\frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 + 3}\right| + C $$

💡 Example 3.5: Higher Power Transcendental Form (Standard $x(x^n+1)$ Type)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2020, 2025 Sample Paper, 3 Marks] Evaluate:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{dx}{x(x^5 + 1)} $$

Solution:

Step 1: Factor multiplication Multiply numerator and denominator by $x^4$:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \int \frac{x^4\,dx}{x^5(x^5 + 1)} $$

Step 2: Substitution Let $t = x^5 \implies dt = 5x^4\,dx \implies x^4\,dx = \frac{dt}{5}$.

$$ \mathcal{I} = \frac{1}{5}\int \frac{dt}{t(t + 1)} $$

Step 3: Decomposition & Integration

$$ \frac{1}{t(t + 1)} = \frac{1}{t} - \frac{1}{t + 1} $$

$$ \mathcal{I} = \frac{1}{5}\int \left(\frac{1}{t} - \frac{1}{t + 1}\right)\,dt = \frac{1}{5}\left[\ln|t| - \ln|t + 1|\right] + C $$

$$ = \frac{1}{5}\ln\left|\frac{t}{t + 1}\right| + C $$

Back-substituting $t = x^5$:

$$ \mathcal{I} = \frac{1}{5}\ln\left|\frac{x^5}{x^5 + 1}\right| + C $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Mistakes

[!CAUTION]

1. Forgetting Division on Improper Rational Functions

The Error: A student sees $\int \frac{x^3}{x^2 - 1}\,dx$ and directly sets $\frac{x^3}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{x+1}$. The Result: Completely incorrect decomposition. Equating degrees gives an inconsistent system ($x^3 = A(x+1) + B(x-1)$ has no constant solutions for $A, B$). The Fix: Always verify $\deg(P) < \deg(Q)$. If $\deg(P) \ge \deg(Q)$, divide first!

[!CAUTION]

2. Misidentifying Repeated Linear Denominators

The Error: For $(x - 2)^2$, setting the partial fraction as $\frac{A}{x - 2} + \frac{B}{x - 2}$ or $\frac{Ax + B}{(x - 2)^2}$. The Fix: The powers must ascend: $\frac{A}{x - 2} + \frac{B}{(x - 2)^2}$.

[!CAUTION]

3. Sign Errors in Integrals Involving $(a - x)$

The Error: Writing $\int \frac{dx}{3 - x} = \ln|3 - x| + C$. The Fix: $\frac{d}{dx}(3 - x) = -1$, therefore $\int \frac{dx}{3 - x} = -\ln|3 - x| + C$.

[!CAUTION]

4. Illegal Differential Substitution in Even Quadratic Forms

The Error: In $\int \frac{x^2}{(x^2+1)(x^2+2)}\,dx$, substituting $t = x^2$ and writing $\int \frac{t}{(t+1)(t+2)}\,dt$. The Fix: The substitution $y = x^2$ is strictly algebraic to find partial fraction coefficients. Keep $dx$ untouched and restore $x^2$ before integrating.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

5.1 Heaviside Cover-Up Method (Instant Coefficients)

For non-repeated linear factors:

$$ \frac{P(x)}{(x - a_1)(x - a_2)\cdots(x - a_n)} = \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{A_k}{x - a_k} $$

The coefficient $A_k$ is found instantly by covering up $(x - a_k)$ in the original fraction and evaluating the remainder at $x = a_k$:

$$ A_k = \left. \frac{P(x)}{\prod_{j \neq k}(x - a_j)} \right|_{x = a_k} $$

Lightning Example:

Find partial fractions of $\dfrac{3x + 1}{(x - 1)(x + 2)}$: - For $x - 1$: Cover $(x - 1)$, evaluate at $x = 1 \implies A = \dfrac{3(1) + 1}{1 + 2} = \dfrac{4}{3}$ - For $x + 2$: Cover $(x + 2)$, evaluate at $x = -2 \implies B = \dfrac{3(-2) + 1}{-2 - 1} = \dfrac{-5}{-3} = \dfrac{5}{3}$

$$ \therefore \frac{3x + 1}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{4/3}{x - 1} + \frac{5/3}{x + 2} $$

(Mental computation time: < 10 seconds)

5.2 The Generalized Golden Formula for $\int \frac{dx}{x(x^n \pm 1)}$

Memorize this board shortcut to verify answers instantaneously:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{x(x^n + 1)} = \frac{1}{n}\ln\left|\frac{x^n}{x^n + 1}\right| + C $$

$$ \int \frac{dx}{x(x^n - 1)} = \frac{1}{n}\ln\left|\frac{x^n - 1}{x^n}\right| + C $$

5.3 Symmetric Quartic Integrals ($\frac{x^2 \pm 1}{x^4 + kx^2 + 1}$)

Method: Divide numerator and denominator by $x^2$:

$$ \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^4 + kx^2 + 1} = \frac{1 + \frac{1}{x^2}}{x^2 + k + \frac{1}{x^2}} = \frac{d\left(x - \frac{1}{x}\right)}{\left(x - \frac{1}{x}\right)^2 + (k + 2)} $$

$$ \frac{x^2 - 1}{x^4 + kx^2 + 1} = \frac{1 - \frac{1}{x^2}}{x^2 + k + \frac{1}{x^2}} = \frac{d\left(x + \frac{1}{x}\right)}{\left(x + \frac{1}{x}\right)^2 + (k - 2)} $$

This converts a quartic denominator into a standard $\int \frac{du}{u^2 \pm a^2}$ integral in two lines.

💡 Example 1: Solitary Inverse Trigonometric Function
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Source: [CBSE 2024, 3 Marks] Problem: Evaluate $\int \tan^{-1} x \, dx$.

Solution

Step 1: Write the integrand as a product with $1$:

$$ I = \int (\tan^{-1} x) \cdot 1 \, dx $$

By ILATE: - First Function: $\text{I} = \tan^{-1} x$ (Inverse Trigonometric) - Second Function: $\text{II} = 1$ (Algebraic)

Step 2: Apply Integration by Parts:

$$ I = \tan^{-1} x \int 1 \, dx - \int \left[ \frac{d}{dx}(\tan^{-1} x) \cdot \int 1 \, dx \right] dx $$

$$ I = x \tan^{-1} x - \int \left( \frac{1}{1+x^2} \cdot x \right) dx $$

Step 3: Evaluate the remaining integral $I_2 = \int \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx$. Multiply and divide by $2$:

$$ I_2 = \frac{1}{2} \int \frac{2x}{1+x^2} \, dx $$

Let $t = 1+x^2 \implies dt = 2x \, dx$:

$$ I_2 = \frac{1}{2} \int \frac{dt}{t} = \frac{1}{2} \ln|t| = \frac{1}{2} \ln(1+x^2) $$

(Absolute value brackets can be dropped since $1+x^2 > 0$ for all real $x$.)

Step 4: Combine the terms:

$$ I = x \tan^{-1} x - \frac{1}{2}\ln(1+x^2) + C $$

💡 Example 2: Repeated Integration by Parts with Algebraic-Trigonometric Product
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Source: [CBSE 2023, 3 Marks] Problem: Find $\int x^2 \cos x \, dx$.

Solution

Step 1: Apply ILATE: - First Function: $\text{I} = x^2$ (Algebraic) - Second Function: $\text{II} = \cos x$ (Trigonometric)

Step 2: First application of Integration by Parts:

$$ I = x^2 \int \cos x \, dx - \int \left( \frac{d}{dx}[x^2] \cdot \int \cos x \, dx \right) dx $$

$$ I = x^2 \sin x - \int (2x \cdot \sin x) \, dx $$

$$ I = x^2 \sin x - 2 \int x \sin x \, dx $$

Step 3: Apply Integration by Parts to $J = \int x \sin x \, dx$: - $\text{I} = x$ - $\text{II} = \sin x$

$$ J = x \int \sin x \, dx - \int \left( \frac{d}{dx}[x] \cdot \int \sin x \, dx \right) dx $$

$$ J = x(-\cos x) - \int 1 \cdot (-\cos x) \, dx $$

$$ J = -x \cos x + \int \cos x \, dx = -x \cos x + \sin x $$

Step 4: Substitute $J$ back into $I$:

$$ I = x^2 \sin x - 2 \left( -x \cos x + \sin x \right) + C $$

$$ I = x^2 \sin x + 2x \cos x - 2\sin x + C $$

$$ I = (x^2 - 2)\sin x + 2x \cos x + C $$

💡 Example 3: Cyclical Integration (The Looping Integral)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Source: [CBSE 2022 Term-2, 4 Marks] Problem: Evaluate $\int e^{2x} \sin x \, dx$.

Solution

Step 1: Let:

$$ I = \int e^{2x} \sin x \, dx $$

By ILATE: - First Function: $\text{I} = \sin x$ (Trigonometric) - Second Function: $\text{II} = e^{2x}$ (Exponential)

Step 2: First application of Integration by Parts:

$$ I = \sin x \left( \frac{e^{2x}}{2} \right) - \int \cos x \left( \frac{e^{2x}}{2} \right) dx $$

$$ I = \frac{1}{2} e^{2x} \sin x - \frac{1}{2} \int e^{2x} \cos x \, dx $$

Step 3: Apply Integration by Parts to the new integral: - $\text{I} = \cos x$ - $\text{II} = e^{2x}$

$$ \int e^{2x} \cos x \, dx = \cos x \left( \frac{e^{2x}}{2} \right) - \int (-\sin x) \left( \frac{e^{2x}}{2} \right) dx $$

$$ \int e^{2x} \cos x \, dx = \frac{1}{2} e^{2x} \cos x + \frac{1}{2} \int e^{2x} \sin x \, dx $$

Step 4: Substitute back into the equation for $I$:

$$ I = \frac{1}{2} e^{2x} \sin x - \frac{1}{2} \left[ \frac{1}{2} e^{2x} \cos x + \frac{1}{2} I \right] $$

$$ I = \frac{1}{2} e^{2x} \sin x - \frac{1}{4} e^{2x} \cos x - \frac{1}{4} I $$

Step 5: Group all terms containing $I$ on the left-hand side:

$$ I + \frac{1}{4}I = e^{2x} \left( \frac{1}{2}\sin x - \frac{1}{4}\cos x \right) $$

$$ \frac{5}{4} I = \frac{e^{2x} (2\sin x - \cos x)}{4} $$

$$ I = \frac{e^{2x}}{5} (2\sin x - \cos x) + C $$

💡 Example 4: The Rational Form of the Exponential Integrand
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Source: [CBSE 2021 Compartment / Sample Paper, 3 Marks] Problem: Evaluate $\int e^x \left[ \frac{x-3}{(x-1)^3} \right] dx$.

Solution

Step 1: Rewrite the rational expression inside the brackets in terms of $(x-1)$:

$$ \frac{x-3}{(x-1)^3} = \frac{(x-1) - 2}{(x-1)^3} = \frac{x-1}{(x-1)^3} - \frac{2}{(x-1)^3} = \frac{1}{(x-1)^2} - \frac{2}{(x-1)^3} $$

Step 2: Identify $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$: Let:

$$ f(x) = \frac{1}{(x-1)^2} = (x-1)^{-2} $$

Compute the derivative:

$$ f'(x) = -2(x-1)^{-3} = -\frac{2}{(x-1)^3} $$

The integral now matches the standard form:

$$ I = \int e^x \left[ f(x) + f'(x) \right] dx \quad \text{where } f(x) = \frac{1}{(x-1)^2} $$

Step 3: Apply Theorem 2:

$$ \int e^x [f(x) + f'(x)] \, dx = e^x f(x) + C $$

$$ I = \frac{e^x}{(x-1)^2} + C $$

💡 Example 5: Trigonometric Exponential Form
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Source: [CBSE 2025 Board Model / 2020 Standard, 3 Marks] Problem: Evaluate $\int e^x \left( \frac{1 + \sin x}{1 + \cos x} \right) dx$.

Solution

Step 1: Use half-angle identities to simplify the integrand: - $1 + \sin x = 1 + 2\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$ - $1 + \cos x = 2\cos^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$

Step 2: Split into two fractions:

$$ \frac{1 + \sin x}{1 + \cos x} = \frac{1}{2\cos^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)} + \frac{2\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)}{2\cos^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)} $$

$$ = \frac{1}{2}\sec^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

Step 3: Identify $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$: Let:

$$ f(x) = \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

Compute the derivative using the chain rule:

$$ f'(x) = \sec^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sec^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

The integrand now matches the standard form:

$$ I = \int e^x \left[ \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + \frac{1}{2}\sec^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \right] dx = \int e^x \left[ f(x) + f'(x) \right] dx $$

Step 4: Write down the final result:

$$ I = e^x \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + C $$

💡 Example 6: Substitution Leading to Integration by Parts
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Source: [CBSE 2023, 5 Marks] Problem: Evaluate $\int \sin^{-1}\left( \frac{2x}{1+x^2} \right) dx$.

Solution

Step 1: Substitute $x = \tan \theta \implies dx = \sec^2 \theta \, d\theta$, where $\theta = \tan^{-1} x$:

$$ \sin^{-1}\left( \frac{2x}{1+x^2} \right) = \sin^{-1}\left( \frac{2\tan\theta}{1+\tan^2\theta} \right) = \sin^{-1}(\sin 2\theta) = 2\theta = 2\tan^{-1} x $$

(Assuming the principal branch condition $|x| \le 1$.)

Step 2: Substitute into the integral:

$$ I = \int 2\tan^{-1} x \, dx = 2 \int (\tan^{-1} x \cdot 1) \, dx $$

Step 3: Apply Integration by Parts: - $\text{I} = \tan^{-1} x$ - $\text{II} = 1$

$$ I = 2 \left[ x \tan^{-1} x - \int \frac{x}{1+x^2} \, dx \right] $$

$$ I = 2 \left[ x \tan^{-1} x - \frac{1}{2}\ln(1+x^2) \right] + C $$

$$ I = 2x \tan^{-1} x - \ln(1+x^2) + C $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap 1: The Missing Parentheses Sign Error

When expanding the nested integral in:

$$ \int u \cdot v \, dx = u \int v \, dx - \int \left( u' \int v \, dx \right) dx $$

If the second integral $\int v \, dx$ evaluates to a negative expression (e.g., $\int \sin x \, dx = -\cos x$), students often drop the double negative:

$$ \int x \sin x \, dx = x(-\cos x) - \int (1 \cdot (-\cos x)) \, dx $$

$$ \text{Incorrect:} \quad -x\cos x - \int \cos x \, dx = -x\cos x - \sin x $$

$$ \text{Correct:} \quad -x\cos x + \int \cos x \, dx = -x\cos x + \sin x + C $$

Trap 2: Switching Functions in Cyclical (Looping) Integrals

In cyclical integrals like $\int e^{ax} \sin(bx) \, dx$: - If you set $\text{I} = \sin(bx)$ in the first integration step, you must set the trigonometric function as $\text{I} = \cos(bx)$ in the second integration step. - If you switch conventions midway and set $\text{I} = e^{ax}$ in the second step, the equation degenerates into the trivial identity $0 = 0$:

$$ I = \dots \implies I = I \implies 0 = 0 $$

Always maintain the same functional category for $\text{I}$ across all iterations.

Trap 3: Direct Formula Application Without Stating the Derivative

In board exams, applying $\int e^x [f(x) + f'(x)] dx = e^x f(x) + C$ without explicitly defining both $f(x)$ and $f'(x)$ leads to mark deductions.

CBSE Marking Scheme Requirement: 1. State the standard formula explicitly:

$$ \int e^x [f(x) + f'(x)] \, dx = e^x f(x) + C $$

  1. Identify and write out each component clearly:

$$ f(x) = \dots \quad \text{and} \quad f'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)] = \dots $$

  1. Write the final answer with the integration constant $+ \, C$.

Trap 4: Non-Unit Linear Coefficients in the Exponential Term

Consider $\int e^{3x} \left( 3\tan x + \sec^2 x \right) dx$. Students often mistakenly factor out a $\frac{1}{3}$ or mismatch the linear argument: - The standard form is:

$$ \int e^{kx} \left[ k f(x) + f'(x) \right] dx = e^{kx} f(x) + C $$

  • Here, $k = 3$, $f(x) = \tan x$, and $f'(x) = \sec^2 x$.
  • Therefore, the integral evaluates directly to:

$$ e^{3x} \tan x + C $$

  • Do not divide the entire result by $3$ again.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

5.1 The Tabular Method (DI Method / Stand and Deliver)

For integrands of the form $\int P_n(x) \cdot g(x) \, dx$, where $P_n(x)$ is an algebraic polynomial of degree $n$ and $g(x)$ can be integrated repeatedly (e.g., $\sin(ax), \cos(ax), e^{ax}$), use the DI Method (Differentiation-Integration Table).

Algorithm

  1. Create two columns: $D$ (Differentiate) and $I$ (Integrate).
  2. Set $P_n(x)$ in the $D$ column and $g(x)$ in the $I$ column.
  3. Repeatedly differentiate $P_n(x)$ until it reaches $0$.
  4. Repeatedly integrate $g(x)$ the same number of times.
  5. Assign alternating signs starting with $+$ on the first term: $(+), (-), (+), (-), \dots$
  6. Pair the terms diagonally: multiply each $D$ entry by the $I$ entry in the row below it.
  7. Sum these products to obtain the complete integral.

Illustration: Evaluate $\int x^3 e^{2x} \, dx$

Row Sign $D$ (Differentiate) $I$ (Integrate)
0 $x^3$ $e^{2x}$
1 $(+)$ $3x^2$ $\frac{1}{2}e^{2x}$
2 $(-)$ $6x$ $\frac{1}{4}e^{2x}$
3 $(+)$ $6$ $\frac{1}{8}e^{2x}$
4 $(-)$ $0$ $\frac{1}{16}e^{2x}$

Pair each term diagonally: - Row 0 to Row 1: $(+) \cdot x^3 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{2}e^{2x}\right) = \frac{1}{2}x^3 e^{2x}$ - Row 1 to Row 2: $(-) \cdot 3x^2 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{4}e^{2x}\right) = -\frac{3}{4}x^2 e^{2x}$ - Row 2 to Row 3: $(+) \cdot 6x \cdot \left(\frac{1}{8}e^{2x}\right) = +\frac{3}{4}x e^{2x}$ - Row 3 to Row 4: $(-) \cdot 6 \cdot \left(\frac{1}{16}e^{2x}\right) = -\frac{3}{8}e^{2x}$

Final Result:

$$ \int x^3 e^{2x} \, dx = e^{2x} \left( \frac{x^3}{2} - \frac{3x^2}{4} + \frac{3x}{4} - \frac{3}{8} \right) + C $$

5.2 Closed-Form Solutions for Exponential-Trigonometric Products

You can write down the integrals of $e^{ax}\sin(bx)$ and $e^{ax}\cos(bx)$ directly using their closed-form solutions:

$$ \int e^{ax} \sin(bx) \, dx = \frac{e^{ax}}{a^2 + b^2} \left[ a \sin(bx) - b \cos(bx) \right] + C $$

$$ \int e^{ax} \cos(bx) \, dx = \frac{e^{ax}}{a^2 + b^2} \left[ a \cos(bx) + b \sin(bx) \right] + C $$

Mnemonic for Signs

  • $\sin(bx)$ begins with a negative sign on its cross-derivative term: $[a\sin(bx) \mathbf{-} b\cos(bx)]$.
  • $\cos(bx)$ begins with a positive sign on its cross-derivative term: $[a\cos(bx) \mathbf{+} b\sin(bx)]$.

💡 Example 3.1: Fundamental Theorem with Algebraic Rational Substitution
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
Problem: Evaluate the definite integral:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{1} \frac{x}{1 + \sqrt{x}} \, dx $$

Solution: * Step 1: Rationalizing Substitution Let $\sqrt{x} = t \implies x = t^2$.
Differentiating both sides: $dx = 2t \, dt$. * Step 2: Change of Limits * When $x = 0 \implies t = \sqrt{0} = 0$. * When $x = 1 \implies t = \sqrt{1} = 1$. * Step 3: Transform Integrand

$$ I = \int_{0}^{1} \frac{t^2}{1 + t} (2t \, dt) = 2 \int_{0}^{1} \frac{t^3}{t + 1} \, dt $$

  • Step 4: Polynomial Division / Algebraic Decomposition Add and subtract $1$ in the numerator:

$$ \frac{t^3}{t+1} = \frac{(t^3 + 1) - 1}{t + 1} = \frac{(t+1)(t^2 - t + 1) - 1}{t + 1} = (t^2 - t + 1) - \frac{1}{t + 1} $$

  • Step 5: Integration and Application of FTC-2

$$ I = 2 \int_{0}^{1} \left( t^2 - t + 1 - \frac{1}{t+1} \right) dt $$

$$ I = 2 \left[ \frac{t^3}{3} - \frac{t^2}{2} + t - \ln|t + 1| \right]_{0}^{1} $$

  • Step 6: Boundary Evaluation At upper limit $t = 1$:

$$ \left( \frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2} + 1 - \ln 2 \right) = \left( \frac{2 - 3 + 6}{6} - \ln 2 \right) = \frac{5}{6} - \ln 2 $$

At lower limit $t = 0$:

$$ (0 - 0 + 0 - \ln 1) = 0 $$

Therefore:

$$ I = 2 \left( \frac{5}{6} - \ln 2 \right) = \frac{5}{3} - 2\ln 2 $$

💡 Example 3.2: Trigonometric Definite Integral with Incomplete Period Limits
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2023, 3 Marks]
Problem: Evaluate:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi/4} \frac{\sin x + \cos x}{9 + 16 \sin 2x} \, dx $$

Solution: * Step 1: Algebraic Manipulation Strategy Notice the numerator is $(\sin x + \cos x) \, dx = d(\sin x - \cos x)$. Let $t = \sin x - \cos x$. * Step 2: Relate $t^2$ to the Denominator

$$ t^2 = (\sin x - \cos x)^2 = \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x - 2\sin x \cos x = 1 - \sin 2x $$

$$ \implies \sin 2x = 1 - t^2 $$

Substitute this into the denominator:

$$ 9 + 16\sin 2x = 9 + 16(1 - t^2) = 9 + 16 - 16t^2 = 25 - 16t^2 $$

  • Step 3: Limit Transformation
  • When $x = 0$: $t = \sin 0 - \cos 0 = -1$.
  • When $x = \frac{\pi}{4}$: $t = \sin \frac{\pi}{4} - \cos \frac{\pi}{4} = 0$.
  • Step 4: Standard Form Integration

$$ I = \int_{-1}^{0} \frac{dt}{25 - 16t^2} = \frac{1}{16} \int_{-1}^{0} \frac{dt}{\left(\frac{5}{4}\right)^2 - t^2} $$

Recall the standard integral: $\int \frac{dt}{a^2 - t^2} = \frac{1}{2a} \ln \left| \frac{a+t}{a-t} \right|$. Here $a = \frac{5}{4}$, so:

$$ \frac{1}{2a} = \frac{1}{2 \cdot \frac{5}{4}} = \frac{2}{5} $$

  • Step 5: Compute Antiderivative via FTC-2

$$ I = \frac{1}{16} \cdot \frac{2}{5} \left[ \ln \left| \frac{\frac{5}{4} + t}{\frac{5}{4} - t} \right| \right]_{-1}^{0} = \frac{1}{40} \left[ \ln \left| \frac{5 + 4t}{5 - 4t} \right| \right]_{-1}^{0} $$

  • Upper limit ($t = 0$):

$$ \ln \left| \frac{5+0}{5-0} \right| = \ln 1 = 0 $$

  • Lower limit ($t = -1$):

$$ \ln \left| \frac{5 - 4}{5 - (-4)} \right| = \ln \left( \frac{1}{9} \right) = -\ln 9 $$

  • Step 6: Evaluation

$$ I = \frac{1}{40} \Big[ 0 - (-\ln 9) \Big] = \frac{\ln 9}{40} = \frac{\ln(3^2)}{40} = \frac{2\ln 3}{40} = \frac{1}{20} \ln 3 $$

💡 Example 3.3: Definite Integration by Parts with Limits
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2022 Term-2, 2 Marks]
Problem: Evaluate:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{1} x \tan^{-1} x \, dx $$

Solution: * Step 1: Choose Parts via ILATE * Inverse trigonometric function: $u = \tan^{-1} x \implies u' = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ * Algebraic function: $v' = x \implies v = \frac{x^2}{2}$ * Step 2: Apply Definite Integration by Parts Formula

$$ \int_{0}^{1} u v' \, dx = \Big[ u v \Big]_{0}^{1} - \int_{0}^{1} v u' \, dx $$

$$ I = \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \tan^{-1} x \right]_{0}^{1} - \int_{0}^{1} \frac{x^2}{2(1 + x^2)} \, dx $$

  • Step 3: Evaluate the Boundary Term

$$ \left[ \frac{x^2}{2} \tan^{-1} x \right]_{0}^{1} = \left( \frac{1^2}{2} \tan^{-1}(1) \right) - 0 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{8} $$

  • Step 4: Integrate the Remaining Integral

$$ \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{1} \frac{x^2}{1 + x^2} \, dx = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{1} \frac{(x^2 + 1) - 1}{1 + x^2} \, dx = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{1} \left(1 - \frac{1}{1 + x^2}\right) dx $$

$$ = \frac{1}{2} \Big[ x - \tan^{-1} x \Big]_{0}^{1} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \left(1 - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) - (0 - 0) \right] = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{\pi}{8} $$

  • Step 5: Final Combination

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{8} - \left( \frac{1}{2} - \frac{\pi}{8} \right) = \frac{\pi}{8} - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\pi}{8} = \frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{1}{2} $$

💡 Example 3.4: Logarithmic-Trigonometric Boundary Verification
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2020, 4 Marks]
Problem: Evaluate:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \frac{x + \sin x}{1 + \cos x} \, dx $$

Solution: * Step 1: Trigonometric Half-Angle Decomposition Recall: $1 + \cos x = 2\cos^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$ and $\sin x = 2\sin\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)\cos\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$. Substitute these into the integral:

$$ \frac{x + \sin x}{1 + \cos x} = \frac{x}{2\cos^2(x/2)} + \frac{2\sin(x/2)\cos(x/2)}{2\cos^2(x/2)} = \frac{1}{2} x \sec^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) + \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) $$

  • Step 2: Split the Integrals

$$ I = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi/2} x \sec^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \, dx + \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \, dx $$

Label the first integral $I_1 = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi/2} x \sec^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \, dx$. * Step 3: Integrate $I_1$ by Parts Let $u = x \implies u' = 1$ and $v' = \sec^2\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \implies v = \frac{\tan(x/2)}{1/2} = 2\tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right)$.

$$ I_1 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \Big[ 2x \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \Big]_{0}^{\pi/2} - \int_{0}^{\pi/2} 2\tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \, dx \right) $$

$$ I_1 = \Big[ x \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \Big]_{0}^{\pi/2} - \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \, dx $$

  • Step 4: Total Re-assembly & Cancellation Substitute $I_1$ back into $I$:

$$ I = \left( \Big[ x \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \Big]_{0}^{\pi/2} - \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \, dx \right) + \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \, dx $$

The integral terms cancel:

$$ I = \Big[ x \tan\left(\frac{x}{2}\right) \Big]_{0}^{\pi/2} $$

  • Step 5: Apply Limits
  • At $x = \frac{\pi}{2}$: $\frac{\pi}{2} \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot 1 = \frac{\pi}{2}$
  • At $x = 0$: $0 \cdot \tan(0) = 0$

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} - 0 = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

4. Examiner Traps & High-Frequency Common Mistakes

1. The "Forgot to Change the Limits" Trap

  • The Error: A student substitutes $u = g(x)$, transforms the integrand to $f(u) du$, but keeps the original limits $a$ and $b$.
  • Examiner Grading: Immediate loss of 1 to 1.5 marks.
  • The Rule: The moment a new variable is introduced, calculate the new limits $u_1 = g(a)$ and $u_2 = g(b)$ and replace them immediately. Never write $\int_{a}^{b} f(u) du$.

2. Discontinuity Within the Interval of Integration

  • The Error: Blindly using FTC-2 across a point of discontinuity.

$$ \text{Incorrect: } \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1}{x^2} \, dx = \left[ -\frac{1}{x} \right]_{-1}^{1} = -1 - (1) = -2 $$

Why this is impossible: The integrand $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2} > 0$ for all $x \ne 0$. A strictly positive function cannot have a negative area. * The Trap: $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$ has an infinite discontinuity at $x = 0 \in [-1, 1]$. FTC-2 requires continuity on $[a, b]$. This is an improper integral that diverges to $+\infty$. * Board Takeaway: Always scan the denominator of rational and trigonometric functions on $[a, b]$ for roots before integrating.

3. Sign Flipping in Lower Limits

  • The Error: Losing minus signs when computing $F(b) - F(a)$, especially when $F(a)$ contains negative values or logarithms.

$$ \text{Incorrect: } [ -\cos x ]_{\pi/2}^{\pi} = -\cos\pi - \cos(\pi/2) = -(-1) - 0 = 1 \quad (\text{often dropped as } -1) $$

  • Pedagogical Safeguard: Use nested square brackets:

$$ \Big[ F(x) \Big]_{a}^{b} = \Big[ F(b) \Big] - \Big[ F(a) \Big] $$

4. Splitting Modulus Across Roots Incorrectly

  • For $I = \int_{0}^{2} |x^2 - 3x + 2| \, dx$:
  • Factor $x^2 - 3x + 2 = (x-1)(x-2)$.
  • Root inside interval is $x = 1$.
  • On $[0, 1]$: $x^2 - 3x + 2 \ge 0 \implies |f(x)| = x^2 - 3x + 2$.
  • On $[1, 2]$: $x^2 - 3x + 2 \le 0 \implies |f(x)| = -(x^2 - 3x + 2) = -x^2 + 3x - 2$.
  • Mistake: Forgetting to negate the integrand in the negative region.

5. Speed Hacks, Shortcuts & Golden Revision Points

1. Leibniz Rule for Differentiating Under the Integral Sign (Fast-Track for Competitions & Verification)

If $F(x) = \int_{u(x)}^{v(x)} f(t) \, dt$, then:

$$ F'(x) = f(v(x)) \cdot v'(x) - f(u(x)) \cdot u'(x) $$

Example: $\frac{d}{dx} \int_{0}^{x^2} \sin(t^2) \, dt = \sin((x^2)^2) \cdot (x^2)' - 0 = 2x \sin(x^4)$.

2. Linear Scaling Shift Shortcut

$$ \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx = \frac{1}{m} \int_{ma+c}^{mb+c} f\left(\frac{u - c}{m}\right) du \quad \text{for } u = mx + c $$

Useful for quick linear transformations:

$$ \int_{0}^{1} (ax + b)^n \, dx = \frac{1}{a(n+1)} \left[ (a + b)^{n+1} - b^{n+1} \right] $$

3. Symmetrical Zero Check

For an odd function $f(-x) = -f(x)$:

$$ \int_{-a}^{a} f(x) \, dx = 0 $$

Mental Check: Always check whether $f(-x) = -f(x)$ for symmetric limits $[-a, a]$. If it is odd, write the answer as $0$ with the standard one-line NCERT justification.

4. Golden Step Sequence for Board Exam Presentation

  1. Define: State the integral clearly as $I = \int_{a}^{b} f(x) \, dx$.
  2. Substitute: When substituting $t = g(x)$, write $dt = g'(x) dx$ and construct a clean limit table:

$$ \begin{array}{|c|c|c|} \hline x & a & b \\ \hline t & g(a) & g(b) \\ \hline \end{array} $$

  1. Integrate: Display the antiderivative inside square brackets with upper and lower limits: $\Big[ F(t) \Big]_{g(a)}^{g(b)}$.
  2. Expand: Write $[F(g(b))] - [F(g(a))]$ explicitly before simplifying the arithmetic.
  3. Box: Highlight the final answer: $\boxed{I = \text{Value}}$.

Solution: * Step 1: Partial fraction decomposition (algebraic substitution):
Temporarily let $x^2 = y$ (for decomposition purposes only):

$$ \frac{y}{(y + 1)(y + 4)} = \frac{A}{y + 1} + \frac{B}{y + 4} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

For $y = -4 \implies -4 = -3B \implies B = \frac{4}{3}$. $\quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]}$

Solution: * Step 1: Applying property $\int_0^a f(x)dx = \int_0^a f(a - x)dx$:
Let $I = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{x \sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx \quad \text{--- (1)}$

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{(\pi - x) \sin(\pi - x)}{1 + \cos^2(\pi - x)} \, dx = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{(\pi - x) \sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx \quad \text{--- (2)} \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 2: Adding (1) and (2):

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\pi \sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx \implies I = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} \frac{\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x} \, dx \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 3: Substitution & Final Evaluation:
    Let $u = \cos x \implies du = -\sin x \, dx$.
    When $x = 0$, $u = 1$; when $x = \pi$, $u = -1$.

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_{1}^{-1} \frac{-du}{1 + u^2} = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_{-1}^{1} \frac{du}{1 + u^2} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \tan^{-1} u \right]_{-1}^{1} = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \frac{\pi}{4} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \right] = \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi^2}{4} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

Question 11

[CBSE 2023 / 2024 Pattern, 4 Marks]
Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Case Study Context:
In electrical engineering, alternating current (AC) is represented by sinusoidal waves. The power consumption and heat dissipated in circuits over a cycle $[0, T]$ require definite integrals of trigonometric powers. A testing laboratory models current passing through an inductor as $i(t) = I_0 \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)$ over the time interval $[0, 4]$.

          i(t)
           ^
       I_0 |      .--.
           |     /    \
         0 +----+------+----+--> t
           |   0 \    / 2   4
      -I_0 |      '--'
  1. Evaluate $\int_{0}^{2} \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) dt$. (1 Mark)
  2. Find the average value given by $\frac{1}{2}\int_0^2 \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) dt$. (1 Mark)
  3. Evaluate $\int_{0}^{4} \left| \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) \right| dt$. (2 Marks)
    OR
    Evaluate $\int_{0}^{1} t \cdot e^{\frac{\pi t}{2}} \, dt$. (2 Marks)

Step-by-Step Marking Scheme &

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Solution:

Sub-part (1):

$$ \int_{0}^{2} \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) dt = \int_{0}^{2} \frac{1 - \cos(\pi t)}{2} \, dt \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ = \frac{1}{2} \left[ t - \frac{\sin(\pi t)}{\pi} \right]_{0}^{2} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \left(2 - \frac{\sin(2\pi)}{\pi}\right) - (0) \right] = \frac{1}{2}(2 - 0) = 1 \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

Sub-part (2):

$$ \text{Average value} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ -\frac{\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)}{\frac{\pi}{2}} \right]_{0}^{2} = -\frac{1}{\pi} [\cos(\pi) - \cos(0)] \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ = -\frac{1}{\pi} [-1 - 1] = \frac{2}{\pi} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

Sub-part (3):

  • Sign analysis of modulus:
    On $[0, 2]$, $\frac{\pi t}{2} \in [0, \pi] \implies \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) \ge 0$.
    On $[2, 4]$, $\frac{\pi t}{2} \in [\pi, 2\pi] \implies \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) \le 0$. $\quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]}$

  • Splitting the integral:

$$ \int_{0}^{4} \left|\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)\right| dt = \int_{0}^{2} \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) dt - \int_{2}^{4} \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) dt \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ \int_{0}^{2} \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) dt = \frac{4}{\pi} \quad \text{and} \quad \int_{2}^{4} \sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right) dt = \left[-\frac{2}{\pi}\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{2}\right)\right]_{2}^{4} = -\frac{2}{\pi}[1 - (-1)] = -\frac{4}{\pi} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ \text{Total} = \frac{4}{\pi} - \left(-\frac{4}{\pi}\right) = \frac{8}{\pi} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

(OR branch for Sub-part 3):

$$ \int_{0}^{1} t \cdot e^{\frac{\pi t}{2}} \, dt $$

Using Integration by Parts: $\int u v \, dt = u \int v \, dt - \int (u' \int v \, dt) dt$ Let $u = t, v = e^{\frac{\pi t}{2}}$:

$$ \left[ t \cdot \frac{e^{\frac{\pi t}{2}}}{\pi/2} \right]_{0}^{1} - \int_{0}^{1} 1 \cdot \frac{e^{\frac{\pi t}{2}}}{\pi/2} \, dt \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ = \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ 1 \cdot e^{\pi/2} - 0 \right] - \frac{2}{\pi} \left[ \frac{e^{\frac{\pi t}{2}}}{\pi/2} \right]_{0}^{1} = \frac{2}{\pi} e^{\pi/2} - \frac{4}{\pi^2} \left[ e^{\pi/2} - 1 \right] = \left(\frac{2}{\pi} - \frac{4}{\pi^2}\right)e^{\pi/2} + \frac{4}{\pi^2} \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ \text{Evaluate: } \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\sin x) \, dx $$

Step-by-Step Marking Scheme &

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Solution: * Step 1: Set up and apply King's Property:
Let:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\sin x) \, dx \quad \text{--- (1)} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

Using $\int_{0}^{a} f(x) \, dx = \int_{0}^{a} f(a - x) \, dx$:

$$ I = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log\left(\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - x\right)\right) dx = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\cos x) \, dx \quad \text{--- (2)} \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 2: Adding the two equations:

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} [\log(\sin x) + \log(\cos x)] \, dx = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\sin x \cos x) \, dx \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log\left(\frac{\sin 2x}{2}\right) dx = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\sin 2x) \, dx - \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log 2 \, dx \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ 2I = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\sin 2x) \, dx - \frac{\pi}{2} \log 2 \quad \text{--- (3)} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 3: Evaluating $\int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\sin 2x) \, dx$:
    Let $2x = t \implies dx = \frac{dt}{2}$.
    Limits: when $x = 0 \implies t = 0$; when $x = \frac{\pi}{2} \implies t = \pi$.

$$ I_1 = \frac{1}{2} \int_{0}^{\pi} \log(\sin t) \, dt \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

Using property $\int_{0}^{2a} f(t) \, dt = 2 \int_{0}^{a} f(t) \, dt$ if $f(2a - t) = f(t)$:
Since $\sin(\pi - t) = \sin t$, we have:

$$ I_1 = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 2 \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\sin t) \, dt = \int_{0}^{\pi/2} \log(\sin x) \, dx = I \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 4: Equating and finding $I$:
    Substitute $I_1 = I$ into equation (3):

$$ 2I = I - \frac{\pi}{2} \log 2 \implies I = -\frac{\pi}{2} \log 2 \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{\pi}{2} \log\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

Solution: * Step 1: Dividing numerator and denominator by $x^2$:

$$ I = \int \frac{1 + \frac{1}{x^2}}{x^2 + \frac{1}{x^2}} \, dx \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 2: Completing square in denominator:
    Notice that $\frac{d}{dx}\left(x - \frac{1}{x}\right) = 1 + \frac{1}{x^2}$.

    Let $u = x - \frac{1}{x} \implies du = \left(1 + \frac{1}{x^2}\right) dx$. $\quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]}$

$$ I = \int \frac{du}{u^2 + (\sqrt{2})^2} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ I = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{u}{\sqrt{2}}\right) + C \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 5: Back-substituting $u = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x}$:

Step-by-Step Marking Scheme &

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Solution: * Step 1: Factorizing denominator:

$$ x^3 - x^2 - x + 1 = x^2(x - 1) - 1(x - 1) = (x - 1)(x^2 - 1) = (x - 1)^2 (x + 1) \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 2: Partial fractions decomposition:

$$ \frac{3x + 5}{(x - 1)^2 (x + 1)} = \frac{A}{x - 1} + \frac{B}{(x - 1)^2} + \frac{C}{x + 1} \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ 3x + 5 = A(x - 1)(x + 1) + B(x + 1) + C(x - 1)^2 $$

  • Step 3: Finding coefficients $A, B, C$:
  • Put $x = 1$: $3(1) + 5 = B(2) \implies 8 = 2B \implies B = 4$
  • Put $x = -1$: $3(-1) + 5 = C(-2)^2 \implies 2 = 4C \implies C = \frac{1}{2}$

  • Compare coefficients of $x^2$: $0 = A + C \implies A = -C = -\frac{1}{2}$ $\quad \mathbf{[1.5 \text{ Marks}]}$

  • Step 4: Integration:

$$ I = \int \left( \frac{-1/2}{x - 1} + \frac{4}{(x - 1)^2} + \frac{1/2}{x + 1} \right) dx \quad \mathbf{[1 \text{ Mark}]} $$

$$ I = -\frac{1}{2} \ln|x - 1| - \frac{4}{x - 1} + \frac{1}{2} \ln|x + 1| + C \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

  • Step 5: Clean logarithmic grouping:

$$ I = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left| \frac{x + 1}{x - 1} \right| - \frac{4}{x - 1} + C \quad \mathbf{[1/2 \text{ Mark}]} $$

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

(Use these techniques for quick evaluation, rough validation, and MCQs. For subjective questions, use them to verify your final step in under 10 seconds.)

1.1 The Tabular Method (DI Method) for Integration by Parts

When integrating algebraic $\times$ transcendental functions: $\int P_n(x) \cdot g(x)\,dx$ where $P_n(x)$ is a polynomial. * Algorithm: 1. Create two columns: $D$ (Differentiate) and $I$ (Integrate). 2. Differentiate $P_n(x)$ repeatedly until it reaches $0$. 3. Integrate $g(x)$ the same number of times. 4. Assign alternating signs starting with $+$, then $-, +, -, \dots$ to the diagonal pairings. * Example: $\int x^3 \cos(2x)\,dx$

$$ \begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{Sign} & D & I \\ \hline + & x^3 & \cos(2x) \\ - & 3x^2 & \frac{1}{2}\sin(2x) \\ + & 6x & -\frac{1}{4}\cos(2x) \\ - & 6 & -\frac{1}{8}\sin(2x) \\ + & 0 & \frac{1}{16}\cos(2x) \end{array} $$

$$ \implies I = \frac{x^3\sin(2x)}{2} + \frac{3x^2\cos(2x)}{4} - \frac{6x\sin(2x)}{8} - \frac{6\cos(2x)}{16} + C $$

1.2 Cover-Up Rule for Non-Repeated Linear Partial Fractions

To find constants in $\frac{P(x)}{(x-a)(x-b)(x-c)} = \frac{A}{x-a} + \frac{B}{x-b} + \frac{C}{x-c}$: * To compute $A$: Cover $(x-a)$ on the LHS and substitute $x = a$.

$$ A = \left. \frac{P(x)}{(x-b)(x-c)} \right|_{x=a} $$

  • Speed Hack for Integrals:

$$ \int \frac{dx}{(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)} = \frac{\ln|x-1|}{(1-2)(1-3)} + \frac{\ln|x-2|}{(2-1)(2-3)} + \frac{\ln|x-3|}{(3-1)(3-2)} + C $$

$$ = \frac{1}{2}\ln|x-1| - \ln|x-2| + \frac{1}{2}\ln|x-3| + C $$

1.3 Rapid Special Forms & Pattern Recognition

  1. Exponential Distribution Property:

$$ \int e^x \big[f(x) + f'(x)\big]\,dx = e^x f(x) + C $$

Generalized form:

$$ \int e^{kx} \big[k f(x) + f'(x)\big]\,dx = e^{kx} f(x) + C $$

  1. Derivative-over-Function Shortcuts:

$$ \int \frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\,dx = \ln|f(x)| + C \qquad\text{and}\qquad \int \frac{f'(x)}{\sqrt{f(x)}}\,dx = 2\sqrt{f(x)} + C $$

$$ \int [f(x)]^n f'(x)\,dx = \frac{[f(x)]^{n+1}}{n+1} + C \quad (n \neq -1) $$

  1. Algebraic Twin Fractions:

$$ \int \frac{x^2 \pm 1}{x^4 + kx^2 + 1}\,dx $$

Divide numerator and denominator by $x^2$:

$$ \frac{1 \pm \frac{1}{x^2}}{x^2 + k + \frac{1}{x^2}} = \frac{d\left(x \mp \frac{1}{x}\right)}{\left(x \mp \frac{1}{x}\right)^2 + (k \pm 2)} $$

1.4 Symmetry & Definite Integral "King’s Property" Short-Circuits

  • Property IV (King's Rule):

$$ I = \int_a^b \frac{f(x)}{f(x) + f(a+b-x)}\,dx = \frac{b-a}{2} $$

Applicable directly in MCQs for:

$$ \int_0^{\pi/2} \frac{\sin^n x}{\sin^n x + \cos^n x}\,dx = \frac{\pi/2 - 0}{2} = \frac{\pi}{4} $$

$$ \int_2^3 \frac{\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{5-x}}\,dx = \frac{3-2}{2} = \frac{1}{2} $$

  • Removal of $x$ (Jack’s Rule): If $f(a+b-x) = f(x)$, then:

$$ \int_a^b x f(x)\,dx = \frac{a+b}{2} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx $$

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

In CBSE paper correction, marks are awarded strictly based on the official CBSE Marking Scheme (MS). Examiners look for specific milestones. Losing $\frac{1}{2}$ mark or $1$ mark usually happens at these exact junctures:

Total Loss Potential per Question: 1/2 to 1 1/2 Marks
 ├── Indefinite Integral: Missing "+ C" (-1/2 mark)
 ├── Missing absolute value in logarithms: ln(x) instead of ln|x| (-1/2 mark)
 ├── Incomplete Partial Fractions: Direct integration without showing partial fraction form (-1 mark)
 ├── Definite Integral Substitution: Not changing limits of integration (-1/2 to -1 mark)
 └── Modulus Splitting: Not explicitly showing sign intervals (-1/2 mark)

2.1 The Missing $+ C$ Penalty

  • CBSE Policy: A deduction of $\frac{1}{2}$ mark is applied once across the section or on the specific step if an indefinite integral lacks the arbitrary constant of integration $+ C$.
  • Examiner Insight: If a student solves $I = \int f(x)\,dx$ through 3 substitutions and writes $F(x)$ at the end without $+ C$, the final answer step mark ($1/2$ or $1$) is curtailed.

2.2 Omission of the Absolute Value Sign in Logarithms

  • Mistake: Writing $\int \frac{dx}{x} = \log(x) + C$ or $\int \tan x\,dx = \log(\sec x) + C$.
  • Requirement: You must write $\ln|x| + C$ or $\log|\sec x| + C$.
  • Reason: In $\mathbb{R}$, the logarithmic function is strictly defined for positive reals: $\text{Dom}(\ln) = (0, \infty)$. Omitting $|\cdot|$ invites a direct $\frac{1}{2}$ mark deduction.

2.3 Limits of Integration Under Variable Substitution

  • Mistake:

$$ I = \int_0^1 x e^{x^2}\,dx \quad\longrightarrow\quad \text{Let } t = x^2 \implies dt = 2x\,dx $$

Writing:

$$ I = \frac{1}{2} \int_0^1 e^t\,dt \quad \text{(leaving limits unstated or unconverted)} $$

  • The Penalty: If limits are left as $x$-values while the differential is written as $dt$, examiners can deduct up to $1$ whole mark for incorrect mathematical notation, even if the student substitutes back $x^2$ before evaluating.
  • Standard Representation to Guarantee Full Marks:

$$ \text{Let } t = x^2 \implies dt = 2x\,dx $$

$$ \text{When } x = 0 \implies t = 0; \quad \text{When } x = 1 \implies t = 1^2 = 1 $$

2.4 Modulus Functions: Splitting Intervals Without Sign Verification

  • Trap Example: $\int_{-1}^2 |x^3 - x|\,dx$
  • Required Presentation:
  • Solve $x^3 - x = 0 \implies x(x-1)(x+1) = 0 \implies x = -1, 0, 1$.
  • Test signs explicitly on each sub-interval:
    • For $x \in (-1, 0)$: $x^3 - x > 0 \implies |x^3 - x| = x^3 - x$
    • For $x \in (0, 1)$: $x^3 - x < 0 \implies |x^3 - x| = -(x^3 - x)$
    • For $x \in (1, 2)$: $x^3 - x > 0 \implies |x^3 - x| = x^3 - x$
  • Write the split integral:

$$ \int_{-1}^2 |x^3 - x|\,dx = \int_{-1}^0 (x^3 - x)\,dx - \int_0^1 (x^3 - x)\,dx + \int_1^2 (x^3 - x)\,dx $$

  • Pitfall: Skipping the sign-testing step leads to an algebraic arithmetic error where zero step-marks are awarded for the final integration.

2.5 Improper Partial Fraction Decomposition

  • Trap: Forgetting to check if the rational function is proper ($\deg(P) < \deg(Q)$).

$$ \int \frac{x^3 + x + 1}{x^2 - 1}\,dx $$

  • Deduction: If a student attempts $\frac{x^3+x+1}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{x+1}$, zero marks are awarded for the entire question.
  • Rule: First perform long division:

$$ \frac{x^3 + x + 1}{x^2 - 1} = x + \frac{2x + 1}{x^2 - 1} $$

2.6 Inverse Trigonometric Function Range Traps

  • Trap: $\int \sqrt{1 - \cos(2x)}\,dx = \int \sqrt{2\sin^2 x}\,dx = \sqrt{2}\int \sin x\,dx$
  • The Catch: $\sqrt{\sin^2 x} = |\sin x|$, not $\sin x$.
  • If the question has limits in $[\frac{\pi}{2}, \pi]$, $\sin x > 0$; but if limits fall in $[\pi, \frac{3\pi}{2}]$, then $\sqrt{\sin^2 x} = -\sin x$. Missing the sign causes a sign error in the final answer.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

3.1 Continuity Requirement in the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

  • Theorem: If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $F'(x) = f(x)$, then:

$$ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) $$

  • Examiner Warning: If $f(x)$ has an infinite discontinuity inside $(a, b)$, FTC cannot be applied directly.
  • Classic Counter-Example: $\int_{-1}^1 \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx \neq \left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{-1}^1 = -1 - (1) = -2$.
  • The integral diverges because $\frac{1}{x^2} \to \infty$ at $x = 0$.

3.2 Odd/Even Property Restrictions

$$ I = \int_{-a}^a f(x)\,dx = \begin{cases} 2\int_0^a f(x)\,dx, & \text{if } f(-x) = f(x) \text{ (Even)} \\ 0, & \text{if } f(-x) = -f(x) \text{ (Odd)} \end{cases} $$

  • Examiner Step Requirement: You must explicitly state and prove whether the integrand is even or odd:

$$ \text{Let } f(x) = \sin^5 x \cos^4 x \implies f(-x) = \sin^5(-x) \cos^4(-x) = -\sin^5 x \cos^4 x = -f(x) $$

$$ \therefore f(x) \text{ is an odd function. Hence, by property, } I = 0. $$

Skipping the explicit test of $f(-x)$ costs $\frac{1}{2}$ mark.

3.3 The Periodicity Property

$$ \int_0^{2a} f(x)\,dx = \begin{cases} 2\int_0^a f(x)\,dx, & \text{if } f(2a-x) = f(x) \\ 0, & \text{if } f(2a-x) = -f(x) \end{cases} $$

  • Must not be confused with the odd/even property. Here the shift is around $x = a$, evaluated at $2a - x$.

3.4 Integration by Parts Hierarchy (ILATE)

  • Order of priority for selecting First Function $u(x)$:

$$ \mathbf{I} \text{ (Inverse Trig)} > \mathbf{L} \text{ (Logarithmic)} > \mathbf{A} \text{ (Algebraic)} > \mathbf{T} \text{ (Trigonometric)} > \mathbf{E} \text{ (Exponential)} $$

  • Formula Formulation Requirement:

$$ \int u \cdot v\,dx = u \int v\,dx - \int \left( \frac{du}{dx} \int v\,dx \right) dx $$

  • Board Presentation Rule: Write this general formula on the side margin in a box. It secures partial marks even if a minor algebraic error occurs down the line.

5. Exemplary Presentation Template for Board Examiners

When evaluating subjective problems, examiners follow this exact step-mark breakdown:

$$ \mathbf{Example:} \quad \text{Evaluate } I = \int_0^{\pi} \frac{x\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x}\,dx $$

  1. Step 1: State the integral and identity (1 Mark)

$$ I = \int_0^{\pi} \frac{x\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x}\,dx \quad \tag{1} $$

Using property $\int_0^a f(x)\,dx = \int_0^a f(a-x)\,dx$:

$$ I = \int_0^{\pi} \frac{(\pi - x)\sin(\pi - x)}{1 + \cos^2(\pi - x)}\,dx = \int_0^{\pi} \frac{(\pi - x)\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x}\,dx \quad \tag{2} $$

  1. Step 2: Add equations to eliminate $x$ (1 Mark) Adding $(1)$ and $(2)$:

$$ 2I = \pi \int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x}\,dx \implies I = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_0^{\pi} \frac{\sin x}{1 + \cos^2 x}\,dx $$

  1. Step 3: Substitution and limit change (1 Mark) Let $t = \cos x \implies dt = -\sin x\,dx$.
  2. Limits: When $x = 0 \implies t = 1$; When $x = \pi \implies t = -1$.

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_1^{-1} \frac{-dt}{1 + t^2} = \frac{\pi}{2} \int_{-1}^1 \frac{dt}{1 + t^2} $$

  1. Step 4: Final evaluation with limits (1 Mark)

$$ I = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \tan^{-1} t \right]_{-1}^1 = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \tan^{-1}(1) - \tan^{-1}(-1) \right] = \frac{\pi}{2} \left[ \frac{\pi}{4} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) \right] = \frac{\pi^2}{4} $$
