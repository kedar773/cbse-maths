Direction Cosines and Direction Ratios of Lines

Three Dimensional Geometry: Direction Cosines and Direction Ratios of Lines

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Foundations

1.1 Direction Angles and Direction Cosines of a Directed Line

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Fundamental concept underpinning the entire 3D vector and Cartesian geometry)

Let $L$ be a directed line passing through the origin $O$ in a right-handed Cartesian coordinate system $OXYZ$.

         ^
         |      / L (Directed line)
         |    /
         |  /  gamma
         |/___________> Y
        / \  beta
       /   \

    X

Definition 1.1.1 (Direction Angles)

If the directed line $L$ passing through origin $O$ makes angles $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ with the positive directions of the coordinate axes $OX$, $OY$, and $OZ$ respectively, measured in the interval $[0, \pi]$, then $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are called the direction angles of the line $L$.

Definition 1.1.2 (Direction Cosines - DC)

The cosines of these direction angles, denoted by:

$$ l = \cos\alpha, \quad m = \cos\beta, \quad n = \cos\gamma $$

are called the direction cosines of the directed line $L$.

  • Reversal of Sense: If the direction of the line $L$ is reversed, the angles made with the positive coordinate axes become $\pi - \alpha$, $\pi - \beta$, and $\pi - \gamma$. Consequently, the new direction cosines are:

$$ \cos(\pi - \alpha) = -\cos\alpha = -l $$

$$ \cos(\pi - \beta) = -\cos\beta = -m $$

$$ \cos(\pi - \gamma) = -\cos\gamma = -n $$

  • Undirected Line Convention: A line in 3D space extended indefinitely in both directions has two opposite sets of direction cosines: $(l, m, n)$ or $(-l, -m, -n)$. To uniquely specify a directed ray, one set is fixed.

1.2 Direction Ratios (DRs) of a Line

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Essential for forming lines, planes, and computing intersection angles)

Definition 1.2.1 (Direction Ratios)

Any three numbers $a, b, c$ which are proportional to the direction cosines $l, m, n$ of a line are called the direction ratios (or direction numbers) of the line:

$$ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = \lambda \quad (\lambda \neq 0) $$

  • Direction cosines are unique up to a global sign $(\pm l, \pm m, \pm n)$, but direction ratios are infinite in number because $\lambda$ can be any non-zero real scalar.
  • Any vector parallel to the line, $\vec{v} = a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}$, directly provides the direction ratios $\langle a, b, c \rangle$.
  • If $\vec{u}$ is a unit vector along the line, its components are precisely the direction cosines:

$$ \hat{u} = l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k} $$

1.3 Direction Cosines of Coordinate Axes

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐

  • X-axis: Makes angles $0, \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}$ with $OX, OY, OZ$.

$$ l = \cos 0 = 1, \quad m = \cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0, \quad n = \cos\frac{\pi}{2} = 0 \implies \langle 1, 0, 0 \rangle $$

  • Y-axis: Makes angles $\frac{\pi}{2}, 0, \frac{\pi}{2}$ with $OX, OY, OZ$.

$$ l = 0, \quad m = 1, \quad n = 0 \implies \langle 0, 1, 0 \rangle $$

  • Z-axis: Makes angles $\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}, 0$ with $OX, OY, OZ$.

$$ l = 0, \quad m = 0, \quad n = 1 \implies \langle 0, 0, 1 \rangle $$

Theorem 2.1: Fundamental Identity of Direction Cosines

Statement: If $l, m, n$ are the direction cosines of any line, then:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

         v       \   |
        X         \  |
                   \ |

                     N

Proof:

  1. Given: A directed line $L$ passing through origin $O(0, 0, 0)$ with direction angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ and direction cosines $l = \cos\alpha$, $m = \cos\beta$, $n = \cos\gamma$.
  2. Construction:
  3. Take an arbitrary point $P(x, y, z)$ on line $L$.
  4. Let the distance $OP = r = |\vec{r}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$.
  5. Draw perpendicular $PA$ from $P$ onto the positive $X$-axis. Then $OA = x$, and $\triangle OAP$ is a right-angled triangle with the right angle at $A$.
  6. Geometric Analysis: In the right-angled triangle $\triangle OAP$:

$$ \cos\alpha = \frac{\text{Adjacent}}{\text{Hypotenuse}} = \frac{OA}{OP} = \frac{x}{r} \implies x = lr $$

Similarly, dropping perpendiculars from $P$ onto the $Y$-axis and $Z$-axis yields:

$$ \cos\beta = \frac{y}{r} \implies y = mr $$

$$ \cos\gamma = \frac{z}{r} \implies z = nr $$

  1. Vector / Algebraic Relation: The position vector of $P$ is:

$$ \vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k} = (lr)\hat{i} + (mr)\hat{j} + (nr)\hat{k} = r(l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k}) $$

Taking the magnitude squared on both sides:

$$ |\vec{r}|^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 $$

Substitute $x = lr$, $y = mr$, $z = nr$:

$$ r^2 = (lr)^2 + (mr)^2 + (nr)^2 $$

$$ r^2 = r^2(l^2 + m^2 + n^2) $$

Since $P \neq O$, $r > 0 \implies r^2 \neq 0$. Dividing both sides by $r^2$:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

$$ \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Corollaries:

  • In terms of sines:

$$ (1 - \sin^2\alpha) + (1 - \sin^2\beta) + (1 - \sin^2\gamma) = 1 $$

$$ \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma = 2 $$

  • In terms of double angles:

$$ \frac{1 + \cos 2\alpha}{2} + \frac{1 + \cos 2\beta}{2} + \frac{1 + \cos 2\gamma}{2} = 1 $$

$$ \cos 2\alpha + \cos 2\beta + \cos 2\gamma = -1 $$

Theorem 2.2: Relation Between Direction Cosines and Direction Ratios

Statement: If $a, b, c$ are the direction ratios of a line, then its direction cosines are:

$$ l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad m = \pm \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad n = \pm \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

(where either all positive signs or all negative signs are chosen simultaneously).

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Proof:

  1. Given: Real numbers $a, b, c$ such that:

$$ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = k \quad (k \neq 0) $$

  1. Expressing DCs in terms of $k$:

$$ l = ak, \quad m = bk, \quad n = ck $$

  1. Applying Theorem 2.1:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

$$ (ak)^2 + (bk)^2 + (ck)^2 = 1 $$

$$ k^2(a^2 + b^2 + c^2) = 1 $$

$$ k^2 = \frac{1}{a^2 + b^2 + c^2} $$

$$ k = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

  1. Conclusion: Substituting $k$ back into $l, m, n$:

$$ l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

$$ m = \pm \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

$$ n = \pm \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.3: Direction Cosines and Ratios of a Line Passing Through Two Points

Statement: The direction ratios of the line segment joining $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ are proportional to $(x_2 - x_1), (y_2 - y_1), (z_2 - z_1)$, and its direction cosines are:

$$ l = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{PQ}, \quad m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{PQ}, \quad n = \frac{z_2 - z_1}{PQ} $$

where $PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}$.

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Proof:

  1. Given: Two distinct points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$.
  2. Vector Method: The displacement vector directed from $P$ to $Q$ is:

$$ \vec{PQ} = \vec{r}_Q - \vec{r}_P = (x_2 - x_1)\hat{i} + (y_2 - y_1)\hat{j} + (z_2 - z_1)\hat{k} $$

  1. Magnitude:

$$ |\vec{PQ}| = d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2} $$

  1. Unit Vector Determination: The unit vector $\hat{u}$ in the direction of $\vec{PQ}$ is:

$$ \hat{u} = \frac{\vec{PQ}}{|\vec{PQ}|} = \left(\frac{x_2 - x_1}{d}\right)\hat{i} + \left(\frac{y_2 - y_1}{d}\right)\hat{j} + \left(\frac{z_2 - z_1}{d}\right)\hat{k} $$

  1. Comparison with Standard Form: Any unit vector is uniquely expressed as $\hat{u} = l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k}$, where $l, m, n$ are the direction cosines. Equating coefficients:

$$ l = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{d}, \quad m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{d}, \quad n = \frac{z_2 - z_1}{d} $$

Since the denominator $d$ is constant for all three components:

$$ \frac{l}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{m}{y_2 - y_1} = \frac{n}{z_2 - z_1} = \frac{1}{d} $$

Hence, $(x_2 - x_1), (y_2 - y_1), (z_2 - z_1)$ are the direction ratios of the line passing through $P$ and $Q$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.4: Angle Between Two Directed Lines

Statement: Let two lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ have direction cosines $(l_1, m_1, n_1)$ and $(l_2, m_2, n_2)$ (or direction ratios $(a_1, b_1, c_1)$ and $(a_2, b_2, c_2)$). The acute angle $\theta$ between them is given by:

$$ \cos\theta = |l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2| = \frac{|a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2|}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + b_1^2 + c_1^2}\sqrt{a_2^2 + b_2^2 + c_2^2}} $$

Board Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Proof:

  1. Let $\hat{u}_1$ and $\hat{u}_2$ be unit vectors parallel to lines $L_1$ and $L_2$:

$$ \hat{u}_1 = l_1\hat{i} + m_1\hat{j} + n_1\hat{k}, \quad \hat{u}_2 = l_2\hat{i} + m_2\hat{j} + n_2\hat{k} $$

  1. By the scalar dot product definition:

$$ \hat{u}_1 \cdot \hat{u}_2 = |\hat{u}_1| |\hat{u}_2| \cos\theta = (1)(1)\cos\theta = \cos\theta $$

  1. Evaluating the dot product component-wise:

$$ \hat{u}_1 \cdot \hat{u}_2 = l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2 $$

$$ \implies \cos\theta = l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2 $$

For the acute angle between two geometric lines:

$$ \cos\theta = |l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2| $$

  1. In terms of direction ratios:

$$ l_i = \frac{a_i}{\sqrt{a_i^2 + b_i^2 + c_i^2}}, \quad m_i = \frac{b_i}{\sqrt{a_i^2 + b_i^2 + c_i^2}}, \quad n_i = \frac{c_i}{\sqrt{a_i^2 + b_i^2 + c_i^2}} \quad (i=1, 2) $$

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{|a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2|}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + b_1^2 + c_1^2}\sqrt{a_2^2 + b_2^2 + c_2^2}} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Critical Conditions Derived:

  1. Condition of Perpendicularity (Orthogonality):

$$ \theta = \frac{\pi}{2} \iff \cos\theta = 0 \iff l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2 = 0 \iff a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2 = 0 $$

  1. Condition of Parallelism (Collinearity):

$$ \theta = 0 \text{ or } \pi \iff \frac{l_1}{l_2} = \frac{m_1}{m_2} = \frac{n_1}{n_2} \iff \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} $$

Vector and Cartesian Equations of a Line in Space

Class 12 Mathematics: Three Dimensional Geometry

Topic: Vector and Cartesian Equations of a Line in Space

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Uniqueness of a Line in Space

In Euclidean 3-space ($\mathbb{R}^3$), an infinite number of lines can pass through a single point. A straight line is uniquely determined if: 1. Case I: It passes through a given point and has a given direction (parallel to a given vector / fixed direction ratios). [Priority: $\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar$] 2. Case II: It passes through two distinct given points. [Priority: $\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar$]

Geometric Interpretation: A line is a 1-dimensional continuum. Specifying a point anchors the line at a definite position in space; specifying a direction vector or a second point restricts its rotational freedom, completely freezing its position and orientation in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

1.2 Direction Cosines (DC) and Direction Ratios (DR) of a Line [Priority: $\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar$]

  • Direction Angles: If a directed line $L$ passing through the origin makes angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ with the positive directions of the coordinate axes $x, y, z$ respectively, then $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in [0, \pi]$ are called the direction angles.
  • Direction Cosines (DCs): The cosines of these angles are denoted by:

$$ l = \cos\alpha, \quad m = \cos\beta, \quad n = \cos\gamma $$

Fundamental Identity:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

  • Direction Ratios (DRs): Any three real numbers $a, b, c$ proportional to the direction cosines $l, m, n$ are called the direction ratios of the line:

$$ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

Hence:

$$ l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad m = \pm \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}, \quad n = \pm \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

NCERT Distinction Note: Direction ratios of a line are not unique (infinitely many sets of triplets exist for a single line, scaled by any non-zero scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$), whereas the direction cosines are unique up to a sign factor $\pm$ depending on the sense of direction along the line.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Derivations

Derivation 1: Equation of a Line Passing Through a Given Point and Parallel to a Given Vector [Priority: $\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar$]

A. Vector Form

  • Given: A line $L$ passes through a fixed point $A$ with position vector $\vec{a} = x_1\hat{i} + y_1\hat{j} + z_1\hat{k}$ and is parallel to a non-zero vector $\vec{b} = a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}$.
  • To Prove: The position vector $\vec{r}$ of any arbitrary point $P$ on $L$ satisfies:

$$ \vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b}, \quad \lambda \in \mathbb{R} $$

  • Construction / Setup: Let $O$ be the origin of the 3D Cartesian system. Let $P(x, y, z)$ be an arbitrary point on the line $L$ with position vector $\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$. Join $OA$ and $OP$.
           P (r)
          /
         /
        A (a)   ||   b (direction vector)
       /
      /
     O (Origin)

Proof: 1. By triangle law of vector addition in $\triangle OAP$:

$$ \vec{OA} + \vec{AP} = \vec{OP} $$

$$ \vec{AP} = \vec{OP} - \vec{OA} = \vec{r} - \vec{a} $$

  1. Since the line $L$ contains the points $A$ and $P$, the vector $\vec{AP}$ is collinear (parallel) to the given vector $\vec{b}$.
  2. By the condition of collinearity of two vectors, there exists a unique scalar parameter $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$ \vec{AP} = \lambda\vec{b} $$

  1. Substituting $\vec{AP} = \vec{r} - \vec{a}$:

$$ \vec{r} - \vec{a} = \lambda\vec{b} $$

$$ \vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b}, \quad \lambda \in \mathbb{R} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

B. Cartesian Form (Symmetrical Form)

Proof: 1. Express $\vec{r}, \vec{a},$ and $\vec{b}$ in rectangular component forms:

$$ \vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{a} = x_1\hat{i} + y_1\hat{j} + z_1\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{b} = a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k} $$

  1. Substitute into the vector equation $\vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b}$:

$$ x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k} = (x_1\hat{i} + y_1\hat{j} + z_1\hat{k}) + \lambda(a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}) $$

$$ x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k} = (x_1 + \lambda a)\hat{i} + (y_1 + \lambda b)\hat{j} + (z_1 + \lambda c)\hat{k} $$

  1. Equating the corresponding coefficients of $\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$:

$$ x = x_1 + \lambda a \implies \lambda = \frac{x - x_1}{a} $$

$$ y = y_1 + \lambda b \implies \lambda = \frac{y - y_1}{b} $$

$$ z = z_1 + \lambda c \implies \lambda = \frac{z - z_1}{c} $$

  1. Eliminating the scalar parameter $\lambda$ gives the standard Cartesian equation:

$$ \frac{x - x_1}{a} = \frac{y - y_1}{b} = \frac{z - z_1}{c} \quad (= \lambda) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Remark (When a DR component is zero): If $a = 0, b \neq 0, c \neq 0$, the line lies in the plane $x = x_1$. The equation is written as:

$$ x = x_1, \quad \frac{y - y_1}{b} = \frac{z - z_1}{c} $$

Derivation 2: Equation of a Line Passing Through Two Distinct Given Points [Priority: $\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar\bigstar$]

A. Vector Form

  • Given: Line $L$ passes through points $A(\vec{a})$ and $B(\vec{b})$, where $\vec{a} \neq \vec{b}$.
  • To Prove:

$$ \vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda(\vec{b} - \vec{a}), \quad \lambda \in \mathbb{R} $$

Proof: 1. Let $P(\vec{r})$ be any point on the line $L$. 2. The vector joining $A$ and $B$ is:

$$ \vec{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a} $$

  1. The vector joining $A$ and $P$ is:

$$ \vec{AP} = \vec{r} - \vec{a} $$

  1. Since $A, B,$ and $P$ are collinear points on the same line $L$, the vector $\vec{AP}$ is collinear with $\vec{AB}$.
  2. Therefore, there exists a real parameter $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$ \vec{AP} = \lambda\vec{AB} $$

$$ \vec{r} - \vec{a} = \lambda(\vec{b} - \vec{a}) $$

$$ \vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda(\vec{b} - \vec{a}) $$

(Alternatively: $\vec{r} = (1 - \lambda)\vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b}$)

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

B. Cartesian Form

Proof: 1. Let $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$. 2. The direction ratios of line $AB$ are given by the components of $\vec{b} - \vec{a}$:

$$ a = (x_2 - x_1), \quad b = (y_2 - y_1), \quad c = (z_2 - z_1) $$

  1. Substituting these DRs into the single-point Cartesian equation:

$$ \frac{x - x_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{y - y_1}{y_2 - y_1} = \frac{z - z_1}{z_2 - z_1} \quad (= \lambda) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Angle Between Two Lines, Parallelism and Perpendicularity

Three Dimensional Geometry (Class 12 Mathematics)

Teaching Note: Angle Between Two Lines, Parallelism, and Perpendicularity

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Core Geometric Concept

In three-dimensional Euclidean space, the angle between two lines (whether intersecting or skew) is defined as the angle between two intersecting lines drawn through an arbitrary point (often the origin) parallel to the given lines.

By convention in CBSE Class 12 and NCERT:

The angle $\theta$ between two lines is defined as the acute angle between their direction vectors, so $0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$.

1.2 Direction Cosines (DC) vs. Direction Ratios (DR)

  • Direction Cosines ($l, m, n$): If a directed line makes angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ with the positive $x, y, z$-axes respectively, then:

$$ l = \cos\alpha, \quad m = \cos\beta, \quad n = \cos\gamma $$

Fundamental identity:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

Unit vector along the line: $\hat{u} = l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k}$.

  • Direction Ratios ($a, b, c$): Any three real numbers proportional to the direction cosines:

$$ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} $$

Vector along the line: $\vec{b} = a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}$.

Derivation 1: Vector Form of Angle Between Two Lines ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be two straight lines whose vector equations are:

$$ L_1: \vec{r} = \vec{a}_1 + \lambda \vec{b}_1 $$

$$ L_2: \vec{r} = \vec{a}_2 + \mu \vec{b}_2 $$

where $\vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{b}_2$ are non-zero vectors parallel to $L_1$ and $L_2$ respectively, and $\lambda, \mu \in \mathbb{R}$. The acute angle $\theta$ between $L_1$ and $L_2$ is given by:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{|\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2|}{|\vec{b}_1||\vec{b}_2|} $$

Proof

  1. Given: Two lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ directed along vectors $\vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{b}_2$.
  2. Construction / Geometry: Translate vectors $\vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{b}_2$ such that their initial points coincide at the origin $O$. Let $\vec{b}_1 = \vec{OP}$ and $\vec{b}_2 = \vec{OQ}$. The angle between $L_1$ and $L_2$ is equal to the angle $\theta$ between $\vec{OP}$ and $\vec{OQ}$.
  3. By definition of scalar (dot) product:

$$ \vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2 = |\vec{b}_1||\vec{b}_2|\cos\theta $$

  1. Solving for $\cos\theta$:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1||\vec{b}_2|} $$

  1. Acute Angle Convention: Since two intersecting lines form two supplementary angles ($\theta$ and $\pi - \theta$), by NCERT convention we report the acute angle. Hence:

$$ \cos\theta = \left|\frac{\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1||\vec{b}_2|}\right| = \frac{|\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2|}{|\vec{b}_1||\vec{b}_2|} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2: Cartesian Form of Angle Between Two Lines ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Let the Cartesian equations of two lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ be:

$$ L_1: \frac{x - x_1}{a_1} = \frac{y - y_1}{b_1} = \frac{z - z_1}{c_1} $$

$$ L_2: \frac{x - x_2}{a_2} = \frac{y - y_2}{b_2} = \frac{z - z_2}{c_2} $$

where $\langle a_1, b_1, c_1 \rangle$ and $\langle a_2, b_2, c_2 \rangle$ are the direction ratios of lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ respectively. The acute angle $\theta$ between them is given by:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{|a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2|}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + b_1^2 + c_1^2}\,\sqrt{a_2^2 + b_2^2 + c_2^2}} $$

Proof

  1. Express the direction vectors in component form:

$$ \vec{b}_1 = a_1\hat{i} + b_1\hat{j} + c_1\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{b}_2 = a_2\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + c_2\hat{k} $$

  1. Compute the dot product $\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2$:

$$ \vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2 = (a_1\hat{i} + b_1\hat{j} + c_1\hat{k}) \cdot (a_2\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + c_2\hat{k}) = a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2 $$

  1. Compute the magnitudes $|\vec{b}_1|$ and $|\vec{b}_2|$:

$$ |\vec{b}_1| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + b_1^2 + c_1^2} $$

$$ |\vec{b}_2| = \sqrt{a_2^2 + b_2^2 + c_2^2} $$

  1. Substituting into the vector formula:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{|a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2|}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + b_1^2 + c_1^2}\,\sqrt{a_2^2 + b_2^2 + c_2^2}} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Corollary (When Direction Cosines are Given): If $\langle l_1, m_1, n_1 \rangle$ and $\langle l_2, m_2, n_2 \rangle$ are direction cosines: Since $l_1^2 + m_1^2 + n_1^2 = 1$ and $l_2^2 + m_2^2 + n_2^2 = 1$:

$$ \cos\theta = |l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2| $$

Derivation 3: Deduction of Perpendicularity Condition ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Two lines with direction ratios $\langle a_1, b_1, c_1 \rangle$ and $\langle a_2, b_2, c_2 \rangle$ are mutually perpendicular ($L_1 \perp L_2$) if and only if:

$$ a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2 = 0 $$

Proof

  1. Lines are perpendicular $\iff \theta = 90^\circ \iff \cos\theta = \cos 90^\circ = 0$.
  2. Using the angle formula:

$$ \frac{|a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2|}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + b_1^2 + c_1^2}\,\sqrt{a_2^2 + b_2^2 + c_2^2}} = 0 $$

  1. Since direction ratios define valid non-zero lines, denominators are strictly positive ($|\vec{b}_1| \neq 0, |\vec{b}_2| \neq 0$).
  2. Therefore, the numerator must be identically zero:

$$ a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2 = 0 $$

In terms of direction cosines:

$$ l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2 = 0 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 4: Deduction of Parallelism Condition ⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Two lines with direction ratios $\langle a_1, b_1, c_1 \rangle$ and $\langle a_2, b_2, c_2 \rangle$ are parallel ($L_1 \parallel L_2$) if and only if:

$$ \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} $$

Proof

  1. $L_1 \parallel L_2 \iff \vec{b}_1$ is collinear with $\vec{b}_2$.
  2. By vector collinearity, there exists a non-zero scalar $k \in \mathbb{R}$ such that:

$$ \vec{b}_1 = k \vec{b}_2 $$

  1. In component form:

$$ a_1\hat{i} + b_1\hat{j} + c_1\hat{k} = k(a_2\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + c_2\hat{k}) $$

  1. Equating respective components:

$$ a_1 = k a_2, \quad b_1 = k b_2, \quad c_1 = k c_2 $$

  1. Eliminating $k$:

$$ \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} = k $$

In terms of direction cosines:

$$ l_1 = \pm l_2, \quad m_1 = \pm m_2, \quad n_1 = \pm n_2 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Shortest Distance Between Skew Lines and Parallel Lines

Class 12 Three-Dimensional Geometry: Shortest Distance Between Two Lines

1. Pedagogical Theory, Geometric Intuition & Core Definitions

In two-dimensional Euclidean geometry, two distinct coplanar straight lines either intersect at a unique point or are parallel. However, in three-dimensional space, lines do not necessarily lie in the same plane.

1.1 Classification of Pairs of Lines in Space

Given two distinct straight lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ in $\mathbb{R}^3$:

  1. Intersecting Lines: The lines lie in a common plane (coplanar) and share exactly one common point.

$$ \text{Distance } d = 0 $$

  1. Parallel Lines: The lines lie in a common plane (coplanar) and do not intersect, regardless of extension. Their direction vectors are collinear:

$$ \vec{b}_1 = k\vec{b}_2 \quad (k \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}) $$

  1. Skew Lines: Lines in space that are neither parallel nor intersecting.
  2. Skew lines are strictly non-coplanar; no single plane can contain both lines simultaneously.

1.2 The Line of Shortest Distance

  • Definition: Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be two skew lines. There exists a unique line segment $PQ$ with endpoint $P$ on $L_1$ and endpoint $Q$ on $L_2$ such that $PQ$ is perpendicular to both $L_1$ and $L_2$.
  • The length of this segment $|\vec{PQ}|$ is the shortest distance between $L_1$ and $L_2$.
  • The straight line containing segment $PQ$ is called the line of shortest distance.

2. Rigorous Derivations & Mathematical Proofs

Theorem 1: Shortest Distance Between Two Skew Lines (Vector Form) [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Statement

Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be two skew lines with vector equations:

$$ L_1: \vec{r} = \vec{a}_1 + \lambda \vec{b}_1 $$

$$ L_2: \vec{r} = \vec{a}_2 + \mu \vec{b}_2 $$

where $\vec{a}_1, \vec{a}_2$ are position vectors of fixed points $A$ and $B$ on $L_1$ and $L_2$ respectively, and $\vec{b}_1, \vec{b}_2$ are their non-zero, non-collinear direction vectors. The shortest distance $d$ between $L_1$ and $L_2$ is:

$$ d = \left| \frac{(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|} \right| $$

Proof

1. Given: - Line $L_1$ passes through $A(\vec{a}_1)$ parallel to $\vec{b}_1$. - Line $L_2$ passes through $B(\vec{a}_2)$ parallel to $\vec{b}_2$. - $L_1$ and $L_2$ are non-parallel ($\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 \neq \vec{0}$) and non-intersecting.

2. Construction: - Let $PQ$ be the unique line segment of shortest distance between $L_1$ and $L_2$, with $P$ lying on $L_1$ and $Q$ lying on $L_2$. - Join point $A$ on $L_1$ to point $B$ on $L_2$ to form the displacement vector $\vec{AB} = \vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1$.

3. Orthogonality Condition: Since $PQ$ represents the shortest distance, segment $\vec{PQ}$ is perpendicular to both $L_1$ and $L_2$:

$$ \vec{PQ} \perp \vec{b}_1 \quad \text{and} \quad \vec{PQ} \perp \vec{b}_2 $$

Therefore, $\vec{PQ}$ is parallel to the normal vector $\vec{n}$ defined by the cross product of the direction vectors:

$$ \vec{n} = \vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 $$

The unit vector $\hat{n}$ along the line of shortest distance is:

$$ \hat{n} = \frac{\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|} $$

4. Geometric Projection: The magnitude of the shortest distance segment $PQ$ equals the absolute value of the scalar projection of the arbitrary join vector $\vec{AB}$ onto the unit normal vector $\hat{n}$:

$$ d = |\text{Projection of } \vec{AB} \text{ on } \vec{PQ}| = |\vec{AB} \cdot \hat{n}| $$

5. Algebraic Substitution: Substitute $\vec{AB} = \vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1$ and $\hat{n} = \frac{\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|}$:

$$ d = \left| (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) \cdot \frac{\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|} \right| $$

Using the commutativity of the scalar dot product:

$$ d = \left| \frac{(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|} \right| $$

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

Corollary 1.1: Cartesian Formulation for Skew Lines

Let the equations of lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ be given in Cartesian form:

$$ L_1: \frac{x - x_1}{a_1} = \frac{y - y_1}{b_1} = \frac{z - z_1}{c_1} $$

$$ L_2: \frac{x - x_2}{a_2} = \frac{y - y_2}{b_2} = \frac{z - z_2}{c_2} $$

Here:

$$ \vec{a}_1 = x_1\hat{i} + y_1\hat{j} + z_1\hat{k}, \quad \vec{a}_2 = x_2\hat{i} + y_2\hat{j} + z_2\hat{k} \implies \vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1 = (x_2 - x_1)\hat{i} + (y_2 - y_1)\hat{j} + (z_2 - z_1)\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{b}_1 = a_1\hat{i} + b_1\hat{j} + c_1\hat{k}, \quad \vec{b}_2 = a_2\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + c_2\hat{k} $$

The numerator scalar triple product is given by the determinant:

$$ (\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) = \begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} $$

The cross product magnitude in the denominator expands by Lagrange's identity / component expansion:

$$ \vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = (b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1)\hat{i} - (a_1 c_2 - a_2 c_1)\hat{j} + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)\hat{k} $$

$$ |\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2| = \sqrt{(b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1)^2 + (c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1)^2 + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)^2} $$

Thus, the Cartesian formula is:

$$ d = \frac{\left| \begin{matrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{matrix} \right|}{\sqrt{(b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1)^2 + (c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1)^2 + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)^2}} $$

Corollary 1.2: Condition for Coplanarity / Intersection of Two Lines [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Two non-parallel lines $L_1$ and $L_2$ intersect if and only if the shortest distance between them is zero ($d = 0$).

$$ \left| \frac{(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|} \right| = 0 \iff (\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) = 0 $$

In Cartesian coordinates:

$$ \begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} = 0 $$

Theorem 2: Shortest Distance Between Two Parallel Lines [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Statement

Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be two parallel lines with vector equations:

$$ L_1: \vec{r} = \vec{a}_1 + \lambda \vec{b} $$

$$ L_2: \vec{r} = \vec{a}_2 + \mu \vec{b} $$

where both lines share the same direction vector $\vec{b}$ (or scalar multiples thereof). The perpendicular distance $d$ between them is:

$$ d = \frac{|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)|}{|\vec{b}|} $$

Proof

           Line L₂         B (a₂)
     ------------------------*----------------
                            /|
                           / |
              (a₂ - a₁)  /  | d
                         /   |
                        / θ  |
     ------------------*-----*----------------
           Line L₁    A (a₁) P       Vector b -->

1. Given: - Line $L_1$ passes through point $A(\vec{a}_1)$ along direction $\vec{b}$. - Line $L_2$ passes through point $B(\vec{a}_2)$ along the same direction $\vec{b}$. - Distinct lines: $\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1$ is not parallel to $\vec{b}$.

2. Construction: - From point $B$ on $L_2$, drop a perpendicular $BP$ onto $L_1$, meeting $L_1$ at foot $P$. - Length $BP = d$ is the shortest distance between the lines. - Join $A$ and $B$ to form vector $\vec{AB} = \vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1$. - Let $\theta$ be the interior angle between $\vec{AB}$ and vector $\vec{b}$.

3. Trigonometric Formulation: In right-angled triangle $\triangle APB$:

$$ d = |\vec{AB}| \sin\theta $$

4. Vector Cross Product Definition: By definition of the vector cross product between $\vec{b}$ and $\vec{AB}$:

$$ |\vec{b} \times \vec{AB}| = |\vec{b}| |\vec{AB}| \sin\theta $$

Substitute $|\vec{AB}| \sin\theta = d$ into the cross product identity:

$$ |\vec{b} \times \vec{AB}| = |\vec{b}| \cdot d $$

5. Isolating $d$:

$$ d = \frac{|\vec{b} \times \vec{AB}|}{|\vec{b}|} = \frac{|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)|}{|\vec{b}|} $$

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

💡 Example 3.1: Finding Direction Cosines from Equal Angles
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2020, 1 Mark] Problem: A line makes equal acute angles with the coordinate axes. Find its direction cosines.

Step-by-Step Solution: 1. Let the direction angles be $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$. 2. Given that the line makes equal acute angles:

$$ \alpha = \beta = \gamma \implies \cos\alpha = \cos\beta = \cos\gamma \implies l = m = n $$

  1. By the fundamental identity:

$$ l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

$$ l^2 + l^2 + l^2 = 1 \implies 3l^2 = 1 \implies l^2 = \frac{1}{3} \implies l = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} $$

  1. Since the angles are specified as acute, $\cos\alpha > 0$:

$$ l = m = n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} $$

Answer: The direction cosines are $\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)$.

💡 Example 3.2: Collinearity of Three Points Using Direction Ratios
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2022 Term-II, 2 Marks] Problem: Using direction ratios, prove that the points $A(2, 3, -4)$, $B(1, -2, 3)$, and $C(3, 8, -11)$ are collinear.

$$ a_1 = x_2 - x_1 = 1 - 2 = -1 $$

$$ b_1 = y_2 - y_1 = -2 - 3 = -5 $$

$$ c_1 = z_2 - z_1 = 3 - (-4) = 7 $$

$$ \implies \langle a_1, b_1, c_1 \rangle = \langle -1, -5, 7 \rangle $$

  1. Direction ratios of the line segment $BC$ are:

$$ a_2 = x_3 - x_2 = 3 - 1 = 2 $$

$$ b_2 = y_3 - y_2 = 8 - (-2) = 10 $$

$$ c_2 = z_3 - z_2 = -11 - 3 = -14 $$

$$ \implies \langle a_2, b_2, c_2 \rangle = \langle 2, 10, -14 \rangle $$

  1. Check proportionality:

$$ \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{-1}{2}, \quad \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{-5}{10} = -\frac{1}{2}, \quad \frac{c_1}{c_2} = \frac{7}{-14} = -\frac{1}{2} $$

$$ \frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} = -\frac{1}{2} $$

  1. Conclusion: The line segments $AB$ and $BC$ are parallel. Since point $B$ is common to both segments, $A, B$, and $C$ must lie on the same straight line. Hence Proved.

$$ \frac{a}{-2 - (-4)} = \frac{b}{0 - 1} = \frac{c}{2 - 0} $$

$$ \frac{a}{2} = \frac{b}{-1} = \frac{c}{2} $$

  3. $a_2 = -1, b_2 = 1, c_2 = 1$

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{|a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2|}{\sqrt{a_1^2 + b_1^2 + c_1^2}\sqrt{a_2^2 + b_2^2 + c_2^2}} $$

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{|(1)(-1) + (1)(1) + (1)(1)|}{\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2}\sqrt{(-1)^2 + 1^2 + 1^2}} = \frac{|-1 + 1 + 1|}{\sqrt{3}\sqrt{3}} = \frac{1}{3} $$

$$ \implies \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

1. Vector Projection Shortcut

The projection of a line segment connecting $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$ onto a line having direction cosines $(l, m, n)$ is computed in a single mental step:

$$ \text{Projection of } \vec{PQ} = |l(x_2 - x_1) + m(y_2 - y_1) + n(z_2 - z_1)| $$

2. The $\sin^2$ Quick Convert

If a question presents sum of squares of sines of direction angles:

$$ \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma = 2 $$

Example: If a line makes $45^\circ$ with $X$ and $60^\circ$ with $Y$, $\sin^2\gamma = 2 - \sin^2 45^\circ - \sin^2 60^\circ = 2 - \frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{4} \implies \sin\gamma = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \implies \gamma = 60^\circ \text{ or } 120^\circ$.

3. Normalization Rule for Rapid DR-to-DC Conversion

For recurring Pythagorean triples in 3D: * $\langle 1, 2, 2 \rangle \implies \sqrt{1^2 + 2^2 + 2^2} = 3 \implies \left\langle \frac{1}{3}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{3} \right\rangle$ * $\langle 2, 3, 6 \rangle \implies \sqrt{2^2 + 3^2 + 6^2} = 7 \implies \left\langle \frac{2}{7}, \frac{3}{7}, \frac{6}{7} \right\rangle$ * $\langle 1, 4, 8 \rangle \implies \sqrt{1^2 + 4^2 + 8^2} = 9 \implies \left\langle \frac{1}{9}, \frac{4}{9}, \frac{8}{9} \right\rangle$

4. Zero-Component Rule

If one of the direction cosines of a line is zero (e.g., $l = 0$): * Since $\cos\alpha = 0 \implies \alpha = 90^\circ$, the line is perpendicular to the $X$-axis. * Consequently, the line is parallel to or lies in the $YZ$-coordinate plane. * If two direction cosines are zero (e.g., $l = 0, m = 0$), then $n = \pm 1$, meaning the line is parallel to or coincides with the $Z$-axis.

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Blunders

Trap 1: Non-Unit Coefficients in Cartesian Form

Students often read DRs straight from the denominator without ensuring standard form:

$$ \text{Given: } \frac{3 - x}{4} = \frac{2y + 1}{6} = \frac{z}{2} $$

  • Mistake: Direction ratios taken as $\langle 4, 6, 2 \rangle$. ❌
  • Correction: Convert numerators to lead with $+1 \cdot \text{variable}$:

$$ \frac{x - 3}{-4} = \frac{y + \frac{1}{2}}{3} = \frac{z - 0}{2} \implies \text{DRs} = \langle -4, 3, 2 \rangle. \; \checkmark $$

Trap 2: Omitting Modulus in $\cos\theta$

  • Mistake: Calculating $\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1||\vec{b}_2|}$, obtaining a negative value (e.g., $-\frac{1}{2}$), and stating $\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$.
  • Correction: In CBSE Class 12, the angle between two straight lines is acute:

$$ \cos\theta = \left|\frac{\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1||\vec{b}_2|}\right| = \left|-\frac{1}{2}\right| = \frac{1}{2} \implies \theta = \frac{\pi}{3}. $$

Trap 3: Confusing Position Vectors ($\vec{a}$) with Direction Vectors ($\vec{b}$)

  • In $\vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b}$, the angle depends strictly on $\vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{b}_2$. $\vec{a}_1$ and $\vec{a}_2$ represent passing points and have zero impact on the angle.

  • When a direction ratio is zero (e.g., $a_1 = 0$), write $a_2 = 0$ as well. Avoid writing $\frac{0}{0}$. State:

  • If $\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = \vec{0} \implies \theta = 0^\circ$ (parallel).

💡 Example 1: Standard Skew Lines Distance [CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Find the shortest distance between the lines:

$$ \vec{r} = (\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}) + \lambda(2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 4\hat{k}) $$

$$ \vec{r} = (2\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 5\hat{k}) + \mu(3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 5\hat{k}) $$

Solution:

Step 1: Extract standard vector parameters

$$ \vec{a}_1 = \hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}, \quad \vec{b}_1 = 2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 4\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{a}_2 = 2\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 5\hat{k}, \quad \vec{b}_2 = 3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + 5\hat{k} $$

Step 2: Compute displacement vector $(\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)$

$$ \vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1 = (2 - 1)\hat{i} + (4 - 2)\hat{j} + (5 - 3)\hat{k} = \hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 2\hat{k} $$

Step 3: Compute cross product $(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2)$

$$ \vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 2 & 3 & 4 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{vmatrix} $$

$$ = \hat{i}(15 - 16) - \hat{j}(10 - 12) + \hat{k}(8 - 9) = -\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} - \hat{k} $$

Step 4: Compute magnitude $|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|$

$$ |\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2| = \sqrt{(-1)^2 + 2^2 + (-1)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt{6} $$

Step 5: Compute scalar dot product $(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)$

$$ (\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) = (-1)(1) + (2)(2) + (-1)(2) = -1 + 4 - 2 = 1 $$

Step 6: Calculate shortest distance $d$

$$ d = \left| \frac{(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|} \right| = \left| \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \text{ units} \quad \left(\text{or } \frac{\sqrt{6}}{6} \text{ units}\right) $$

💡 Example 2: Non-Standard Cartesian Conversion Trap [CBSE 2023, 5 Marks]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Find the shortest distance between the two lines:

$$ L_1: \frac{1 - x}{2} = \frac{y - 2}{3} = \frac{z + 1}{4} $$

$$ L_2: \frac{x + 1}{1} = \frac{2 - y}{-1} = \frac{z - 1}{2} $$

Solution:

Step 1: Reduce equations to standard symmetric form Ensure coefficients of $x, y, z$ in the numerators are $+1$.

For $L_1$:

$$ \frac{-(x - 1)}{2} = \frac{y - 2}{3} = \frac{z - (-1)}{4} \implies \frac{x - 1}{-2} = \frac{y - 2}{3} = \frac{z - (-1)}{4} $$

Points and direction ratios:

$$ (x_1, y_1, z_1) = (1, 2, -1), \quad \vec{b}_1 = -2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 4\hat{k} $$

For $L_2$:

$$ \frac{x - (-1)}{1} = \frac{-(y - 2)}{-1} = \frac{z - 1}{2} \implies \frac{x - (-1)}{1} = \frac{y - 2}{1} = \frac{z - 1}{2} $$

Points and direction ratios:

$$ (x_2, y_2, z_2) = (-1, 2, 1), \quad \vec{b}_2 = \hat{i} + \hat{j} + 2\hat{k} $$

Step 2: Compute $\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1$

$$ \vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1 = (-1 - 1)\hat{i} + (2 - 2)\hat{j} + (1 - (-1))\hat{k} = -2\hat{i} + 0\hat{j} + 2\hat{k} $$

Step 3: Evaluate numerator using the determinant

$$ \Delta = \begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} -2 & 0 & 2 \\ -2 & 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} $$

Expand along row 1:

$$ \Delta = -2(6 - 4) - 0 + 2(-2 - 3) = -2(2) + 2(-5) = -4 - 10 = -14 $$

Step 4: Compute vector cross product for the denominator

$$ \vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ -2 & 3 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = \hat{i}(6 - 4) - \hat{j}(-4 - 4) + \hat{k}(-2 - 3) = 2\hat{i} + 8\hat{j} - 5\hat{k} $$

$$ |\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2| = \sqrt{2^2 + 8^2 + (-5)^2} = \sqrt{4 + 64 + 25} = \sqrt{93} $$

Step 5: Compute distance

$$ d = \frac{|\Delta|}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|} = \frac{|-14|}{\sqrt{93}} = \frac{14}{\sqrt{93}} \text{ units} $$

💡 Example 3: Unspecified Line Type (Parallel Trap) [CBSE 2022, 3 Marks]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Find the shortest distance between the pair of lines:

$$ \vec{r} = (\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} - 4\hat{k}) + \lambda(2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 6\hat{k}) $$

$$ \vec{r} = (3\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 5\hat{k}) + \mu(4\hat{i} + 6\hat{j} + 12\hat{k}) $$

Solution:

Step 1: Check direction vectors for collinearity Direction vector of $L_1$: $\vec{b}_1 = 2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 6\hat{k}$
Direction vector of $L_2$: $\vec{b}_2 = 4\hat{i} + 6\hat{j} + 12\hat{k} = 2(2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 6\hat{k}) = 2\vec{b}_1$

Because $\vec{b}_2 \parallel \vec{b}_1$, these lines are parallel.
Applying the skew lines formula produces $\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = \vec{0}$ in the denominator, yielding an undefined result $\frac{0}{0}$.

Step 2: Apply the parallel lines formula

$$ d = \frac{|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)|}{|\vec{b}|} $$

Take $\vec{b} = 2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 6\hat{k}$.

Step 3: Calculate $\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1$

$$ \vec{a}_1 = \hat{i} + 2\hat{j} - 4\hat{k}, \quad \vec{a}_2 = 3\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 5\hat{k} $$

$$ \vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1 = (3 - 1)\hat{i} + (3 - 2)\hat{j} + (-5 - (-4))\hat{k} = 2\hat{i} + \hat{j} - \hat{k} $$

Step 4: Compute $\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)$

$$ \vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ 2 & 3 & 6 \\ 2 & 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} $$

$$ = \hat{i}(-3 - 6) - \hat{j}(-2 - 12) + \hat{k}(2 - 6) = -9\hat{i} + 14\hat{j} - 4\hat{k} $$

Step 5: Magnitudes and Final Answer

$$ |\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)| = \sqrt{(-9)^2 + 14^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{81 + 196 + 16} = \sqrt{293} $$

$$ |\vec{b}| = \sqrt{2^2 + 3^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{49} = 7 $$

$$ d = \frac{\sqrt{293}}{7} \text{ units} $$

💡 Example 4: Parameter Finding & Intersection [CBSE 2020, 2025 Sample Paper, 5 Marks]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Show that the following lines intersect, and find their point of intersection:

$$ \frac{x - 1}{2} = \frac{y + 1}{3} = \frac{z - 1}{4} \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{x - 3}{1} = \frac{y - k}{2} = \frac{z}{1} $$

Hence, determine the value of $k$.

Solution:

Step 1: Write parametric coordinates for arbitrary points Let an arbitrary point on line $L_1$ be $P(\lambda)$:

$$ \frac{x - 1}{2} = \frac{y + 1}{3} = \frac{z - 1}{4} = \lambda \implies P = (2\lambda + 1, \; 3\lambda - 1, \; 4\lambda + 1) $$

Let an arbitrary point on line $L_2$ be $Q(\mu)$:

$$ \frac{x - 3}{1} = \frac{y - k}{2} = \frac{z}{1} = \mu \implies Q = (\mu + 3, \; 2\mu + k, \; \mu) $$

Step 2: Equate coordinates at the point of intersection If lines intersect, there exist parameters $\lambda$ and $\mu$ such that $P \equiv Q$: 1. $2\lambda + 1 = \mu + 3 \implies 2\lambda - \mu = 2$ --- (Equation 1) 2. $3\lambda - 1 = 2\mu + k \implies 3\lambda - 2\mu = k + 1$ --- (Equation 2) 3. $4\lambda + 1 = \mu \implies 4\lambda - \mu = -1$ --- (Equation 3)

Step 3: Solve for $\lambda$ and $\mu$ using independent equations (1) and (3) Subtract Eq. (1) from Eq. (3):

$$ (4\lambda - \mu) - (2\lambda - \mu) = -1 - 2 $$

$$ 2\lambda = -3 \implies \lambda = -\frac{3}{2} $$

Substitute $\lambda = -\frac{3}{2}$ into Eq. (1):

$$ 2\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) - \mu = 2 \implies -3 - \mu = 2 \implies \mu = -5 $$

Step 4: Substitute parameters into Eq. (2) to find $k$

$$ 3\left(-\frac{3}{2}\right) - 2(-5) = k + 1 $$

$$ -\frac{9}{2} + 10 = k + 1 \implies \frac{11}{2} = k + 1 \implies k = \frac{11}{2} - 1 = \frac{9}{2} $$

Step 5: Determine coordinates of intersection point Using $\mu = -5$ in $Q$:

$$ x = -5 + 3 = -2 $$

$$ y = 2(-5) + \frac{9}{2} = -10 + 4.5 = -5.5 = -\frac{11}{2} $$

$$ z = -5 $$

Point of Intersection = $\left(-2, -\frac{11}{2}, -5\right)$.

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap Type Student Error Correct Concept & Fix
The Non-Standard Cartesian Trap Directly reading $a_1$ from $\frac{1-x}{2}$ as $2$ and point as $x_1 = 1$. Rewrite $\frac{1-x}{2} = \frac{-(x-1)}{2} = \frac{x-1}{-2}$. The point is $+1$, but the DR is $-2$.
The Blind Skew Formula Trap Using $\frac{\|(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2)\cdot(\vec{a}_2-\vec{a}_1)\|}{\|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2\|}$ when lines are parallel. If $\vec{b}_1 = k\vec{b}_2$, then $\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = \vec{0}$, giving $\frac{0}{0}$. Always check for proportional DRs first.
Sign Flip in $(x_2 - x_1)$ Writing $x_1 - x_2$ or making sign errors when points contain negative coordinates like $z - (-3)$. Clearly write out $\vec{a}_1$ and $\vec{a}_2$ as explicit vectors before computing the subtraction $(\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)$.
Omitting Absolute Value Leaving the final distance negative: $d = \frac{-14}{\sqrt{93}}$. Distance is a non-negative scalar. The numerator contains an outer absolute value: $|\Delta| = |-14| = 14$.
The Parallel Cross Product Order Computing $(\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) \times \vec{b}$ and dropping the modulus, or leaving out the magnitude divisor $|\vec{b}|$. The formula is $d = \frac{|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)|}{|\vec{b}|}$. The denominator is the scalar length of the direction vector.

5. Speed Hacks, Mnemonics & Golden Points

1. The Proportionality Pre-Check (5-Second Filter)

Before performing any cross products, inspect the denominators (DRs):

$$ \frac{a_1}{a_2} \stackrel{?}{=} \frac{b_1}{b_2} \stackrel{?}{=} \frac{c_1}{c_2} $$

  • Equal ratios $\implies$ Lines are Parallel. Use the single cross product formula.
  • Unequal ratios $\implies$ Lines are Skew / Intersecting. Use the triple product determinant.

2. Sarrus / Chio Shortcut for Determinants

When writing the numerator in Cartesian form, evaluate:

$$ \begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} $$

Notice that the cofactors of the first row $(x_2 - x_1, y_2 - y_1, z_2 - z_1)$ are the exact components of the cross product $\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2$:

$$ C_1 = (b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1), \quad C_2 = -(a_1 c_2 - a_2 c_1), \quad C_3 = (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1) $$

Compute these three bracketed values first: 1. Denominator $= \sqrt{C_1^2 + C_2^2 + C_3^2}$ 2. Numerator $= |(x_2 - x_1)C_1 + (y_2 - y_1)C_2 + (z_2 - z_1)C_3|$
This avoids computing cross products and determinants separately.

3. Memory Mnemonic: "Point Diff Dot Normal over Normal"

$$ \text{Distance} = \left| \frac{\Delta \vec{r} \cdot \vec{n}}{|\vec{n}|} \right| $$

  • $\Delta \vec{r} = (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)$ (Difference between starting points)
  • $\vec{n} = \vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2$ (Vector perpendicular to both line directions)
  • Shortest distance is simply the scalar projection of the displacement vector onto the common normal.

EXAMINER TIPS & COMMON ERROR PITFALLS

  1. Sign errors in symmetric form: When converting $1 - x$ to $x - 1$, remember to flip the sign of the corresponding direction ratio in the denominator (e.g., $\frac{1-x}{3} = \frac{x-1}{-3}$).
  2. Parallel lines vs Skew lines: Before calculating shortest distance, always check if the direction ratios are scalar multiples of each other ($\vec{b}_1 = k\vec{b}_2$). If they are parallel, use $d = \frac{|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)|}{|\vec{b}|}$. Using the skew-lines formula yields $0/0$ because $\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = \vec{0}$.
  3. Foot of Perpendicular coordinates: Always write out the general parametric coordinates clearly with $\lambda$, then formulate the scalar product of perpendicular vectors ($a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2 = 0$) to secure full method marks.

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1. Vector Form to Cartesian Form Conversion (and Vice Versa) in Seconds

  • Line Equation Conversion:

$$ \vec{r} = (x_1\hat{i} + y_1\hat{j} + z_1\hat{k}) + \lambda(a\hat{i} + b\hat{j} + c\hat{k}) \iff \frac{x - x_1}{a} = \frac{y - y_1}{b} = \frac{z - z_1}{c} $$

  • Mental Shortcut: Directly read the point from the free vector $\vec{a}$ and direction ratios (DRs) from the scalar multiple $\lambda \vec{b}$.
  • Trap to bypass: If a component has $0$ in the denominator, e.g., $\frac{x-1}{2} = \frac{y+3}{0} = \frac{z-4}{5}$, write it immediately as parametric form:

$$ x = 2\lambda + 1, \quad y = -3, \quad z = 5\lambda + 4 $$

2. Shortest Distance (SD) Determinant Method (Skew Lines)

Avoid calculating cross products and dot products in separate, messy scratchpad steps: For lines $\vec{r} = \vec{a}_1 + \lambda \vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{r} = \vec{a}_2 + \mu \vec{b}_2$:

$$ (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) \cdot (\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) = \begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} $$

  • Shortcut:
  • Evaluate this single $3 \times 3$ determinant $D$.
  • Compute $|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2| = \sqrt{(b_1c_2 - b_2c_1)^2 + (c_1a_2 - c_2a_1)^2 + (a_1b_2 - a_2b_1)^2}$.
  • Distance $d = \left|\frac{D}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|}\right|$.
  • If $D = 0$, lines are coplanar/intersecting; shortest distance is $0$.

$$ Q = (a\lambda + x_1, \; b\lambda + y_1, \; c\lambda + z_1) $$

  1. Vector $\vec{PQ} = (a\lambda + x_1 - x_0)\hat{i} + (b\lambda + y_1 - y_0)\hat{j} + (c\lambda + z_1 - z_0)\hat{k}$.
  2. Solve $\vec{PQ} \cdot \vec{b} = 0$ in a single combined line:

$$ \lambda = -\frac{a(x_1 - x_0) + b(y_1 - y_0) + c(z_1 - z_0)}{a^2 + b^2 + c^2} $$

  1. Image Shortcut: If $Q$ is the foot, the image point $P'$ is simply:

$$ P' = 2Q - P $$

(Do not set up extra variables $x', y', z'$—just use the midpoint inversion property directly).

4. Fast Check for Collinearity of Three Points

To check if $A, B, C$ are collinear: * Compute DRs of $\vec{AB} = (x_2 - x_1, y_2 - y_1, z_2 - z_1)$ and $\vec{BC} = (x_3 - x_2, y_3 - y_2, z_3 - z_2)$. * Check proportionality:

$$ \frac{x_3 - x_2}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{y_3 - y_2}{y_2 - y_1} = \frac{z_3 - z_2}{z_2 - z_1} $$

If ratios are equal, points are collinear (since point $B$ is common). Takes under 15 seconds.

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

1. Non-Standard Cartesian Form Trap (Most Frequent Deductions: $-\frac{1}{2}$ to $-1$ mark)

  • The Error: Given line $\frac{3 - x}{2} = \frac{2y + 1}{4} = \frac{1 - 3z}{6}$. Students directly copy $(2, 4, 6)$ as DRs.
  • The Correction: Standard form requires the coefficients of $x, y, z$ to be $+1$:

$$ \frac{-(x - 3)}{2} = \frac{2\left(y + \frac{1}{2}\right)}{4} = \frac{-3\left(z - \frac{1}{3}\right)}{6} \implies \frac{x - 3}{-2} = \frac{y + \frac{1}{2}}{2} = \frac{z - \frac{1}{3}}{-2} $$

Actual DRs: $(-2, 2, -2)$ or $(1, -1, 1)$, and base point is $\left(3, -\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{3}\right)$.

2. Parallel Lines Shortest Distance Trap

  • The Error: Applying the skew lines formula $d = \frac{|(\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) \cdot (\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2)|}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|}$ when $\vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{b}_2$ are proportional!
  • Since $\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = \vec{0}$, the formula yields $\frac{0}{0}$.
  • Evaluation Rule: Examiners deduct full formula and answer marks.
  • The Mandatory Check: Always check first if $\vec{b}_1 = k \vec{b}_2$. If yes, use the parallel lines distance formula:

$$ d = \frac{|(\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) \times \vec{b}|}{|\vec{b}|} $$

3. Missing Absolute Value Signs ($-\frac{1}{2}$ mark)

  • Distance is a scalar magnitude:

$$ d = \left|\frac{(\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) \cdot (\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2)}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|}\right| $$

  • Omitting the outer absolute value brackets and writing a negative distance or leaving answers unbracketed leads to an immediate step-mark deduction.

4. Conflating Direction Cosines (DCs) and Direction Ratios (DRs)

  • $l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$ applies only to Direction Cosines (DCs).
  • For Direction Ratios $a, b, c$, $a^2 + b^2 + c^2 \neq 1$ in general.
  • If a question asks for DCs, giving DRs $(a, b, c)$ without normalizing by $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}$ forfeits the final answer mark.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

  1. Direction Cosine Identities:

$$ l = \cos\alpha, \quad m = \cos\beta, \quad n = \cos\gamma $$

$$ \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 $$

$$ \sin^2\alpha + \sin^2\beta + \sin^2\gamma = 2 $$

Here, $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are angles made with positive coordinate axes (axes direction angles).

  1. Sign Ambiguity in Direction Cosines: A directed line has unique DCs $(l, m, n)$. An undirected line segment or line has two opposite sets of DCs:

$$ \pm \left(\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}, \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}, \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}\right) $$

  1. Perpendicularity and Parallelism Criterion:
  2. Perpendicular: $a_1a_2 + b_1b_2 + c_1c_2 = 0 \iff \vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2 = 0$

  3. Parallel: $\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2} \iff \vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = \vec{0}$

  4. Coplanarity Condition for Two Lines: Lines $\frac{x-x_1}{a_1} = \frac{y-y_1}{b_1} = \frac{z-z_1}{c_1}$ and $\frac{x-x_2}{a_2} = \frac{y-y_2}{b_2} = \frac{z-z_2}{c_2}$ are coplanar if:

$$ \begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} = 0 $$

  1. Skew Lines Definition: Lines that are neither intersecting nor parallel are called skew lines. They can exist only in three or higher dimensions. The shortest distance between two skew lines lies along the line perpendicular to both.

1. Pedagogical Foundation & Geometric Intuition

1.1 Definition of a Plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$

A surface is called a plane if for any two points $A$ and $B$ lying on it, the straight line joining $A$ and $B$ lies completely on the surface.

In 3D space, a line is uniquely fixed by 2 independent conditions (e.g., two points, or one point and a direction). A plane requires three independent conditions to be determined uniquely: 1. Normal vector to the plane and its perpendicular distance from the origin. 2. A point through which it passes and a direction perpendicular (normal) to it. 3. Three non-collinear points. 4. Two intersecting or parallel straight lines.

2. Exhaustive Derivations & Complete Mathematical Proofs

Theorem 1: Normal Form of the Equation of a Plane ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
The vector equation of a plane at a perpendicular distance $d$ from the origin, having a unit normal vector $\hat{n}$ directed from origin to plane, is:

$$ \vec{r} \cdot \hat{n} = d $$

Derivation (Vector Form):

  • Given:
    Let $ON$ be the normal from the origin $O$ to the plane such that $|\vec{ON}| = d$.
    Let $\hat{n}$ be the unit vector along $\vec{ON}$.
    Then, the position vector of the foot of perpendicular $N$ is:

$$ \vec{ON} = d\hat{n} $$

  • Construction:
    Let $P(\vec{r})$ be any arbitrary point on the plane. Join $NP$.
  • Proof:
    Since $ON$ is perpendicular to the plane, it is perpendicular to every line lying in the plane. In particular:

$$ \vec{NP} \perp \vec{ON} \implies \vec{NP} \cdot \vec{ON} = 0 $$

By triangle law in $\triangle ONP$:

$$ \vec{NP} = \vec{r} - \vec{ON} = \vec{r} - d\hat{n} $$

Substituting this into the orthogonality condition:

$$ (\vec{r} - d\hat{n}) \cdot (d\hat{n}) = 0 $$

Since $d \neq 0$:

$$ (\vec{r} - d\hat{n}) \cdot \hat{n} = 0 \implies \vec{r} \cdot \hat{n} - d(\hat{n} \cdot \hat{n}) = 0 $$

Because $\hat{n}$ is a unit vector, $\hat{n} \cdot \hat{n} = 1$:

$$ \vec{r} \cdot \hat{n} = d \quad \blacksquare $$

Conversion to Cartesian Form:

Let $\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$ and let the direction cosines (DC's) of the normal vector be $l, m, n$, so that:

$$ \hat{n} = l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k} \quad \text{where } l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1 $$

Substituting into $\vec{r} \cdot \hat{n} = d$:

$$ (x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}) \cdot (l\hat{i} + m\hat{j} + n\hat{k}) = d $$

$$ lx + my + nz = d \quad \blacksquare $$

[!CAUTION] In $Ax + By + Cz = D$, the constants $A, B, C$ are Direction Ratios (DR's) of the normal, NOT Direction Cosines.
The distance from origin is NOT $D$; the true perpendicular distance is:

$$ d = \frac{|D|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + C^2}} $$

Theorem 2: Plane Passing Through a Point and Perpendicular to a Given Vector ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
The vector equation of a plane passing through a point $A$ with position vector $\vec{a}$ and perpendicular to a vector $\vec{N}$ is:

$$ (\vec{r} - \vec{a}) \cdot \vec{N} = 0 \quad \iff \quad \vec{r} \cdot \vec{N} = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{N} $$

Derivation (Vector Form):

  • Given: Point $A(\vec{a})$ lies on the plane, and vector $\vec{N}$ is perpendicular to the plane.
  • Construction: Let $P(\vec{r})$ be any point on the plane. Join $AP$.
  • Proof:
    The vector $\vec{AP}$ lies completely in the plane for all positions of $P$:

$$ \vec{AP} = \vec{r} - \vec{a} $$

Since $\vec{N}$ is perpendicular to the plane, $\vec{AP} \perp \vec{N}$:

$$ \vec{AP} \cdot \vec{N} = 0 $$

$$ (\vec{r} - \vec{a}) \cdot \vec{N} = 0 \implies \vec{r} \cdot \vec{N} = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{N} \quad \blacksquare $$

Cartesian Form:

Let $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, $P(x, y, z)$, and $\vec{N} = A\hat{i} + B\hat{j} + C\hat{k}$.

$$ \vec{r} - \vec{a} = (x - x_1)\hat{i} + (y - y_1)\hat{j} + (z - z_1)\hat{k} $$

$$ (\vec{r} - \vec{a}) \cdot \vec{N} = 0 \implies A(x - x_1) + B(y - y_1) + C(z - z_1) = 0 \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 3: Plane Passing Through Three Non-Collinear Points ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
The equation of a plane passing through three non-collinear points $A(\vec{a})$, $B(\vec{b})$, and $C(\vec{c})$ is:

$$ (\vec{r} - \vec{a}) \cdot [(\vec{b} - \vec{a}) \times (\vec{c} - \vec{a})] = 0 $$

Derivation:

  • Given: Points $A(\vec{a})$, $B(\vec{b})$, $C(\vec{c})$ lie on the plane.
  • Proof:
    Vectors $\vec{AB} = \vec{b} - \vec{a}$ and $\vec{AC} = \vec{c} - \vec{a}$ both lie in the plane.
    Since $A, B, C$ are non-collinear, $(\vec{b} - \vec{a}) \times (\vec{c} - \vec{a})$ produces a non-zero vector perpendicular to both $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{AC}$, and hence normal to the plane:

$$ \vec{N} = (\vec{b} - \vec{a}) \times (\vec{c} - \vec{a}) $$

For any point $P(\vec{r})$ on the plane, $\vec{AP} = \vec{r} - \vec{a}$ is coplanar with $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{AC}$.
Therefore, the scalar triple product vanishes:

$$ (\vec{r} - \vec{a}) \cdot [(\vec{b} - \vec{a}) \times (\vec{c} - \vec{a})] = 0 \quad \blacksquare $$

Cartesian Form:

Let $A(x_1, y_1, z_1)$, $B(x_2, y_2, z_2)$, $C(x_3, y_3, z_3)$, and $P(x, y, z)$.

$$ \begin{vmatrix} x - x_1 & y - y_1 & z - z_1 \\ x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ x_3 - x_1 & y_3 - y_1 & z_3 - z_1 \end{vmatrix} = 0 \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 4: Intercept Form of the Equation of a Plane ⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
If a plane cuts non-zero intercepts $a, b, c$ on the $x$-, $y$-, and $z$-axes respectively, its equation is:

$$ \frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} + \frac{z}{c} = 1 $$

Proof:

  • The plane passes through $A(a, 0, 0)$, $B(0, b, 0)$, and $C(0, 0, c)$.
  • Using the general Cartesian equation $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$:
  • Point $A$ on plane: $A(a) + D = 0 \implies A = -D/a$
  • Point $B$ on plane: $B(b) + D = 0 \implies B = -D/b$
  • Point $C$ on plane: $C(c) + D = 0 \implies C = -D/c$
  • Substitute $A, B, C$ into $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$:

$$ -\frac{D}{a}x - \frac{D}{b}y - \frac{D}{c}z + D = 0 $$

Divide by $-D$ ($D \neq 0$ since intercepts are non-zero):

$$ \frac{x}{a} + \frac{y}{b} + \frac{z}{c} = 1 \quad \blacksquare $$

Theorem 5: Plane Passing Through the Line of Intersection of Two Planes ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement:
Let $\Pi_1: \vec{r} \cdot \vec{n}_1 = d_1$ and $\Pi_2: \vec{r} \cdot \vec{n}_2 = d_2$.
The equation of any plane $\Pi$ passing through the line of intersection of $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$ is:

$$ (\vec{r} \cdot \vec{n}_1 - d_1) + \lambda(\vec{r} \cdot \vec{n}_2 - d_2) = 0 \quad (\lambda \in \mathbb{R}) $$

Or in Cartesian form:

$$ (A_1x + B_1y + C_1z - D_1) + \lambda(A_2x + B_2y + C_2z - D_2) = 0 $$

Proof:

  1. Linearity: The equation is linear in $x, y, z$, so it represents a plane.
  2. Incidence: Let $P_0(\vec{r}_0)$ be any point on the intersection line of $\Pi_1$ and $\Pi_2$.
    Then $P_0$ satisfies both equations simultaneously:

$$ \vec{r}_0 \cdot \vec{n}_1 = d_1 \implies \vec{r}_0 \cdot \vec{n}_1 - d_1 = 0 $$

$$ \vec{r}_0 \cdot \vec{n}_2 = d_2 \implies \vec{r}_0 \cdot \vec{n}_2 - d_2 = 0 $$

Substituting $\vec{r}_0$ into the combined equation:

$$ 0 + \lambda(0) = 0 $$

This identity holds for all $\lambda \in \mathbb{R}$.
Hence, every point on the line of intersection lies on the plane $\Pi$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 6: Intersection of a Line and a Plane ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given a line $L: \frac{x - x_0}{a} = \frac{y - y_0}{b} = \frac{z - z_0}{c} = \mu$ and a plane $\Pi: Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$:

  1. Any general point on the line is:

$$ P(\mu) = (x_0 + a\mu, \; y_0 + b\mu, \; z_0 + c\mu) $$

  1. Substitute $P(\mu)$ into $\Pi$:

$$ A(x_0 + a\mu) + B(y_0 + b\mu) + C(z_0 + c\mu) + D = 0 $$

$$ \mu(Aa + Bb + Cc) + (Ax_0 + By_0 + Cz_0 + D) = 0 $$

$$ \mu = -\frac{Ax_0 + By_0 + Cz_0 + D}{Aa + Bb + Cc} $$

Three Classical Cases:

  • Case 1: Unique Point of Intersection: $Aa + Bb + Cc \neq 0$ (Line is not parallel to the plane).
  • Case 2: Line is Parallel to Plane (No Intersection): $Aa + Bb + Cc = 0$ and $Ax_0 + By_0 + Cz_0 + D \neq 0$.
  • Case 3: Line Lies Entirely in the Plane: $Aa + Bb + Cc = 0$ and $Ax_0 + By_0 + Cz_0 + D = 0$.

3. High-Yield Worked Examples & 5-Year PYQs (2020–2025)

Example 1: Line of Intersection of Two Planes [CBSE 2024, 4 Marks]

Problem:
Find the vector and Cartesian equations of the plane passing through the line of intersection of the planes:

$$ \vec{r} \cdot (\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}) - 4 = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \vec{r} \cdot (2\hat{i} + \hat{j} - \hat{k}) + 5 = 0 $$

and perpendicular to the plane $\vec{r} \cdot (5\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 6\hat{k}) + 8 = 0$.

Solution:
Step 1: Family of planes:

$$ [\vec{r} \cdot (\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 3\hat{k}) - 4] + \lambda [\vec{r} \cdot (2\hat{i} + \hat{j} - \hat{k}) + 5] = 0 $$

$$ \vec{r} \cdot [(1 + 2\lambda)\hat{i} + (2 + \lambda)\hat{j} + (3 - \lambda)\hat{k}] + (5\lambda - 4) = 0 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Normal vector $\vec{N}$ of this plane is:

$$ \vec{N} = (1 + 2\lambda)\hat{i} + (2 + \lambda)\hat{j} + (3 - \lambda)\hat{k} $$

Step 2: Apply orthogonality condition:
The plane is perpendicular to $\Pi_3: \vec{r} \cdot (5\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 6\hat{k}) + 8 = 0$, so $\vec{N} \cdot \vec{N}_3 = 0$:

$$ 5(1 + 2\lambda) + 3(2 + \lambda) - 6(3 - \lambda) = 0 $$

$$ 5 + 10\lambda + 6 + 3\lambda - 18 + 6\lambda = 0 $$

$$ 19\lambda - 7 = 0 \implies \lambda = \frac{7}{19} $$

Step 3: Substitute $\lambda$ back into (1):

$$ \vec{r} \cdot \left[\left(1 + \frac{14}{19}\right)\hat{i} + \left(2 + \frac{7}{19}\right)\hat{j} + \left(3 - \frac{7}{19}\right)\hat{k}\right] + \left(5\left(\frac{7}{19}\right) - 4\right) = 0 $$

$$ \vec{r} \cdot \left(\frac{33}{19}\hat{i} + \frac{45}{19}\hat{j} + \frac{50}{19}\hat{k}\right) + \frac{35 - 76}{19} = 0 $$

Multiply throughout by 19:

$$ \vec{r} \cdot (33\hat{i} + 45\hat{j} + 50\hat{k}) - 41 = 0 \quad \text{(Vector Form)} $$

Cartesian Form:

$$ 33x + 45y + 50z = 41 $$

Example 2: Intersection Point and Distance [CBSE 2023, 5 Marks]

Problem:
Find the coordinates of the point where the line:

$$ \frac{x + 1}{2} = \frac{y - 2}{3} = \frac{z + 3}{4} $$

meets the plane $2x + 3y - 4z = 26$. Hence, find the distance of this point from $A(3, -1, 2)$.

Solution:
Step 1: Parametric representation of general point on the line: Let:

$$ \frac{x + 1}{2} = \frac{y - 2}{3} = \frac{z + 3}{4} = k $$

$$ x = 2k - 1, \quad y = 3k + 2, \quad z = 4k - 3 $$

General point $P(2k - 1, 3k + 2, 4k - 3)$.

Step 2: Point lies on the plane: Substitute coordinates into $2x + 3y - 4z = 26$:

$$ 2(2k - 1) + 3(3k + 2) - 4(4k - 3) = 26 $$

$$ 4k - 2 + 9k + 6 - 16k + 12 = 26 $$

$$ -3k + 16 = 26 \implies -3k = 10 \implies k = -\frac{10}{3} \quad \text{--- Wait, let's re-verify:} $$

$$ 4 - 2 + 6 + 12 = 16. \quad 4k+9k-16k = -3k. \quad -3k + 16 = 26 \implies k = -\frac{10}{3} $$

Let coordinates of $P$ be:

$$ x = 2\left(-\frac{10}{3}\right) - 1 = -\frac{23}{3} $$

$$ y = 3\left(-\frac{10}{3}\right) + 2 = -8 = -\frac{24}{3} $$

$$ z = 4\left(-\frac{10}{3}\right) - 3 = -\frac{49}{3} $$

Intersection point: $P\left(-\frac{23}{3}, -8, -\frac{49}{3}\right)$.

Step 3: Distance from $A(3, -1, 2)$:

$$ AP = \sqrt{\left(-\frac{23}{3} - 3\right)^2 + (-8 - (-1))^2 + \left(-\frac{49}{3} - 2\right)^2} $$

$$ = \sqrt{\left(-\frac{32}{3}\right)^2 + (-7)^2 + \left(-\frac{55}{3}\right)^2} $$

$$ = \sqrt{\frac{1024}{9} + 49 + \frac{3025}{9}} = \sqrt{\frac{4049 + 441}{9}} = \sqrt{\frac{4490}{9}} = \frac{\sqrt{4490}}{3} \text{ units} $$

Example 3: Foot of Perpendicular and Image of a Point in a Plane [CBSE 2022 / 2020, 5 Marks]

Problem:
Find the position vector of the foot of the perpendicular and the image of the point $P(1, 2, 3)$ with respect to the plane:

$$ \vec{r} \cdot (2\hat{i} - \hat{j} + \hat{k}) + 9 = 0 $$

Solution:
Step 1: Equation of normal line through $P(1, 2, 3)$:
The direction ratios of the normal to the plane are $(2, -1, 1)$.
Equation of line $PN$:

$$ \frac{x - 1}{2} = \frac{y - 2}{-1} = \frac{z - 3}{1} = \lambda $$

Any general point on this line is:

$$ Q(2\lambda + 1, \; -\lambda + 2, \; \lambda + 3) $$

Step 2: Foot of perpendicular $N$ lies on the plane:
Cartesian equation of plane: $2x - y + z + 9 = 0$.
Substitute $Q$:

$$ 2(2\lambda + 1) - (-\lambda + 2) + (\lambda + 3) + 9 = 0 $$

$$ 4\lambda + 2 + \lambda - 2 + \lambda + 3 + 9 = 0 $$

$$ 6\lambda + 12 = 0 \implies \lambda = -2 $$

Substitute $\lambda = -2$:

$$ x_N = 2(-2) + 1 = -3 $$

$$ y_N = -(-2) + 2 = 4 $$

$$ z_N = -2 + 3 = 1 $$

Foot of perpendicular: $N(-3, 4, 1)$.
Position vector: $\vec{r}_N = -3\hat{i} + 4\hat{j} + \hat{k}$.

Step 3: Image Point $P'(x', y', z')$:
$N$ is the midpoint of $PP'$:

$$ \frac{x' + 1}{2} = -3 \implies x' = -7 $$

$$ \frac{y' + 2}{2} = 4 \implies y' = 6 $$

$$ \frac{z' + 3}{2} = 1 \implies z' = -1 $$

Image: $P'(-7, 6, -1)$, Position vector: $\vec{r}_{P'} = -7\hat{i} + 6\hat{j} - \hat{k}$.

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap / Pitfall Common Misconception Correct Method & Verification
Normal Form Distance $d$ Assuming in $\vec{r} \cdot (2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} - 6\hat{k}) = 14$ that $d = 14$. Normal vector must be converted to a unit normal $\hat{n}$. Divide by $|\vec{N}| = \sqrt{4+9+36} = 7 \implies d = \frac{14}{7} = 2$.
Negative Distance in Normal Form In $lx + my + nz = -5$, claiming $d = -5$. Distance must be non-negative ($d \ge 0$). Multiply entire equation by $-1$: $(-l)x + (-m)y + (-n)z = 5 \implies d = 5$.
Line Parallel to Plane Condition Using $a_1a_2 + b_1b_2 + c_1c_2 = 0$ for parallel lines to planes. For a line to be parallel to a plane, the line is perpendicular to the plane's normal: $\vec{b} \cdot \vec{n} = 0$.
Angle Between Line and Plane Using $\cos \theta = \frac{\vec{b} \cdot \vec{n}}{|\vec{b}||\vec{n}|}$. The angle $\theta$ between line and plane satisfies $\sin \theta = \frac{|\vec{b} \cdot \vec{n}|}{|\vec{b}||\vec{n}|}$ (since $\vec{n}$ is normal to plane, the complementary angle is $\pi/2 - \theta$).
Coplanarity of Two Lines Forgetting that two lines can be skew in 3D space. For lines to be coplanar: $(\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) \cdot (\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) = 0$.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Shortcuts for Competitive / Board Exams

Shortcut 1: Direct Formula for Foot of Perpendicular & Image

To find foot of perpendicular $(x, y, z)$ from point $(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ to plane $Ax + By + Cz + D = 0$:

$$ \frac{x - x_1}{A} = \frac{y - y_1}{B} = \frac{z - z_1}{C} = -\frac{Ax_1 + By_1 + Cz_1 + D}{A^2 + B^2 + C^2} $$

To find the Image Point $(x', y', z')$:

$$ \frac{x' - x_1}{A} = \frac{y' - y_1}{B} = \frac{z' - z_1}{C} = -2\left(\frac{Ax_1 + By_1 + Cz_1 + D}{A^2 + B^2 + C^2}\right) $$

Example Verification from Example 3:
$P(1, 2, 3)$, Plane: $2x - y + z + 9 = 0$.
Value: $Ax_1 + By_1 + Cz_1 + D = 2(1) - 2 + 3 + 9 = 12$.
$A^2 + B^2 + C^2 = 4 + 1 + 1 = 6$.
Foot ratio: $-\frac{12}{6} = -2 \implies x = 1 + 2(-2) = -3, \; y = 2 - 1(-2) = 4, \; z = 3 + 1(-2) = 1$. (Instantaneous 10-second check!)

Shortcut 2: Distance Between Parallel Planes

For planes $Ax + By + Cz + D_1 = 0$ and $Ax + By + Cz + D_2 = 0$:

$$ D_{\text{sep}} = \frac{|D_1 - D_2|}{\sqrt{A^2 + B^2 + C^2}} $$

(Always ensure the coefficients of $x, y, z$ are identical before taking $|D_1 - D_2|$).

Shortcut 3: Normal Vector via Cross Product

When finding a plane passing through two directions $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$, immediately take:

$$ \vec{N} = \vec{u} \times \vec{v} $$

And write the plane as $\vec{r} \cdot \vec{N} = \vec{a} \cdot \vec{N}$ without setting up large linear systems.
