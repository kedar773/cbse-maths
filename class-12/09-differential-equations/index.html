Basic Concepts: Order, Degree, and Verification of Solutions of Differential Equations

Chapter: Differential Equations (Class 12 CBSE / State Boards)

Subtopic 1: Basic Concepts — Order, Degree, and Verification of Solutions

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Definition of a Differential Equation

An equation involving an independent variable (usually $x$), a dependent variable (usually $y$), and one or more derivative(s) of the dependent variable with respect to the independent variable:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx}, \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}, \dots, \frac{d^ny}{dx^n} $$

is called a Differential Equation (D.E.).

  • Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE): Involves derivatives with respect to a single independent variable. (The Class 12 syllabus deals exclusively with ODEs).
  • General Functional Form of an ODE of order $n$:

$$ F\left(x, y, y', y'', \dots, y^{(n)}\right) = 0 $$

where $y^{(k)} = \frac{d^k y}{dx^k}$.

1.2 Order of a Differential Equation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definition (NCERT): The order of a differential equation is defined as the order of the highest-order derivative of the dependent variable with respect to the independent variable appearing in the given differential equation.

Key Principles:

  1. Always Defined: The order of any valid differential equation is always a positive integer ($n \in \mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$). It is never undefined.

  2. Identification Strategy: Scan the entire equation and locate all terms involving derivatives: $\frac{dy}{dx}$ (Order 1), $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ (Order 2), $\frac{d^3y}{dx^3}$ (Order 3), etc. The maximum index of differentiation is the order.

  3. Geometric Intuition: The order represents the intrinsic dimensionality or the number of independent physical/geometric degrees of freedom (e.g., in kinematics: position $\to$ order 0, velocity $\to$ order 1, acceleration $\to$ order 2).

1.3 Degree of a Differential Equation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definition (NCERT): To study the degree of a differential equation, the differential equation must be expressible as a polynomial equation in its derivatives (i.e., in $y', y'', y''', \dots$). When so expressible, the degree is the highest power (positive integral exponent) to which the highest-order derivative is raised, after the equation has been made completely free from radicals (fractional powers) and negative exponents involving the derivatives.

Critical Existence Condition:

The degree of a differential equation is defined IF AND ONLY IF the equation can be written in the form:

$$ A_0(x,y)\left(\frac{d^n y}{dx^n}\right)^k + A_1(x,y)\left(\frac{d^n y}{dx^n}\right)^{k-1} + \dots + A_k(x,y) = 0 $$

where all derivative terms appear with non-negative integer powers and are not trapped inside transcendental operations such as:

$$ \sin\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right), \quad \cos\left(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right), \quad e^{\frac{dy}{dx}}, \quad \ln\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right), \quad \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) $$

[!IMPORTANT] - If any derivative term is trapped inside a non-algebraic/transcendental function and cannot be eliminated by algebraic manipulation, the degree is NOT DEFINED. - Transcendental functions of independent/dependent variables (such as $\sin x$, $e^y$, $\ln x$) do NOT affect the polynomial nature of derivatives. Degree depends strictly on the derivatives.

1.4 Solution of a Differential Equation: General vs. Particular ⭐⭐⭐⭐

A function $\phi(x)$ is called a solution (or integral) of a differential equation on an open interval $I$ if, when substituted into the differential equation together with its derivatives, it reduces the equation to an identity over $I$.

1. General Solution (Primitive)

A solution of an $n^{\text{th}}$-order differential equation containing exactly $n$ independent arbitrary constants is called the General Solution (or Complete Integral).

$$ \text{Number of arbitrary constants in the General Solution} = \text{Order of the D.E.} = n $$

2. Particular Solution

A solution obtained from the general solution by assigning specific numerical values to the arbitrary constants (determined via Initial Value Problems or Boundary Conditions) is called a Particular Solution.

$$ \text{Number of arbitrary constants in any Particular Solution} = 0 $$

2. Rigorous Step-by-Step Verifications & Proofs

Theorem 1: Verification of Explicit Solutions

Statement: Prove that the function $y = A\cos(\omega x) + B\sin(\omega x)$ is a general solution of the second-order linear differential equation:

$$ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \omega^2 y = 0 $$

where $A, B, \omega$ are real constants and $\omega \neq 0$.

Proof:

Given:

$$ y(x) = A\cos(\omega x) + B\sin(\omega x) \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

To Prove:

$$ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \omega^2 y = 0 $$

Step 1: First-Order Differentiation Differentiating equation (1) with respect to $x$ using linearity and standard chain rule:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}\big[A\cos(\omega x) + B\sin(\omega x)\big] $$

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = -A\omega\sin(\omega x) + B\omega\cos(\omega x) \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Step 2: Second-Order Differentiation Differentiating equation (2) with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\big[-A\omega\sin(\omega x) + B\omega\cos(\omega x)\big] $$

$$ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega x) - B\omega^2\sin(\omega x) $$

Factoring out $-\omega^2$:

$$ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -\omega^2\big[A\cos(\omega x) + B\sin(\omega x)\big] \quad \text{--- (3)} $$

Step 3: Direct Substitution Substitute equation (1) into equation (3):

$$ \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = -\omega^2 y $$

$$ \implies \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \omega^2 y = 0 $$

Conclusion: 1. The function satisfies the differential equation identically for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$. 2. The differential equation is of order 2, and the solution contains exactly 2 arbitrary constants ($A$ and $B$). Hence, $y = A\cos(\omega x) + B\sin(\omega x)$ is the general solution. $\quad \blacksquare$

Theorem 2: Verification of Implicit Relations

Statement: Prove that the implicit relation $x^2 = 2y^2 \ln y$ is a solution of the differential equation:

$$ (x^2 + y^2)\frac{dy}{dx} - xy = 0 $$

for all $y > 0$ and $x > 0$.

Proof:

Given:

$$ x^2 = 2y^2 \ln y \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

To Prove:

$$ (x^2 + y^2)\frac{dy}{dx} - xy = 0 $$

Step 1: Implicit Differentiation Differentiating both sides of (1) with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(x^2) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(2y^2 \ln y\right) $$

$$ 2x = 2 \left[ \frac{d}{dx}(y^2) \cdot \ln y + y^2 \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(\ln y) \right] $$

$$ 2x = 2 \left[ 2y\frac{dy}{dx}\ln y + y^2 \cdot \frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx} \right] $$

$$ 2x = 2y \frac{dy}{dx} \left[ 2\ln y + 1 \right] $$

Divide both sides by 2:

$$ x = y(2\ln y + 1)\frac{dy}{dx} \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Step 2: Express $\frac{dy}{dx}$ in Terms of $x$ and $y$ From equation (2):

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x}{y(2\ln y + 1)} $$

From original equation (1), note that:

$$ 2\ln y = \frac{x^2}{y^2} $$

Substitute this relation into the denominator:

$$ 2\ln y + 1 = \frac{x^2}{y^2} + 1 = \frac{x^2 + y^2}{y^2} $$

Therefore:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x}{y \cdot \left(\frac{x^2 + y^2}{y^2}\right)} = \frac{x y^2}{y (x^2 + y^2)} = \frac{xy}{x^2 + y^2} $$

Step 3: Verification of the Differential Equation Cross-multiplying:

$$ (x^2 + y^2)\frac{dy}{dx} = xy $$

$$ (x^2 + y^2)\frac{dy}{dx} - xy = 0 $$

The given relation identically satisfies the differential equation. $\quad \blacksquare$

Differential Equations with Variables Separable and Equations Reducible to Separable Form

TEACHING LECTURE NOTE: DIFFERENTIAL EQUATIONS (CLASS 12 CBSE / ISC / JEE)

Topic: Differential Equations with Variables Separable & Reducible to Separable Form

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Fundamental Definitions & Core Terminology

A first-order, first-degree differential equation has the general form:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y) \quad \text{or} \quad M(x, y)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0 $$

  • General Solution: A relation between $x$ and $y$ satisfying the differential equation containing exactly one arbitrary constant (equal to the order of the differential equation, which is $1$).
  • Particular Solution: A solution obtained from the general solution by assigning a specific numerical value to the arbitrary constant using an initial condition / boundary value (e.g., $y(x_0) = y_0$).
  • Singular Solution: A solution that satisfies the differential equation but cannot be obtained from the general solution for any finite real value of the arbitrary constant. Often arises when dividing by factors that could be zero.

1.2 Variable Separable Form (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Priority)

Definition

A first-order, first-degree differential equation is said to be of the variable separable type if it can be expressed in the form:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x) \cdot g(y) $$

where $f(x)$ is purely a function of $x$ (or a constant) and $g(y)$ is purely a function of $y$ (or a constant).

Geometric Interpretation

Geometrically, the slope $m = \frac{dy}{dx}$ at any point $(x, y)$ in the Cartesian plane decomposes into a product of a horizontal component depending only on abscissa $x$ and a vertical component depending only on ordinate $y$. - Along any vertical line $x = c_1$, the ratio of slopes depends purely on $g(y)$. - Along any horizontal line $y = c_2$, the slope varies directly in proportion to $f(x)$.

The solution represents a 1-parameter family of integral curves whose tangent slopes at every point $(x, y)$ factor as $f(x)g(y)$.

1.3 Equations Reducible to Variable Separable Form (⭐⭐⭐⭐ Priority)

Definition

Equations that are not directly separable, but have the specific structure:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f(ax + by + c) $$

where $a, b, c \in \mathbb{R}$ and $b \neq 0$.

Intuition

The rate of change of $y$ is governed entirely by the linear combination $u = ax + by + c$. By moving into a rotated/scaled coordinate system along the parallel lines $ax + by + c = k$, the entire multivariable dependency collapses into a single independent variable $u$, rendering the differential equation separable with respect to $u$ and $x$.

2. Rigorous Derivations & Mathematical Proofs

Proof 2.1: General Solution of Variable Separable Differential Equations

$$ \begin{aligned} \textbf{Given:} &\quad \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x) \cdot g(y), \quad \text{where } g(y) \neq 0, \text{ and } f, g \text{ are continuous functions.} \\ \textbf{To Prove:} &\quad \int \frac{1}{g(y)}\,dy = \int f(x)\,dx + C, \quad \text{where } C \text{ is an arbitrary constant.} \end{aligned} $$

Step-by-Step Proof:

  1. Divide both sides of the given differential equation by $g(y)$ (since $g(y) \neq 0$):

$$ \frac{1}{g(y)} \frac{dy}{dx} = f(x) $$

  1. Integrate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$ \int \left( \frac{1}{g(y)} \frac{dy}{dx} \right) dx = \int f(x)\,dx $$

  1. Let $y = \phi(x)$. By the definition of differentials, the differential change in $y$ is:

$$ dy = \phi'(x)\,dx = \frac{dy}{dx}\,dx $$

  1. Substitute $dy = \frac{dy}{dx}\,dx$ into the left-hand integral:

$$ \int \frac{1}{g(y)}\,dy = \int f(x)\,dx + C $$

where $C$ is an arbitrary constant of integration.

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Reduction of $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(ax + by + c)$ to Variable Separable Form

$$ \begin{aligned} \textbf{Given:} &\quad \frac{dy}{dx} = f(ax + by + c), \quad b \neq 0 \\ \textbf{To Prove:} &\quad \int \frac{du}{a + b f(u)} = x + C, \quad \text{where } u = ax + by + c. \end{aligned} $$

Step-by-Step Proof:

  1. Define a substitution variable:

$$ u = ax + by + c $$

  1. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{du}{dx} = a + b \frac{dy}{dx} $$

  1. Isolate the derivative $\frac{dy}{dx}$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{b} \left( \frac{du}{dx} - a \right) $$

  1. Equate this expression to $f(u)$ from the original differential equation:

$$ \frac{1}{b} \left( \frac{du}{dx} - a \right) = f(u) $$

  1. Clear the factor of $b$ and isolate $\frac{du}{dx}$:

$$ \frac{du}{dx} - a = b f(u) \implies \frac{du}{dx} = a + b f(u) $$

  1. Separate the variables $u$ and $x$ (assuming $a + b f(u) \neq 0$):

$$ \frac{1}{a + b f(u)}\,du = dx $$

  1. Integrate both sides:

$$ \int \frac{1}{a + b f(u)}\,du = \int 1\,dx + C \implies \int \frac{du}{a + b f(u)} = x + C $$

$$ \tag*{Q.E.D.} $$

Homogeneous Differential Equations: Identification and Solution by Substitution

Class 12 Mathematics: Homogeneous Differential Equations

Chapter: Differential Equations
Subtopic: Identification, Theoretical Foundations, and Solution by Substitution
Target Level: CBSE Board Examinations & Engineering Entrances (JEE Main)

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Homogeneous Functions of Degree $n$

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (High Board Frequency — Mandatory 1-Mark / 2-Mark Justification Question)

NCERT Definition

A real-valued function $F(x, y)$ is said to be a homogeneous function of degree $n$ if for any non-zero real scalar $\lambda$:

$$ F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^n F(x, y) $$

Alternatively, any function $F(x, y)$ that can be expressed strictly in the form:

$$ F(x, y) = x^n \cdot g\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) \quad \text{or} \quad F(x, y) = y^n \cdot h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) $$

is a homogeneous function of degree $n$.

Geometric Intuition

Consider the level sets or ray paths emanating from the origin $O(0,0)$ along a straight line $y = mx$. Along this ray, the ratio $\frac{y}{x} = m$ remains constant.
If $F(x, y)$ is homogeneous of degree $0$, the value of $F(x, y)$ along any ray passing through the origin depends only on the slope $m$, not on the distance $r$ from the origin:

$$ F(x, mx) = x^0 g(m) = g(m) = \text{constant along the ray} $$

Geometrically, a first-order homogeneous differential equation defines a direction field where the integral curves share identical tangential slopes along any radial ray projecting from the origin.

1.2 Homogeneous Differential Equations (HDE)

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Crucial CBSE 3-Mark / 5-Mark Core Topic)

A first-order, first-degree differential equation:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{f(x, y)}{g(x, y)} \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{dy}{dx} = F(x, y) $$

is defined as a Homogeneous Differential Equation if and only if $F(x, y)$ is a homogeneous function of degree zero. That is:

$$ F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^0 F(x, y) = F(x, y) $$

Equivalently, if written in Pfaffian form:

$$ M(x, y)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0 $$

the equation is homogeneous if both $M(x, y)$ and $N(x, y)$ are homogeneous functions of the same degree $n$. Then:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{M(x, y)}{N(x, y)} = -\frac{\lambda^n M(x, y)}{\lambda^n N(x, y)} = F(x, y) $$

2. Complete Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

2.1 Derivation of Reducibility to Separable Form for Type I: $\frac{dy}{dx} = F(x, y)$

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Theorem Statement

Every first-order homogeneous differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = g\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)$ can be reduced to a separable differential equation under the transformation:

$$ y = v \cdot x \quad \text{where } v = v(x) $$

Proof:

Given:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = g\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) $$

Step 1: Product rule differentiation
Let:

$$ y = vx $$

Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ using the product rule:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = v \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(x) + x \cdot \frac{dv}{dx} $$

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = v + x\frac{dv}{dx} $$

Step 2: Substitution into the governing equation
Substitute $\frac{dy}{dx}$ and $\frac{y}{x} = v$ into the given differential equation:

$$ v + x\frac{dv}{dx} = g(v) $$

Step 3: Algebraic isolation of differentials
Isolating the term with derivative:

$$ x\frac{dv}{dx} = g(v) - v $$

Assuming $g(v) - v \neq 0$, divide both sides by $g(v) - v$ and multiply by $\frac{dx}{x}$:

$$ \frac{dv}{g(v) - v} = \frac{dx}{x} $$

Step 4: Integration
Both variables are completely separated. Integrating both sides yields the general solution:

$$ \int \frac{dv}{g(v) - v} = \int \frac{dx}{x} + C = \ln|x| + C $$

After evaluating the integral on the left-hand side, replace $v$ with $\frac{y}{x}$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

2.2 Derivation of Type II: $\frac{dx}{dy} = h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)$

⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Mandatory when terms contain exponential/trigonometric arguments of $\frac{x}{y}$)

Theorem Statement

Differential equations of the form $\frac{dx}{dy} = h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)$ reduce to variable-separable form under the substitution:

$$ x = v \cdot y \quad \text{where } v = v(y) $$

Proof:

Given:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right) $$

Step 1: Product rule differentiation
Differentiating $x = vy$ with respect to $y$:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = v + y\frac{dv}{dy} $$

Step 2: Substitution and separation

$$ v + y\frac{dv}{dy} = h(v) $$

$$ y\frac{dv}{dy} = h(v) - v $$

$$ \frac{dv}{h(v) - v} = \frac{dy}{y} $$

Integrating both sides:

$$ \int \frac{dv}{h(v) - v} = \ln|y| + C $$

Finally, substitute back $v = \frac{x}{y}$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

First-Order Linear Differential Equations and the Integrating Factor Method

Class 12 Mathematics: Differential Equations

Topic: First-Order Linear Differential Equations & The Integrating Factor Method

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Core Definitions & Canonical Forms

A differential equation is said to be linear if the dependent variable and its derivative(s) occur only in the first degree and are not multiplied together.

According to NCERT terminology, a first-order linear differential equation (LDE) appears in one of two canonical forms:

Type I (Dependent Variable: $y$, Independent Variable: $x$) $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x) $$

  • Where $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are continuous functions of $x$ alone, or constants.
  • Degree $= 1$, Order $= 1$.

Type II (Dependent Variable: $x$, Independent Variable: $y$) $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐]

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} + P_1(y)x = Q_1(y) $$

  • Where $P_1(y)$ and $Q_1(y)$ are continuous functions of $y$ alone, or constants.
  • This form is typically invoked when an equation cannot be separated as $\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)$ or when $\frac{dy}{dx}$ has a non-linear expression in the denominator.

1.2 Mathematical Intuition: The Product Rule in Reverse

Consider differentiating the product of an unknown function $y(x)$ and an auxiliary function $I(x)$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\big[y \cdot I(x)\big] = I(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + I'(x)y $$

Now examine the LHS of the canonical Type I equation multiplied by an arbitrary non-zero modifying factor $I(x)$:

$$ I(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)I(x)y = Q(x)I(x) $$

Observe the structural similarity: * Term 1: $I(x)\frac{dy}{dx}$ matches in both expressions. * Term 2: To make the LHS an exact derivative of $[y \cdot I(x)]$, we require:

$$ I'(x)y = P(x)I(x)y \implies \frac{dI}{dx} = P(x)I(x) $$

This transforms an otherwise non-integrable linear combination into a total derivative that can be integrated directly. The function $I(x)$ is termed the Integrating Factor (I.F.).

1.3 Geometric Interpretation

A general first-order differential equation defines a slope field (directional field) in the Cartesian plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. * For an LDE $\frac{dy}{dx} = Q(x) - P(x)y$, the isoclines (curves where the slope $\frac{dy}{dx} = c$) are given by $y = \frac{Q(x) - c}{P(x)}$. * When $P(x)$ is constant, say $k$, the rate of change of slope with respect to $y$ is constant: $\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = -k$. * Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^{\int P\,dx}$ acts as a local stretching/scaling factor along the flow lines of the vector field, converting non-conservative vector fields into gradient (exact) systems. The solution curves form an infinite family of non-intersecting integral curves parameterized by an arbitrary integration constant $C$.

2. Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem 1: Derivation of the Integrating Factor and General Solution (Type I) $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Given: A first-order linear differential equation in standard form:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x) \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

where $P(x)$ and $Q(x)$ are continuous real-valued functions on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

To Prove: 1. The integrating factor is:

$$ \text{I.F.} = e^{\int P(x)\,dx} $$

  1. The general solution is given by:

$$ y \cdot e^{\int P(x)\,dx} = \int \left(Q(x) \cdot e^{\int P(x)\,dx}\right)dx + C $$

Proof:

Step 1: Introduction of the Integrating Factor
Let $g(x)$ be a non-zero differentiable function of $x$. Multiply equation (1) throughout by $g(x)$:

$$ g(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)g(x)y = Q(x)g(x) \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Step 2: Condition for Exactness
We require the LHS of equation (2) to be equal to the derivative of the product $[y \cdot g(x)]$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\big[y \cdot g(x)\big] = g(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + g'(x)y \quad \text{--- (3)} $$

Equating the LHS of (2) and the RHS of (3):

$$ g(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)g(x)y = g(x)\frac{dy}{dx} + g'(x)y $$

Subtracting $g(x)\frac{dy}{dx}$ from both sides:

$$ P(x)g(x)y = g'(x)y $$

Since this must hold for any arbitrary function $y(x)$, we have:

$$ g'(x) = P(x)g(x) \implies \frac{dg}{dx} = P(x)g $$

Step 3: Solving for $g(x)$ (Separation of Variables)
Separating variables for $g$:

$$ \frac{1}{g}\,dg = P(x)\,dx $$

Integrating both sides with respect to their respective variables:

$$ \int \frac{1}{g}\,dg = \int P(x)\,dx $$

$$ \ln |g| = \int P(x)\,dx $$

Exponentiating both sides:

$$ |g(x)| = e^{\int P(x)\,dx} \implies g(x) = \pm e^{\int P(x)\,dx} $$

Since we only require one non-trivial integrating factor, we choose the positive branch and omit the constant of integration at this intermediate stage:

$$ \text{I.F.} = g(x) = e^{\int P(x)\,dx} $$

Step 4: Integration of the Modified Equation
Substitute $g(x)$ back into equation (2):

$$ \frac{d}{dx}\left[y \cdot e^{\int P(x)\,dx}\right] = Q(x)e^{\int P(x)\,dx} $$

Integrating both sides with respect to $x$:

$$ \int \frac{d}{dx}\left[y \cdot e^{\int P(x)\,dx}\right]dx = \int \left(Q(x) \cdot e^{\int P(x)\,dx}\right)dx + C $$

By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:

$$ y \cdot e^{\int P(x)\,dx} = \int \left(Q(x) \cdot e^{\int P(x)\,dx}\right)dx + C $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: General Solution of Type II Linear Differential Equations $\quad$ [Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐]

Given:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} + P_1(y)x = Q_1(y) $$

Proof Summary:
Interchanging the roles of dependent variable $y$ and independent variable $x$:

$$ \text{I.F.} = e^{\int P_1(y)\,dy} $$

Multiplying throughout by $\text{I.F.}$:

$$ \frac{d}{dy}\left[x \cdot e^{\int P_1(y)\,dy}\right] = Q_1(y) \cdot e^{\int P_1(y)\,dy} $$

Integrating both sides with respect to $y$:

$$ x \cdot e^{\int P_1(y)\,dy} = \int \left(Q_1(y) \cdot e^{\int P_1(y)\,dy}\right)dy + C $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Differentiating again with respect to $x$:

Solution:

Step 1: Separate variables

$$ (1 + e^{2x})\,dy = -(1 + y^2)e^x\,dx $$

Divide both sides by $(1 + y^2)(1 + e^{2x})$:

$$ \frac{1}{1 + y^2}\,dy = -\frac{e^x}{1 + e^{2x}}\,dx $$

Step 2: Integrate both sides

$$ \int \frac{1}{1 + y^2}\,dy = -\int \frac{e^x}{1 + (e^x)^2}\,dx $$

For the RHS, let $t = e^x \implies dt = e^x\,dx$:

$$ \tan^{-1}(y) = -\int \frac{dt}{1 + t^2} = -\tan^{-1}(t) + C $$

$$ \tan^{-1}(y) = -\tan^{-1}(e^x) + C $$

$$ \tan^{-1}(y) + \tan^{-1}(e^x) = C \quad \text{--- (General Solution)} $$

Step 3: Apply the initial condition $x = 0, y = 1$

$$ \tan^{-1}(1) + \tan^{-1}(e^0) = C $$

$$ \frac{\pi}{4} + \tan^{-1}(1) = C \implies \frac{\pi}{4} + \frac{\pi}{4} = C \implies C = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Step 4: Final Particular Solution

$$ \tan^{-1}(y) + \tan^{-1}(e^x) = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Using the identity $\tan^{-1}(A) + \cot^{-1}(A) = \frac{\pi}{2} \implies \tan^{-1}(y) = \cot^{-1}(e^x) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{e^x}\right)$:

$$ y = e^{-x} \quad \text{or} \quad y e^x = 1 $$

Solution:

Step 1: Substitution Let $u = x + y$. Differentiating with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{du}{dx} = 1 + \frac{dy}{dx} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{du}{dx} - 1 $$

Step 2: Substitute into the differential equation

$$ \frac{du}{dx} - 1 = \cos(u) + \sin(u) \implies \frac{du}{dx} = 1 + \cos(u) + \sin(u) $$

Step 3: Separate variables

$$ \frac{du}{1 + \cos(u) + \sin(u)} = dx $$

Step 4: Integrate using half-angle substitutions Recall standard half-angle identities:

$$ \cos(u) = \frac{1 - \tan^2\frac{u}{2}}{1 + \tan^2\frac{u}{2}}, \quad \sin(u) = \frac{2\tan\frac{u}{2}}{1 + \tan^2\frac{u}{2}}, \quad 1 = \frac{1 + \tan^2\frac{u}{2}}{1 + \tan^2\frac{u}{2}} $$

$$ 1 + \cos(u) + \sin(u) = \frac{(1 + \tan^2\frac{u}{2}) + (1 - \tan^2\frac{u}{2}) + 2\tan\frac{u}{2}}{1 + \tan^2\frac{u}{2}} = \frac{2 + 2\tan\frac{u}{2}}{\sec^2\frac{u}{2}} $$

Therefore:

$$ \int \frac{\sec^2\frac{u}{2}}{2\left(1 + \tan\frac{u}{2}\right)}\,du = \int dx $$

Let $z = 1 + \tan\frac{u}{2} \implies dz = \frac{1}{2}\sec^2\frac{u}{2}\,du$:

$$ \int \frac{dz}{z} = x + C $$

$$ \ln|z| = x + C \implies \ln\left|1 + \tan\left(\frac{u}{2}\right)\right| = x + C $$

$$ \ln\left|1 + \tan\left(\frac{x + y}{2}\right)\right| = x + C $$

Whenever all integrals yield logarithms:

3. Separation Spotting via Partial Derivatives

To quickly verify if $M(x, y)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0$ is separable:

$$ M(x, y) = f_1(x)g_1(y), \quad N(x, y) = f_2(x)g_2(y) $$

The cross ratio $\frac{M(x, y)}{N(x, y)}$ must depend solely on single-variable products:

$$ \frac{M(x,y)}{N(x,y)} = \frac{f_1(x)}{f_2(x)} \cdot \frac{g_1(y)}{g_2(y)} $$

If any mixed term like $(x^2 + y^2)$ or $(xy + 1)$ cannot be factorized into $(a(x))(b(y))$, it is not separable.

$$ t = \tan\left(\frac{u}{2}\right) \implies du = \frac{2\,dt}{1 + t^2}, \quad \sin u = \frac{2t}{1 + t^2}, \quad \cos u = \frac{1 - t^2}{1 + t^2} $$

Denominator $(1 + t^2)$ always cancels out, yielding a simple rational algebraic integral.

💡 Example 1 [CBSE 2024, 5 Marks]
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Solve the differential equation:

$$ (x^2 - y^2)\,dx + 2xy\,dy = 0, \quad \text{given that } y = 1 \text{ when } x = 1 $$

Solution:

Step 1: Express in standard derivative form and test homogeneity

$$ 2xy\,dy = -(x^2 - y^2)\,dx = (y^2 - x^2)\,dx $$

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{2xy} $$

Let $F(x, y) = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{2xy}$. Testing with $(\lambda x, \lambda y)$:

$$ F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \frac{\lambda^2 y^2 - \lambda^2 x^2}{2(\lambda x)(\lambda y)} = \frac{\lambda^2 (y^2 - x^2)}{\lambda^2 (2xy)} = \lambda^0 F(x, y) $$

Hence, the given differential equation is homogeneous of degree $0$.

Step 2: Apply Type I substitution
Substitute:

$$ y = vx \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = v + x\frac{dv}{dx} $$

Substituting into the differential equation:

$$ v + x\frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{(vx)^2 - x^2}{2x(vx)} = \frac{x^2(v^2 - 1)}{2x^2 v} = \frac{v^2 - 1}{2v} $$

Step 3: Separate variables

$$ x\frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{v^2 - 1}{2v} - v = \frac{v^2 - 1 - 2v^2}{2v} = \frac{-(v^2 + 1)}{2v} $$

$$ \frac{2v}{v^2 + 1}\,dv = -\frac{dx}{x} $$

Step 4: Integrate both sides

$$ \int \frac{2v}{v^2 + 1}\,dv = -\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx $$

Since $\frac{d}{dv}(v^2 + 1) = 2v$:

$$ \ln|v^2 + 1| = -\ln|x| + \ln|C| \quad (C > 0) $$

$$ \ln|v^2 + 1| + \ln|x| = \ln C $$

$$ \ln\left|x(v^2 + 1)\right| = \ln C $$

$$ x(v^2 + 1) = C $$

Step 5: Resubstitute $v = \frac{y}{x}$

$$ x\left(\frac{y^2}{x^2} + 1\right) = C \implies x\left(\frac{y^2 + x^2}{x^2}\right) = C \implies \frac{x^2 + y^2}{x} = C $$

$$ x^2 + y^2 = Cx $$

Step 6: Determine particular solution
Given $y = 1$ when $x = 1$:

$$ 1^2 + 1^2 = C(1) \implies C = 2 $$

Thus, the required particular solution is:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = 2x \quad \text{or} \quad (x - 1)^2 + y^2 = 1 $$

Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Solve the differential equation:

$$ 2y\,e^{x/y}\,dx + (y - 2x\,e^{x/y})\,dy = 0 $$

Solution:

Step 1: Recognize Type II symmetry ($\frac{x}{y}$ argument)
Because the exponential contains $\frac{x}{y}$, we must rearrange the equation for $\frac{dx}{dy}$:

$$ 2y\,e^{x/y}\,dx = (2x\,e^{x/y} - y)\,dy $$

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{2x\,e^{x/y} - y}{2y\,e^{x/y}} $$

Step 2: Verification of homogeneity
Let $F(x, y) = \frac{2x\,e^{x/y} - y}{2y\,e^{x/y}}$.

$$ F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \frac{2(\lambda x)e^{\lambda x / \lambda y} - \lambda y}{2(\lambda y)e^{\lambda x / \lambda y}} = \frac{\lambda (2x\,e^{x/y} - y)}{\lambda (2y\,e^{x/y})} = \lambda^0 F(x, y) $$

Step 3: Substitution $x = vy$

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = v + y\frac{dv}{dy} $$

$$ v + y\frac{dv}{dy} = \frac{2(vy)e^v - y}{2y\,e^v} = \frac{2v\,e^v - 1}{2e^v} = v - \frac{1}{2e^v} $$

$$ y\frac{dv}{dy} = -\frac{1}{2e^v} $$

Step 4: Variable separation & Integration

$$ 2e^v\,dv = -\frac{dy}{y} $$

$$ \int 2e^v\,dv = -\int \frac{dy}{y} $$

$$ 2e^v = -\ln|y| + C $$

$$ 2e^v + \ln|y| = C $$

Step 5: Resubstitute $v = \frac{x}{y}$

$$ 2e^{x/y} + \ln|y| = C $$

Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

Find the particular solution of:

$$ \left[x\sin^2\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) - y\right]dx + x\,dy = 0, \quad \text{given } y = \frac{\pi}{4} \text{ when } x = 1 $$

Solution:

Step 1: Rearrange to compute $\frac{dy}{dx}$

$$ x\,dy = \left[y - x\sin^2\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\right]dx $$

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{x} - \sin^2\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) $$

Step 2: Substitute $y = vx \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = v + x\frac{dv}{dx}$

$$ v + x\frac{dv}{dx} = v - \sin^2(v) $$

$$ x\frac{dv}{dx} = -\sin^2(v) $$

Step 3: Separate variables

$$ -\csc^2(v)\,dv = \frac{dx}{x} $$

Step 4: Integrate

$$ \int -\csc^2(v)\,dv = \int \frac{dx}{x} $$

$$ \cot(v) = \ln|x| + C $$

Step 5: Resubstitute $v = \frac{y}{x}$

$$ \cot\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) = \ln|x| + C $$

Step 6: Compute arbitrary constant $C$
Given $y = \frac{\pi}{4}$ when $x = 1$:

$$ \cot\left(\frac{\pi/4}{1}\right) = \ln(1) + C \implies \cot\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = 0 + C \implies C = 1 $$

Particular solution:

$$ \cot\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) = \ln|x| + 1 $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Mistakes

Trap / Pitfall Area Frequent Student Error Correct Methodical Approach
1. Omitting the Homogeneity Test Jumping straight to $y=vx$ without stating the $\lambda$-test. Boards penalize $1$ full mark for skipping this step. Always write: "Let $F(x,y)=\dots$, evaluate $F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^0 F(x, y)$", and conclude it is homogeneous of degree 0 before substituting.
2. Wrong Choice of Independent Variable Attempting $y = vx$ when the equation features expressions like $e^{x/y}$, $\sin(x/y)$, or $\cos(x/y)$. Leads to intractable non-elementary integration. If arguments are functions of $\frac{x}{y}$, immediately recast as $\frac{dx}{dy} = h(x/y)$ and use $x = vy \implies \frac{dx}{dy} = v + y\frac{dv}{dy}$.
3. Sign Error in Variable Separation When subtracting $v$ from the right-hand side, students write: $v + x\frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{f(v)}{g(v)} \implies x\frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{f(v) - v}{g(v)}$ incorrectly forgetting common denominators. Carefully apply brackets: $x\frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{f(v) - v\cdot g(v)}{g(v)}$.
4. Arbitrary Constant Placement with Logarithms Writing $\ln|A| = \ln|B| + C \implies A = B + C$. Apply index laws: $\ln|A| - \ln|B| = \ln C_1 \implies \left|\frac{A}{B}\right| = C_1 \implies A = C B$.
5. Neglecting Boundary Conditions Leaving the answer in terms of $v$ instead of $x$ and $y$, or calculating $C$ using $v$ instead of original coordinates. Always back-substitute $v = \frac{y}{x}$ (or $\frac{x}{y}$) first, then substitute initial values $(x_0, y_0)$ to find $C$.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

  1. Sum of Degrees Rule (Instant Visual Check):
    In polynomial forms $M(x, y)\,dx + N(x, y)\,dy = 0$, every single term must have the exact same combined sum of exponents of $x$ and $y$.
  2. Example: $x^3 + 3xy^2$ has degree $3$ in both terms ($3$ and $1+2=3$).

  3. If any constant (e.g., $+ 5$) or mismatched power (e.g., $+ x^2$) appears, the equation is not homogeneous.

  4. The "Cancel $v$ Directly" Shortcut:
    If the differential equation has the form:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{x} + \phi\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) $$

Upon substitution $y = vx$, the $v$ on both sides cancels instantly:

$$ v + x\frac{dv}{dx} = v + \phi(v) \implies \frac{dv}{\phi(v)} = \frac{dx}{x} $$

This avoids 2–3 lines of tedious algebraic simplification.

  1. Logarithmic Constant Grouping:
    When all integration terms yield natural logs:

$$ \ln|f(v)| = \ln|x| + C $$

Always write the constant as $\ln|C|$ (where $C > 0$). This allows direct exponentiation:

$$ f(v) = C x $$

bypassing bulky $e^{\ln x + C}$ terms.

  1. Euler's Homogeneous Function Theorem (Cross-Verification for Entrances):
    If $u = F(x, y)$ is a homogeneous function of degree $n$, then:

$$ x\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = n \cdot u $$

For degree $n = 0$:

$$ x\frac{\partial F}{\partial x} + y\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} = 0 $$

💡 Example 1: Type I Standard Trigonometric Form
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
Find the general solution of the differential equation:

$$ \cos^2 x \frac{dy}{dx} + y = \tan x \quad \left(0 \le x < \frac{\pi}{2}\right) $$

Solution:

Step 1: Reduce to Canonical Form
Dividing the entire equation by $\cos^2 x \neq 0$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + \frac{1}{\cos^2 x}y = \frac{\tan x}{\cos^2 x} $$

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + (\sec^2 x)y = \tan x \sec^2 x $$

Comparing with $\frac{dy}{dx} + Py = Q$:

$$ P(x) = \sec^2 x, \quad Q(x) = \tan x \sec^2 x $$

Step 2: Determine the Integrating Factor

$$ \text{I.F.} = e^{\int P(x)\,dx} = e^{\int \sec^2 x\,dx} = e^{\tan x} $$

Step 3: Write the General Solution Equation

$$ y \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q(x) \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dx + C $$

$$ y e^{\tan x} = \int (\tan x \sec^2 x) e^{\tan x}\,dx + C \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Step 4: Evaluate the Integral
Let $I_1 = \int \tan x \sec^2 x e^{\tan x}\,dx$.
Substitute $t = \tan x \implies dt = \sec^2 x\,dx$:

$$ I_1 = \int t e^t\,dt $$

Using Integration by Parts ($\int u v\,dt = u \int v\,dt - \int (u' \int v\,dt)dt$):

$$ I_1 = t e^t - \int 1 \cdot e^t\,dt = t e^t - e^t = e^t(t - 1) $$

Substitute back $t = \tan x$:

$$ I_1 = e^{\tan x}(\tan x - 1) $$

Step 5: Final Solution
Substitute $I_1$ into (1):

$$ y e^{\tan x} = e^{\tan x}(\tan x - 1) + C $$

Dividing by $e^{\tan x}$:

$$ y = (\tan x - 1) + C e^{-\tan x} $$

💡 Example 2: Type II Standard Algebraic Form
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2023, 5 Marks]
Find the general solution of the differential equation:

$$ (1 + y^2)dx = (\tan^{-1} y - x)dy $$

Solution:

Step 1: Identify the Dependent vs Independent Variable
Expressing $\frac{dy}{dx}$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1 + y^2}{\tan^{-1} y - x} $$

The denominator contains both variables non-linearly, which prevents separating or expressing it as $\frac{dy}{dx} + Py = Q$.
Taking the reciprocal:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{\tan^{-1} y - x}{1 + y^2} = \frac{\tan^{-1} y}{1 + y^2} - \frac{x}{1 + y^2} $$

Rearranging into canonical Type II form:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} + \left(\frac{1}{1 + y^2}\right)x = \frac{\tan^{-1} y}{1 + y^2} $$

Comparing with $\frac{dx}{dy} + P_1(y)x = Q_1(y)$:

$$ P_1(y) = \frac{1}{1 + y^2}, \quad Q_1(y) = \frac{\tan^{-1} y}{1 + y^2} $$

Step 2: Find the Integrating Factor

$$ \text{I.F.} = e^{\int P_1(y)\,dy} = e^{\int \frac{1}{1 + y^2}\,dy} = e^{\tan^{-1} y} $$

Step 3: Apply the Solution Formula

$$ x \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q_1(y) \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dy + C $$

$$ x e^{\tan^{-1} y} = \int \left(\frac{\tan^{-1} y}{1 + y^2}\right) e^{\tan^{-1} y}\,dy + C \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Step 4: Evaluate the Integral
Let $u = \tan^{-1} y \implies du = \frac{1}{1 + y^2}\,dy$.

$$ \int u e^u\,du = u e^u - e^u = e^u(u - 1) $$

Substituting back $u = \tan^{-1} y$:

$$ \int \left(\frac{\tan^{-1} y}{1 + y^2}\right) e^{\tan^{-1} y}\,dy = e^{\tan^{-1} y}(\tan^{-1} y - 1) $$

Step 5: Write the Final Relation

$$ x e^{\tan^{-1} y} = e^{\tan^{-1} y}(\tan^{-1} y - 1) + C $$

$$ x = (\tan^{-1} y - 1) + C e^{-\tan^{-1} y} $$

💡 Example 3: Particular Solution with Boundary Condition
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2022, Term-2, 3 Marks]
Find the particular solution of the differential equation:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + y \cot x = 4x \csc x \quad (x \neq 0), \quad \text{given that } y = 0 \text{ when } x = \frac{\pi}{2} $$

Solution:

Step 1: Identify Canonical Components
Equation is already in standard form $\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x)$:

$$ P(x) = \cot x, \quad Q(x) = 4x \csc x $$

Step 2: Calculate $\text{I.F.}$

$$ \text{I.F.} = e^{\int \cot x\,dx} = e^{\ln |\sin x|} = \sin x \quad (\text{for } 0 < x < \pi) $$

Step 3: Solve for the General Integral

$$ y \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q(x) \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dx + C $$

$$ y \sin x = \int (4x \csc x) \cdot \sin x\,dx + C $$

Since $\csc x \cdot \sin x = 1$:

$$ y \sin x = \int 4x\,dx + C $$

$$ y \sin x = 2x^2 + C \quad \text{--- (General Solution)} $$

Step 4: Apply Initial Value Condition
Substitute $x = \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $y = 0$:

$$ 0 \cdot \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 2\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^2 + C $$

$$ 0 = 2\left(\frac{\pi^2}{4}\right) + C \implies C = -\frac{\pi^2}{2} $$

Step 5: State the Particular Solution

$$ y \sin x = 2x^2 - \frac{\pi^2}{2} $$

$$ y = 2x^2 \csc x - \frac{\pi^2}{2}\csc x $$

💡 Example 4: Leading Coefficient Normalization Trap
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2020, 5 Marks]
Solve the differential equation:

$$ (x \log x)\frac{dy}{dx} + y = \frac{2}{x}\log x $$

Solution:

Step 1: Convert to Standard Form
The coefficient of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ must be strictly $1$. Divide by $x \log x$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + \left(\frac{1}{x \log x}\right)y = \frac{2}{x^2} $$

Here, $P(x) = \frac{1}{x \log x}$ and $Q(x) = \frac{2}{x^2}$.

Step 2: Calculate $\text{I.F.}$

$$ \int P(x)\,dx = \int \frac{1}{x \log x}\,dx $$

Substitute $t = \log x \implies dt = \frac{1}{x}\,dx$:

$$ \int \frac{dt}{t} = \log |t| = \log |\log x| $$

Therefore:

$$ \text{I.F.} = e^{\log(\log x)} = \log x $$

Step 3: Integrate to Obtain the Solution

$$ y \cdot \log x = \int \left(\frac{2}{x^2} \cdot \log x\right)dx + C $$

Evaluate $I_2 = 2\int x^{-2} \log x\,dx$ using Integration by Parts: * First function: $u = \log x \implies u' = \frac{1}{x}$ * Second function: $v' = x^{-2} \implies v = -\frac{1}{x}$

$$ I_2 = 2 \left[ (\log x)\left(-\frac{1}{x}\right) - \int \left(-\frac{1}{x}\right)\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)dx \right] $$

$$ I_2 = 2 \left[ -\frac{\log x}{x} + \int x^{-2}\,dx \right] = 2 \left[ -\frac{\log x}{x} - \frac{1}{x} \right] = -\frac{2}{x}(\log x + 1) $$

Step 4: Final Expression

$$ y \log x = -\frac{2}{x}(1 + \log x) + C $$

4. Examiner Traps & Common Mistakes

Trap / Common Mistake Incorrect Student Action Correct Mathematical Protocol
1. Non-Unitary Leading Derivative Identifying $P(x)$ directly from $x\frac{dy}{dx} + 2y = x^2$ as $P=2$. First divide by the leading coefficient ($x$) to get $\frac{dy}{dx} + \frac{2}{x}y = x$. Here $P(x) = \frac{2}{x}$.
2. Sign Blindness in $P(x)$ In $\frac{dy}{dx} - (\tan x)y = \cos x$, treating $P(x) = \tan x$. The standard form is $+ P(x)y$. Thus $P(x) = -\tan x$, giving $\text{I.F.} = e^{-\int \tan x\,dx} = e^{-\ln|\sec x|} = \cos x$.
3. Logarithm Power Law Mismanagement Writing $e^{2\ln x} = 2x$ or $2e^{\ln x}$. Apply the power law first: $e^{k \ln x} = e^{\ln(x^k)} = x^k$. Hence $e^{2\ln x} = x^2$ and $e^{-\ln x} = x^{-1} = \frac{1}{x}$.
4. Premature Omission of $C$ Omitting $C$ in the general solution step and arbitrarily adding it at the very end as $y = \dots + C$. The factor $\text{I.F.}$ multiplies $y$ on the LHS. Dividing by $\text{I.F.}$ at the end affects $C$: $y = f(x) + \frac{C}{\text{I.F.}}$.
5. Type Confusion Forcing an equation with high powers of $y$ into Type I when $\frac{dx}{dy}$ would yield a linear equation in $x$. If $y$ appears with degree $> 1$ or inside difficult arguments, invert derivatives: $\frac{dx}{dy} = \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^{-1}$. Test for Type II.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

5.1 The Exponential Log Cancellation Rule

Always simplify the integrating factor before attempting the RHS integration:

$$ e^{n \ln(f(x))} = \big[f(x)\big]^n $$

  • $e^{-\ln(\cos x)} = (\cos x)^{-1} = \sec x$
  • $e^{-2\ln(x+1)} = (x+1)^{-2} = \frac{1}{(x+1)^2}$
  • $e^{3\ln x} = x^3$

5.2 The 10-Second Type Classifier

Given $f(x, y)\,dx + g(x, y)\,dy = 0$: 1. Check degrees of $x$ and $y$. 2. If $y$ is isolated to the first degree throughout the equation, it is Type I:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x) $$

  1. If $x$ is isolated to the first degree throughout the equation, it is Type II:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} + P_1(y)x = Q_1(y) $$

5.3 Quick Check for Correct I.F.

Differentiate your proposed $[y \cdot \text{I.F.}]$ mentally:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}[y \cdot \text{I.F.}] = \text{I.F.} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx} + (\text{I.F.} \cdot P)y $$

If factoring out $\text{I.F.}$ reproduces your original normalized LHS, your $\text{I.F.}$ is verified.

$$ -\sqrt{1 - y^2} = \sqrt{1 - x^2} - C \quad [1\text{ Mark}] $$

  1. Final General Solution:

$$ \sqrt{1 - x^2} + \sqrt{1 - y^2} = C \quad \left[\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}\right] $$

Question 10

[CBSE 2023 (Set 65/5/1), 3 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Show that the differential equation $(x - y)\frac{dy}{dx} = x + 2y$ is homogeneous and solve it.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution:

  1. Proof of Homogeneity:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x + 2y}{x - y} = F(x, y) $$

$$ F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \frac{\lambda x + 2\lambda y}{\lambda x - \lambda y} = \frac{\lambda(x + 2y)}{\lambda(x - y)} = \lambda^0 F(x, y) $$

Since $F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^0 F(x, y)$, it is homogeneous of degree $0$. $\quad [1\text{ Mark}]$

  1. Substitution $y = vx \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = v + x\frac{dv}{dx}$:

$$ v + x\frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{1 + 2v}{1 - v} $$

$$ x\frac{dv}{dx} = \frac{1 + 2v - v(1 - v)}{1 - v} = \frac{v^2 + v + 1}{1 - v} \quad \left[\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}\right] $$

  1. Variable Separation and Integration:

$$ \frac{v - 1}{v^2 + v + 1}\,dv = -\frac{dx}{x} $$

$$ \frac{1}{2}\int \frac{2v + 1}{v^2 + v + 1}\,dv - \frac{3}{2}\int \frac{dv}{\left(v + \frac{1}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)^2} = -\int \frac{dx}{x} $$

$$ \frac{1}{2}\ln(v^2 + v + 1) - \sqrt{3}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2v + 1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = -\ln|x| + C' \quad [1\text{ Mark}] $$

  1. Resubstituting $v = \frac{y}{x}$:

$$ \ln|x^2 + xy + y^2| - 2\sqrt{3}\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{2y + x}{\sqrt{3}x}\right) = C \quad \left[\frac{1}{2}\text{ Mark}\right] $$

Question 14

[CBSE 2024 / SQP 2023-24, 4 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know]

Case Passage:

A biologist observes the growth of a bacterial colony. The rate of increase of bacteria is directly proportional to the number of bacteria $N(t)$ present at any time $t$ (in hours):

$$ \frac{dN}{dt} = k N $$

At $t = 0$, the initial count was $1000$. After $2$ hours, the population reached $4000$.

Questions:

  1. Solve the differential equation and write the formula for $N(t)$ in terms of $k$ and $t$. [1 Mark]
  2. Find the value of constant $k$. [1 Mark]
  3. Find the bacterial population after $3$ hours. [2 Marks]
    (OR)
    Determine the time taken for the population to reach $16000$. [2 Marks]
📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Model Solution:

Sub-part (1):

$$ \frac{dN}{N} = k\,dt \implies \ln N = kt + C_1 \implies N(t) = C e^{kt} $$

At $t = 0, N = 1000 \implies C = 1000$.

$$ N(t) = 1000 e^{kt} \quad [1\text{ Mark}] $$

Sub-part (2):

At $t = 2, N = 4000$:

$$ 4000 = 1000 e^{2k} \implies e^{2k} = 4 $$

$$ 2k = \ln 4 = 2\ln 2 \implies k = \ln 2 \approx 0.693 \quad [1\text{ Mark}] $$

Sub-part (3):

$$ N(t) = 1000 (e^{\ln 2})^t = 1000 \cdot 2^t \quad [1\text{ Mark}] $$

For $t = 3$:

$$ N(3) = 1000 \cdot 2^3 = 8000 \text{ bacteria} \quad [1\text{ Mark}] $$

(OR Option)

Sub-part (3) Alternative:

$$ 16000 = 1000 \cdot 2^t \quad [1\text{ Mark}] $$

$$ 2^t = 16 = 2^4 \implies t = 4 \text{ hours} \quad [1\text{ Mark}] $$

  3. Form Selection: If $\frac{dy}{dx}$ has variables coupled in denominators, invert it to test for linear in $x$: $\frac{dx}{dy} + P(y)x = Q(y)$.

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1. Inspection & Exact Differential Forms

Before converting a differential equation into standard Linear or Homogeneous form, check if it can be grouped into an exact differential $d(u) = 0$: * $x\,dy + y\,dx = d(xy)$ * $\dfrac{x\,dy - y\,dx}{x^2} = d\left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right)$ $\quad\implies\quad x\,dy - y\,dx = x^2\,d\left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right) = -y^2\,d\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right)$ * $\dfrac{x\,dy - y\,dx}{xy} = d\left(\ln\left|\dfrac{y}{x}\right|\right)$ * $\dfrac{x\,dy - y\,dx}{x^2 + y^2} = d\left(\tan^{-1}\left(\dfrac{y}{x}\right)\right)$ * $\dfrac{x\,dx + y\,dy}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}} = d\left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\right)$ $\quad$ and $\quad x\,dx + y\,dy = \dfrac{1}{2}d(x^2+y^2)$

Shortcut in Action:
Solve $(x + 2y^3)\dfrac{dy}{dx} = y$.
Rearrange: $x\,dy - y\,dx + 2y^3\,dy = 0 \implies -\dfrac{y\,dx - x\,dy}{y^2} + 2y\,dy = 0 \implies -d\left(\dfrac{x}{y}\right) + d(y^2) = 0$.
Directly integrate: $-\dfrac{x}{y} + y^2 = C \implies y^3 - x = Cy$. (Saves 4–5 steps of converting to $\frac{dx}{dy} + P_1 x = Q_1$).

2. Identifying Linear Differential Equations (LDE) in $x$ vs $y$

  • Look at the degree of variables:
  • If $y$ appears only to the power $1$ (and not inside functions like $\sin y, e^y, \ln y$), it is linear in $y$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} + P(x)y = Q(x) $$

  • If $x$ appears only to the power $1$, it is linear in $x$:

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} + P_1(y)x = Q_1(y) $$

  • Quick Test: If $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y}{f(x, y)}$ where denominator cannot be split, take the reciprocal $\dfrac{dx}{dy}$ immediately.

3. Rapid Integrating Factor ($\text{I.F.}$) Rules

Use exponential-logarithm properties directly: * $e^{\int \frac{k}{x}\,dx} = e^{k\ln|x|} = e^{\ln|x|^k} = x^k$ * $e^{\int \tan x\,dx} = e^{\ln|\sec x|} = \sec x$ * $e^{-\int \tan x\,dx} = e^{-\ln|\sec x|} = e^{\ln|\cos x|} = \cos x$ * $e^{\int \cot x\,dx} = e^{\ln|\sin x|} = \sin x$ * $e^{-\int \cot x\,dx} = \csc x$ * $e^{\int \frac{1}{1+x^2}\,dx} = e^{\tan^{-1}x}$ * $e^{\int \frac{2x}{1+x^2}\,dx} = e^{\ln(1+x^2)} = 1+x^2$

4. Homogeneous Equations: Quick $y = vx$ Substitution Check

  • Test degree mentally: $f(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^0 f(x,y)$.
  • When substituting $y = vx \implies \dfrac{dy}{dx} = v + x\dfrac{dv}{dx}$:

$$ \text{Resulting form always separates directly as: } \frac{dv}{F(v) - v} = \frac{dx}{x} $$

  • Calculate $F(v) - v$ first to avoid algebraic mistakes.

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

1. Order and Degree Traps

  • Trap 1: Non-polynomial derivatives.
    If the differential equation contains terms like $\sin\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)$, $e^{\frac{dy}{dx}}$, or $\ln\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)$ that cannot be cleared via algebraic manipulation:

$$ \text{Order is defined, but Degree is \textbf{NOT DEFINED}}. $$

  • Example: $\dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2} + \cos\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right) = 0 \implies \text{Order} = 2, \text{Degree} = \text{Not Defined}$.
  • Contrast: $\sin\left(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right) = c \implies \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \sin^{-1}(c)$, which is a polynomial in derivatives $\implies \text{Order} = 1, \text{Degree} = 1$.
  • Trap 2: Radicals/Fractions.

$$ \left[1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2\right]^{3/2} = k\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} $$

Must be squared to clear fractional powers before identifying degree:

$$ \left[1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2\right]^3 = k^2\left(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right)^2 \implies \text{Order} = 2, \text{Degree} = 2. $$

2. The Arbitrary Constant Pitfall ($+ C$)

  • Missing $+C$ during the integration step costs $1$ full mark.
  • LDE Trap: In solving $y \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dx + C$, students often write:

$$ y = \frac{\int Q \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dx}{\text{I.F.}} + C \quad \mathbf{[FATAL ERROR]} $$

The constant $C$ must also be divided by $\text{I.F.}$:

$$ y = \frac{1}{\text{I.F.}}\left[ \int Q \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dx + C \right] $$

  • In homogeneous equations, write $+ C$ (or $\ln C$) as soon as integrals are evaluated, not at the very end.

3. Absolute Value Signs in Logarithms

  • Writing $\int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln x$ instead of $\ln|x|$ leads to deduction when determining particular solutions with negative initial conditions (e.g., $y(1) = -2$).
  • Always use:

$$ \int \frac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln|x| + C $$

4. Particular Solution & Step Marking Checklist

Examiners use the following typical 5-mark distribution: 1. Form Identification & Setup ($1$ mark): Stating the type (e.g., "Given equation is a linear differential equation of the form $\frac{dy}{dx} + Py = Q$ where $P = \dots, Q = \dots$"). 2. Integrating Factor / Substitution ($1$ mark): Explicit computation of $\text{I.F.} = e^{\int P\,dx}$ or substitution $y = vx, \frac{dy}{dx} = v + x\frac{dv}{dx}$. 3. General Solution Integration ($1.5$ marks): Correct execution of $\int Q \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dx$ with $+ C$. 4. Initial Value Substitution ($1$ mark): Substituting $x = x_0, y = y_0$ and solving for $C$. 5. Final Clean Form ($0.5$ mark): Re-substituting $C$ back into the general solution.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & Edge Cases)

  1. Number of Arbitrary Constants:
  2. General Solution of an $n$-th order differential equation contains exactly $n$ independent arbitrary constants.
  3. Particular Solution contains $0$ arbitrary constants.

  4. MCQ Trick Question: "The number of arbitrary constants in the general solution of a 4th order differential equation is 4. In a particular solution of the same equation, it is 0."

  5. Homogeneous Function Definition Verification:

  6. When asked to "Show that the differential equation is homogeneous and solve it", CBSE requires proving homogeneity first:

$$ F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^0 F(x, y) $$

  • Skipping this proof deducts $1$ mark, even if the differential equation is solved correctly.

  • Domain & Boundary Points:

  • In equations involving $\frac{dx}{dy} + P_1 x = Q_1$, identify variables carefully. A frequent error is writing $x \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int Q_1 \cdot (\text{I.F.})\,dx$ instead of $dy$.

  • Singular Solutions (NCERT Appendix Context):

  • Solutions that cannot be obtained from the general solution by assigning any value to the arbitrary constant are called singular solutions. CBSE tests only general and particular solutions.
