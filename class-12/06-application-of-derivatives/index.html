Rate of Change of Quantities

APPLICATION OF DERIVATIVES (CLASS 12 MATHEMATICS)

Topic: Rate of Change of Quantities

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Fundamental Definition & Geometric Interpretation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In Differential Calculus, derivative is the instantaneous rate of change of one variable with respect to another.

Let $y = f(x)$ be a real-valued function continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$. If $x$ undergoes a small increment $\Delta x$, then the corresponding increment in $y$ is:

$$ \Delta y = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) $$

The average rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$ over the interval $[x, x + \Delta x]$ is given by:

$$ \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} $$

The instantaneous rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$ at a specific point $x$ is defined as the limiting value of the average rate of change as $\Delta x \to 0$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} = f'(x) $$

Geometric Interpretation: $\frac{dy}{dx}\Big|_{x=x_0}$ represents the slope of the tangent line drawn to the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $P(x_0, f(x_0))$.

1.2 Sign Conventions & Physical Meaning ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

  • If $\frac{dy}{dx} > 0$: $y$ increases strictly as $x$ increases (direct relationship).
  • If $\frac{dy}{dx} < 0$: $y$ decreases strictly as $x$ increases (inverse relationship).
  • If $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$: $y$ is stationary with respect to changes in $x$.

Crucial Pedagogical Rule: When a problem states that a quantity is decreasing at a rate of $k$ units/s (where $k > 0$), mathematically its derivative with respect to time must be assigned a negative sign:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = -k $$

1.3 The Chain Rule for Related Rates ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

In practical applications, two variables $x$ and $y$ are functions of a third parameter—most commonly time $t$.

If $x = g(t)$ and $y = h(t)$ are differentiable functions of $t$, and $y = f(x)$, then by the Chain Rule of differentiation:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx} \cdot \frac{dx}{dt} $$

Equivalently, if $\frac{dx}{dt} \neq 0$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\frac{dy}{dt}}{\frac{dx}{dt}} $$

1.4 Marginal Analysis in Economics ⭐⭐⭐

  • Marginal Cost ($MC$): The instantaneous rate of change of the total cost $C(x)$ of producing $x$ units of an output with respect to the number of items produced:

$$ MC = \frac{d}{dx}[C(x)] $$

  • Marginal Revenue ($MR$): The instantaneous rate of change of the total revenue $R(x)$ received from the sale of $x$ units of an output:

$$ MR = \frac{d}{dx}[R(x)] $$

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Derivation 2.1: The Related Rates Formula for Spherical Geometry ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Context: Relating radial expansion $\frac{dr}{dt}$ to volumetric expansion $\frac{dV}{dt}$ and surface area expansion $\frac{dS}{dt}$.

Given: A spherical object of radius $r(t)$ expanding over time $t$. Volume of sphere: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$. Surface area of sphere: $S = 4\pi r^2$.

To Prove: 1. $\frac{dV}{dt} = S \cdot \frac{dr}{dt}$ 2. $\frac{dS}{dt} = \frac{2}{r} \frac{dV}{dt}$

Proof:

$$ V(t) = \frac{4}{3}\pi [r(t)]^3 $$

Differentiating both sides with respect to time $t$ using the Chain Rule:

$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\right) = \frac{4}{3}\pi \cdot \frac{d}{dr}(r^3) \cdot \frac{dr}{dt} $$

$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{4}{3}\pi \cdot \left(3r^2\right) \cdot \frac{dr}{dt} $$

$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2 \cdot \frac{dr}{dt} $$

Since surface area $S = 4\pi r^2$:

$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = S \cdot \frac{dr}{dt} $$

Now, consider the surface area $S(t) = 4\pi [r(t)]^2$. Differentiating with respect to $t$:

$$ \frac{dS}{dt} = 4\pi \cdot \frac{d}{dr}(r^2) \cdot \frac{dr}{dt} = 4\pi \cdot (2r) \cdot \frac{dr}{dt} = 8\pi r \frac{dr}{dt} $$

Rearranging the volume rate equation $\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\frac{dV}{dt}$ and substituting:

$$ \frac{dS}{dt} = 8\pi r \left( \frac{1}{4\pi r^2}\frac{dV}{dt} \right) = \frac{2}{r} \frac{dV}{dt} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2.2: Conical Liquid Level Depletion ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Context: Water pouring into or leaking from an inverted conical vessel.

Given: A right circular cone of fixed semi-vertical angle $\alpha$, or fixed base radius $R$ and height $H$. At time $t$, let $r$ be the radius of the water surface and $h$ be the instantaneous depth of water.

To Prove:

$$ \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{1}{\pi r^2} \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{1}{\pi h^2 \tan^2 \alpha} \frac{dV}{dt} $$

Construction & Setup: Consider the axial cross-section of the cone. From similar triangles:

$$ \frac{r}{h} = \frac{R}{H} = \tan \alpha \implies r = h \tan \alpha $$

Proof: The volume of the liquid cone at any instant is:

$$ V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h $$

Substitute $r = h \tan \alpha$:

$$ V = \frac{1}{3}\pi (h \tan \alpha)^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi \tan^2 \alpha \cdot h^3 $$

Differentiating implicitly with respect to $t$:

$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{1}{3}\pi \tan^2 \alpha \cdot \frac{d}{dt}(h^3) $$

$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{1}{3}\pi \tan^2 \alpha \cdot \left(3h^2 \frac{dh}{dt}\right) $$

$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = \pi h^2 \tan^2 \alpha \frac{dh}{dt} $$

Solving for $\frac{dh}{dt}$:

$$ \frac{dh}{dt} = \frac{1}{\pi h^2 \tan^2 \alpha} \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{1}{\pi (h \tan \alpha)^2}\frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{1}{\pi r^2}\frac{dV}{dt} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Derivation 2.3: Sliding Ladder / Pythagoras Rate Relation ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Given: A ladder of fixed length $L$ leans against a vertical wall. $x(t)$ = distance of the foot of the ladder from the wall. $y(t)$ = height of the top of the ladder from the ground.

To Prove:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{x}{y}\frac{dx}{dt} $$

Proof: In the right-angled triangle formed by the wall, the ground, and the ladder:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = L^2 $$

Differentiating both sides with respect to time $t$, noting that $L$ is constant ($\frac{dL}{dt} = 0$):

$$ \frac{d}{dt}(x^2) + \frac{d}{dt}(y^2) = \frac{d}{dt}(L^2) $$

$$ 2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 $$

Dividing by 2:

$$ x\frac{dx}{dt} + y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 $$

$$ y\frac{dy}{dt} = -x\frac{dx}{dt} \implies \frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{x}{y}\frac{dx}{dt} $$

Since $x > 0$, $y > 0$, and the ladder is pulled away from the wall ($\frac{dx}{dt} > 0$), it follows that:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} < 0 $$

confirming that the height of the top of the ladder decreases over time.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Increasing and Decreasing Functions & Monotonicity Tests

Class 12 Mathematics: Application of Derivatives

Topic: Increasing and Decreasing Functions & Monotonicity Tests

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

Concept Overview & NCERT Formal Definitions ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

  1. Strictly Increasing on $I$:

$$ x_1 < x_2 \implies f(x_1) < f(x_2), \quad \forall\, x_1, x_2 \in I $$

Geometric Meaning: As you move from left to right along the $x$-axis, the graph strictly rises. The tangent at any point makes an acute angle with the positive $x$-axis (except possibly at isolated inflection points).

  1. Increasing (Non-decreasing) on $I$:

$$ x_1 < x_2 \implies f(x_1) \le f(x_2), \quad \forall\, x_1, x_2 \in I $$

Geometric Meaning: The graph never falls. It may contain flat horizontal segments where $f'(x) = 0$ over an entire sub-interval.

  1. Strictly Decreasing on $I$:

$$ x_1 < x_2 \implies f(x_1) > f(x_2), \quad \forall\, x_1, x_2 \in I $$

Geometric Meaning: As you move from left to right, the curve strictly falls. The tangent at every point makes an obtuse angle with the positive $x$-axis.

  1. Decreasing (Non-increasing) on $I$:

$$ x_1 < x_2 \implies f(x_1) \ge f(x_2), \quad \forall\, x_1, x_2 \in I $$

Geometric Meaning: The graph never rises; it falls or stays flat horizontally.

  1. Monotonic Function: A function is said to be monotonic on $I$ if it is either entirely increasing on $I$ or entirely decreasing on $I$.

Theorem (First Derivative Test for Monotonicity) ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on the open interval $(a, b)$.

  1. $f$ is strictly increasing in $[a, b]$ if $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$ (and $f'(x) = 0$ does not hold identically over any open sub-interval).
  2. $f$ is strictly decreasing in $[a, b]$ if $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$ (and $f'(x) = 0$ does not hold identically over any open sub-interval).
  3. $f$ is a constant function in $[a, b]$ if $f'(x) = 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$.

Crucial NCERT Convention on Intervals:
If $f$ is continuous at the endpoints $a$ and $b$, the interval of strictly increasing/decreasing behavior is reported as closed $[a, b]$.
However, if the question asks "Find the intervals in which $f$ is strictly increasing/decreasing", CBSE marking schemes accept $(a, b)$ as well as $[a, b]$, but explicitly stating continuity at endpoints and writing $[a, b]$ guarantees full marks per NCERT exemplar guidelines.

2. Complete Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Theorem: First Derivative Monotonicity Criterion ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Case 1: Strict Increase

  • Given: $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$.
  • To Prove: $f$ is strictly increasing on $[a, b]$, i.e., for any $x_1, x_2 \in [a, b]$ with $x_1 < x_2$, we have $f(x_1) < f(x_2)$.

Proof: Take any two points $x_1, x_2 \in [a, b]$ such that $x_1 < x_2$.
Consider the sub-interval $[x_1, x_2] \subseteq [a, b]$.

  1. Since $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, it is continuous on $[x_1, x_2]$.
  2. Since $f$ is differentiable on $(a, b)$, it is differentiable on $(x_1, x_2)$.

By Lagrange's Mean Value Theorem (LMVT), there exists at least one $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ such that:

$$ f'(c) = \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1} $$

Cross-multiplying:

$$ f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) $$

We are given that:

$$ f'(x) > 0, \quad \forall\, x \in (a, b) \implies f'(c) > 0 $$

Also, since $x_1 < x_2$:

$$ x_2 - x_1 > 0 $$

Therefore, the product of two positive quantities is strictly positive:

$$ f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) > 0 \implies f(x_2) - f(x_1) > 0 \implies f(x_1) < f(x_2) $$

Since $x_1$ and $x_2$ were arbitrary points in $[a, b]$ with $x_1 < x_2$, $f$ is strictly increasing on $[a, b]$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Case 2: Strict Decrease

  • Given: $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$, differentiable on $(a, b)$, and $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$.
  • To Prove: $f$ is strictly decreasing on $[a, b]$, i.e., for any $x_1, x_2 \in [a, b]$ with $x_1 < x_2$, we have $f(x_1) > f(x_2)$.

Proof: Take any two arbitrary points $x_1, x_2 \in [a, b]$ with $x_1 < x_2$.
Applying LMVT on $[x_1, x_2]$, there exists $c \in (x_1, x_2)$ such that:

$$ f(x_2) - f(x_1) = f'(c)(x_2 - x_1) $$

Since $c \in (a, b)$, we have $f'(c) < 0$, while $(x_2 - x_1) > 0$.
The product of a negative number and a positive number is strictly negative:

$$ f(x_2) - f(x_1) < 0 \implies f(x_1) > f(x_2) $$

Hence, $f$ is strictly decreasing on $[a, b]$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Case 3: Identity for $f'(x) = 0$ (Isolated Zeros Principle) ⭐⭐⭐

If $f'(x) \ge 0$ on $(a, b)$, and $f'(x) = 0$ only at discrete/isolated points (i.e., not over any continuous interval $(\alpha, \beta)$), then $f(x)$ is strictly increasing on $[a, b]$.

Example: $f(x) = x^3 \implies f'(x) = 3x^2$.
Here, $f'(0) = 0$, but for all $x \neq 0$, $f'(x) > 0$.
For any $x_1 < x_2$, $x_1^3 < x_2^3$. Thus, $f(x) = x^3$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$.

Maxima and Minima: Local Extrema, First & Second Derivative Tests

Comprehensive Lecture Notes: Maxima and Minima

Class: XII CBSE / Senior Secondary Mathematics
Chapter: Application of Derivatives
Topic: Local Maxima & Minima, Critical Points, First & Second Derivative Tests
Target Audience: Board Aspirants (CBSE/State Boards) & Competitive Exams (JEE Main)

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Core Definitions

1.1 Local Extrema: Definitions & Geometry

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an open or closed interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$, and let $c \in I$.

Definition: Local Maximum

A point $c$ is said to be a point of local maximum of $f$ if there exists an open neighborhood $(c - h, c + h) \subseteq I$ for some $h > 0$ such that:

$$ f(c) \ge f(x) \quad \forall \, x \in (c - h, c + h) $$

The value $f(c)$ is called the local maximum value of $f$.

Definition: Local Minimum

A point $c$ is said to be a point of local minimum of $f$ if there exists an open neighborhood $(c - h, c + h) \subseteq I$ for some $h > 0$ such that:

$$ f(c) \le f(x) \quad \forall \, x \in (c - h, c + h) $$

The value $f(c)$ is called the local minimum value of $f$.

         Local Maxima (Crests)
               /\               /\
              /  \    /\       /  \
  ___________/    \__/  \_____/    \___________
                     \  /
                      \/
               Local Minima (Troughs)

Pedagogical Note: Local extrema are neighborhood properties, not global. A local minimum value can strictly exceed a local maximum value elsewhere on the interval.

1.2 Critical Points

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Definition (NCERT)

A point $c$ in the domain of a function $f$ is called a critical point if: 1. $f'(c) = 0$, OR 2. $f$ is not differentiable at $x = c$.

Key Distinction: Stationary Points vs. Critical Points

  • Stationary Point: Any point where the tangent is horizontal: $f'(c) = 0$.
  • Critical Point: Broader class including stationary points and points of non-differentiability (sharp corners, cusps, vertical tangents).

$$ \text{Stationary Points} \subset \text{Critical Points} $$

1.3 Point of Inflection

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

A point $c$ in the domain of $f$ where $f'(c) = 0$ (or does not exist), but $f'(x)$ does not change sign as $x$ increases through $c$, is neither a point of local maximum nor local minimum. Such a point is called a point of inflection (relative to extrema).

2. Fundamental Theorems & Complete Derivations

Theorem 1: Fermat's Theorem (Interior Extremum Theorem)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement

Let $f$ be defined on an open interval $(a, b)$ and let $c \in (a, b)$ be a point of local extremum. If $f$ is differentiable at $x = c$, then:

$$ f'(c) = 0 $$

Complete Step-by-Step Proof

  • Given:
  • $f$ is defined on $(a, b)$.
  • $c \in (a, b)$ is a point of local maximum.

  • $f'(c)$ exists.

  • To Prove:

$$ f'(c) = 0 $$

  • Proof: Since $c$ is a point of local maximum, there exists $h_0 > 0$ such that:

$$ f(x) \le f(c) \quad \forall \, x \in (c - h_0, c + h_0) $$

Equivalently, for any $h$ such that $0 < |h| < h_0$:

$$ f(c + h) - f(c) \le 0 $$

Case 1: Let $h > 0$ (Approaching from the right) Dividing by positive $h$:

$$ \frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h} \le 0 $$

Taking the right-hand limit as $h \to 0^+$:

$$ Rf'(c) = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h} \le 0 \quad \text{--- (1)} $$

Case 2: Let $h < 0$ (Approaching from the left) Let $h = -k$, where $k > 0$. Dividing $f(c + h) - f(c) \le 0$ by the negative quantity $h$:

$$ \frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h} \ge 0 $$

Taking the left-hand limit as $h \to 0^-$:

$$ Lf'(c) = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h} \ge 0 \quad \text{--- (2)} $$

Since $f$ is differentiable at $x = c$, the two one-sided derivatives must exist and be equal:

$$ f'(c) = Lf'(c) = Rf'(c) $$

From (1) and (2):

$$ 0 \le Lf'(c) = f'(c) = Rf'(c) \le 0 $$

$$ \implies f'(c) = 0 $$

(A symmetric argument applies when $c$ is a point of local minimum, reversing inequalities).

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2: First Derivative Test (FDT)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $f$ be continuous on an open interval $I$ containing critical point $c$, and differentiable on $I \setminus \{c\}$.

  1. Local Maximum: If $f'(x)$ changes sign from positive to negative as $x$ increases through $c$:
  2. $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (c - h, c)$

  3. $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (c, c + h)$ Then $x = c$ is a point of local maximum.

  4. Local Minimum: If $f'(x)$ changes sign from negative to positive as $x$ increases through $c$:

  5. $f'(x) < 0$ for all $x \in (c - h, c)$

  6. $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (c, c + h)$ Then $x = c$ is a point of local minimum.

  7. Neither (Inflection): If $f'(x)$ does not change sign as $x$ passes through $c$, then $c$ is a point of inflection.

Theorem 3: Second Derivative Test (SDT)

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Let $f$ be twice differentiable at a stationary point $c$ where $f'(c) = 0$:

  1. If $f''(c) < 0$, then $x = c$ is a point of local maximum.
  2. If $f''(c) > 0$, then $x = c$ is a point of local minimum.
  3. If $f''(c) = 0$, the test fails / is inconclusive. (Revert to the First Derivative Test or Higher Order Derivative Test).

Derivation of the Second Derivative Test

  • Given:
  • $f'(c) = 0$

  • $f''(c) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f'(c + h) - f'(c)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f'(c + h)}{h}$ exists.

  • Analysis for $f''(c) < 0$: By definition of limit:

$$ \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f'(c + h)}{h} = f''(c) < 0 $$

There exists a sufficiently small $\delta > 0$ such that for $0 < |h| < \delta$:

$$ \frac{f'(c + h)}{h} < 0 $$

  • For $h \in (-\delta, 0)$ ($h$ is negative):

$$ f'(c + h) > 0 \quad (\text{since } \text{numerator} \times \text{denominator} < 0) $$

  • For $h \in (0, \delta)$ ($h$ is positive):

$$ f'(c + h) < 0 $$

Thus, as $x$ increases through $c$:

$$ f'(x) \text{ changes sign from positive to negative} $$

By the First Derivative Test, $x = c$ is a point of local maximum.

  • Analysis for $f''(c) > 0$: Similarly, $\frac{f'(c+h)}{h} > 0$:
  • For $h < 0 \implies f'(c + h) < 0$
  • For $h > 0 \implies f'(c + h) > 0$ $f'(x)$ changes sign from negative to positive, establishing a local minimum.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Applied Optimization & Word Problems on Absolute Maxima and Minima

CHAPTER: APPLICATION OF DERIVATIVES (CLASS 12 MATHEMATICS)

TOPIC: APPLIED OPTIMIZATION & WORD PROBLEMS ON MAXIMA AND MINIMA

1. In-Depth Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 Core Definitions and NCERT Terminology

Let $f$ be a real-valued function defined on an interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$.

  • Local Maximum (⭐⭐⭐⭐): A point $c \in I$ is called a point of local maximum if there exists an open interval $(c - h, c + h) \subset I$ (with $h > 0$) such that:

$$ f(c) \ge f(x), \quad \forall x \in (c - h, c + h) $$

The value $f(c)$ is referred to as the local maximum value of $f$.

  • Local Minimum (⭐⭐⭐⭐): A point $c \in I$ is called a point of local minimum if there exists an open interval $(c - h, c + h) \subset I$ (with $h > 0$) such that:

$$ f(c) \le f(x), \quad \forall x \in (c - h, c + h) $$

The value $f(c)$ is referred to as the local minimum value of $f$.

  • Absolute (Global) Maximum and Minimum on a Closed Interval $[a, b]$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐): Let $f$ be continuous on a closed bounded interval $I = [a, b]$.
  • A point $c \in [a, b]$ is the point of absolute maximum if $f(c) \ge f(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$.
  • A point $d \in [a, b]$ is the point of absolute minimum if $f(d) \le f(x)$ for all $x \in [a, b]$.

  • Extreme Value Theorem: Every continuous function on a closed interval $[a, b]$ attains both its absolute maximum and absolute minimum values at least once.

  • Critical Points (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐): A point $c$ in the domain of $f$ is called a critical point if either:

$$ f'(c) = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad f'(c) \text{ does not exist} $$

1.2 Mathematical Toolkits for Optimization

Theorem 1: First Derivative Test (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $f$ be continuous on an open interval $I$ containing a critical point $c$ where $f'(c) = 0$: 1. If $f'(x)$ changes sign from positive to negative as $x$ increases through $c$ (i.e., $f'(x) > 0$ for $x < c$ and $f'(x) < 0$ for $x > c$), then $c$ is a point of local maximum. 2. If $f'(x)$ changes sign from negative to positive as $x$ increases through $c$ (i.e., $f'(x) < 0$ for $x < c$ and $f'(x) > 0$ for $x > c$), then $c$ is a point of local minimum. 3. If $f'(x)$ does not change sign as $x$ increases through $c$, then $c$ is a point of inflection.

Theorem 2: Second Derivative Test (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $f$ be a function defined on an interval $I$ and $c \in I$. Let $f$ be twice differentiable at $c$: 1. If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) < 0$, then $x = c$ is a point of local maximum, and $f(c)$ is the local maximum value. 2. If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) > 0$, then $x = c$ is a point of local minimum, and $f(c)$ is the local minimum value. 3. If $f'(c) = 0$ and $f''(c) = 0$, the test fails. One must revert to the First Derivative Test.

Proof 2.1: Cylinder of Maximum Volume Inscribed in a Sphere of Radius $R$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

  • Given: A sphere of fixed radius $R$. A right circular cylinder of radius $r$ and height $h$ inscribed within the sphere.
  • To Prove:
  • The height of the cylinder of maximum volume is $h = \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}}$.
  • The maximum volume is $V_{\max} = \frac{4\pi R^3}{3\sqrt{3}}$.
             +-------------------+
             |    .---------.    |
             |   /     |     \   |
             |  |      |h/2   |  |
             |  |   O--+--r---|  |  Sphere center O
             |  |    \        |  |  Hypotenuse = R
             |  |     \ R     |  |
             |   \     |     /   |
             |    '---------'    |
             +-------------------+
  • Proof: From the axial cross-section passing through the center $O$ of the sphere:

$$ \left(\frac{h}{2}\right)^2 + r^2 = R^2 \implies r^2 = R^2 - \frac{h^2}{4} $$

The volume $V$ of the cylinder is:

$$ V = \pi r^2 h $$

Substituting $r^2$:

$$ V(h) = \pi \left(R^2 - \frac{h^2}{4}\right) h = \pi \left(R^2 h - \frac{h^3}{4}\right) $$

Physical domain for height: $h \in (0, 2R)$.

Differentiating with respect to $h$:

$$ \frac{dV}{dh} = \pi \left(R^2 - \frac{3h^2}{4}\right) $$

For extreme values, set $\frac{dV}{dh} = 0$:

$$ \pi \left(R^2 - \frac{3h^2}{4}\right) = 0 \implies \frac{3h^2}{4} = R^2 \implies h^2 = \frac{4R^2}{3} \implies h = \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}} $$

Differentiating again:

$$ \frac{d^2V}{dh^2} = \pi \left(0 - \frac{6h}{4}\right) = -\frac{3\pi h}{2} $$

Evaluating at $h = \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}}$:

$$ \left. \frac{d^2V}{dh^2} \right|_{h = \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}}} = -\frac{3\pi}{2}\left(\frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = -\sqrt{3}\pi R < 0 \quad (\because R > 0) $$

By the Second Derivative Test, $V$ attains its maximum when $h = \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}}$.

Substituting $h = \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}}$ into the volume expression:

$$ V_{\max} = \pi \left( R^2 \cdot \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}} - \frac{1}{4}\cdot \frac{8R^3}{3\sqrt{3}} \right) = \pi R^3 \left( \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} - \frac{2}{3\sqrt{3}} \right) = \frac{4\pi R^3}{3\sqrt{3}} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Cone of Maximum Volume Inscribed in a Sphere of Radius $R$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

  • Given: A sphere of fixed radius $R$. A right circular cone inscribed inside it with base radius $r$ and height $h$.
  • To Prove: The volume of the cone is maximum when $h = \frac{4R}{3}$, and $V_{\text{cone, max}} = \frac{8}{27} V_{\text{sphere}}$.
  • Proof: Let the sphere have center $O(0, 0)$ and radius $R$. Let the vertex of the cone be at $(0, R)$ and its flat base parallel to the horizontal axis at distance $x$ below $O$, where $x \in (-R, R)$.
  • Height of cone: $h = R + x$
  • Radius of base of cone: $r = \sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$

Volume of the cone:

$$ V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \frac{1}{3}\pi (R^2 - x^2)(R + x) $$

Expand the function:

$$ V(x) = \frac{\pi}{3} (R^3 + R^2 x - R x^2 - x^3) $$

Differentiating with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{dV}{dx} = \frac{\pi}{3} (R^2 - 2Rx - 3x^2) = \frac{\pi}{3} (R - 3x)(R + x) $$

For stationary points, set $\frac{dV}{dx} = 0$:

$$ (R - 3x)(R + x) = 0 \implies x = \frac{R}{3} \quad (\text{since } x = -R \text{ yields } h=0 \text{, rejected}) $$

Second derivative:

$$ \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} = \frac{\pi}{3} (-2R - 6x) $$

At $x = \frac{R}{3}$:

$$ \left. \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} \right|_{x = \frac{R}{3}} = \frac{\pi}{3} \left(-2R - 6\left(\frac{R}{3}\right)\right) = \frac{\pi}{3}(-4R) = -\frac{4\pi R}{3} < 0 $$

Hence, $V$ is maximized at $x = \frac{R}{3}$.

  • Height of the cone:

$$ h = R + \frac{R}{3} = \frac{4R}{3} $$

  • Maximum volume:

$$ V_{\max} = \frac{1}{3}\pi \left(R^2 - \frac{R^2}{9}\right)\left(\frac{4R}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{3}\pi \left(\frac{8R^2}{9}\right)\left(\frac{4R}{3}\right) = \frac{32\pi R^3}{81} $$

Comparing to the volume of the sphere $V_s = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3$:

$$ V_{\max} = \frac{8}{27} \left(\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3\right) = \frac{8}{27} V_{\text{sphere}} $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.3: Right Circular Cone of Minimum Curved Surface Area for a Given Volume $V$ (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

  • Given: A right circular cone of fixed volume $V_0$, base radius $r$, height $h$, and slant height $l = \sqrt{r^2 + h^2}$.
  • To Prove: The curved surface area is minimized when $h = \sqrt{2}r$.
  • Proof: Given constant volume:

$$ V_0 = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h \implies h = \frac{3V_0}{\pi r^2} $$

Curved surface area:

$$ S = \pi r l = \pi r \sqrt{r^2 + h^2} $$

To simplify computation, define $Z = S^2$:

$$ Z = \pi^2 r^2 (r^2 + h^2) = \pi^2 r^4 + \pi^2 r^2 h^2 $$

Substitute $h = \frac{3V_0}{\pi r^2}$:

$$ Z(r) = \pi^2 r^4 + \pi^2 r^2 \left(\frac{9V_0^2}{\pi^2 r^4}\right) = \pi^2 r^4 + \frac{9V_0^2}{r^2} $$

Differentiating with respect to $r$:

$$ \frac{dZ}{dr} = 4\pi^2 r^3 - \frac{18V_0^2}{r^3} $$

Set $\frac{dZ}{dr} = 0$:

$$ 4\pi^2 r^3 = \frac{18V_0^2}{r^3} \implies 4\pi^2 r^6 = 18 V_0^2 = 18 \left(\frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h\right)^2 = 18 \cdot \frac{1}{9}\pi^2 r^4 h^2 = 2\pi^2 r^4 h^2 $$

Dividing both sides by $2\pi^2 r^4$ ($r > 0$):

$$ 2r^2 = h^2 \implies h = \sqrt{2}r $$

Second derivative:

$$ \frac{d^2Z}{dr^2} = 12\pi^2 r^2 + \frac{54V_0^2}{r^4} > 0 \quad \forall r > 0 $$

Because $S > 0$, minimizing $Z = S^2$ minimizes $S$. Thus, the curved surface area is minimum when $h = \sqrt{2}r$.

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Problem: A ladder $5\text{ m}$ long is leaning against a wall. The bottom of the ladder is pulled along the ground, away from the wall, at the rate of $2\text{ cm/s}$. How fast is its height on the wall decreasing when the foot of the ladder is $4\text{ m}$ away from the wall?

Solution: Let the distance of the foot of the ladder from the wall be $x\text{ m}$, and the height of the top of the ladder on the wall be $y\text{ m}$. Length of ladder $L = 5\text{ m}$ (constant).

Given:

$$ \frac{dx}{dt} = 2\text{ cm/s} = 0.02\text{ m/s} $$

At the given instant, $x = 4\text{ m}$.

By the Pythagorean theorem:

$$ x^2 + y^2 = 5^2 \implies x^2 + y^2 = 25 $$

When $x = 4\text{ m}$:

$$ 4^2 + y^2 = 25 \implies y^2 = 9 \implies y = 3\text{ m} \quad (\text{since } y > 0) $$

Differentiating $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ with respect to $t$:

$$ 2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \implies x\frac{dx}{dt} + y\frac{dy}{dt} = 0 $$

Substituting $x = 4$, $y = 3$, and $\frac{dx}{dt} = 0.02$:

$$ 4(0.02) + 3\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right) = 0 $$

$$ 0.08 + 3\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right) = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{0.08}{3} = -\frac{8}{300} = -\frac{2}{75}\text{ m/s} $$

Or in $\text{cm/s}$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = -\frac{8}{3}\text{ cm/s} $$

Conclusion: The height of the ladder on the wall is decreasing at the rate of $\frac{8}{3}\text{ cm/s}$ (or $\frac{2}{75}\text{ m/s}$).

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

5.1 The "Derivative of Log" Trick for Products and Quotients

When a formula consists of products of powers, e.g., $V = \pi r^2 h$ (Cylinder):

$$ \ln V = \ln \pi + 2\ln r + \ln h $$

Differentiating with respect to $t$ yields fractional / percentage rates:

$$ \frac{1}{V}\frac{dV}{dt} = 2\left(\frac{1}{r}\frac{dr}{dt}\right) + \frac{1}{h}\frac{dh}{dt} $$

This converts complex geometric product rules into quick single-line mental checks.

5.2 The 1-2-3 Dimension Scaling Shortcut

For any geometric shape scaled proportionally (where all linear dimensions change at rate $\frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt}$): * Linear dimension ($L$): Rate scales with degree $1 \implies \frac{1}{L}\frac{dL}{dt} = k$ * Area ($A \propto L^2$): $\frac{1}{A}\frac{dA}{dt} = 2k$ * Volume ($V \propto L^3$): $\frac{1}{V}\frac{dV}{dt} = 3k$

Example: If the radius of a sphere increases by $2\%$, its surface area increases by $4\%$ and its volume increases by $6\%$.

5.3 Quick Ladder Invariant

For a ladder sliding against a vertical wall:

$$ x \frac{dx}{dt} + y \frac{dy}{dt} = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dt} = -\tan \theta \cdot \frac{dx}{dt} $$

where $\theta$ is the angle made by the ladder with the ground ($\tan \theta = \frac{y}{x}$). * Use this to verify algebraic solutions in 5 seconds.

5.4 Mnemonic for Word Problems: "D-R-D-S-U" Protocol

  • D – Diagram: Sketch the figure; assign variable letters to varying quantities and fixed constants to constants.
  • R – Relation: Write the primary equation connecting the variables ($x^2+y^2=L^2$, $V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h$).
  • D – Differentiate: Differentiate implicitly with respect to $t$ using the Chain Rule.
  • S – Substitute: Plug in the given boundary conditions and rates.
  • U – Units & Sign: Append appropriate units (e.g., $\text{cm}^3/\text{s}$, $\text{m/s}$) and check the final sign.

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{(2+x)^2 - 4(1+x)}{(1+x)(2+x)^2} = \frac{(4 + 4x + x^2) - (4 + 4x)}{(1+x)(2+x)^2} = \frac{x^2}{(1+x)(2+x)^2} $$

  1. Analyze the Sign:
  2. $x^2 \ge 0$ for all real $x$ (with $x^2 = 0 \iff x = 0$).
  3. For $x > -1$: $1 + x > 0$.
  4. For $x > -1$: $(2 + x)^2 > 0$ (since $x \neq -2$).

Thus:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} \ge 0, \quad \forall\, x > -1 $$

Since $\frac{dy}{dx} = 0$ only at the isolated point $x = 0$, $y$ is strictly increasing for all $x > -1$.

Question: Find the least value of $a$ such that the function $f(x) = x^2 + ax + 1$ is strictly increasing on $[1, 2]$.

Solution: 1. Find Derivative:

$$ f'(x) = 2x + a $$

  1. Apply Condition for Strict Increase on $[1, 2]$: $f(x)$ is strictly increasing on $[1, 2]$ if:

$$ f'(x) > 0, \quad \forall\, x \in (1, 2) $$

$$ \implies 2x + a > 0 \implies a > -2x, \quad \forall\, x \in (1, 2) $$

  1. Enforce Boundary Bounds: Since $-2x$ is a decreasing function, its maximum value on $[1, 2]$ occurs at the left endpoint $x = 1$:

$$ \max_{x \in [1, 2]} (-2x) = -2(1) = -2 $$

To guarantee $2x + a \ge 0$ across the entire interval $[1, 2]$, we require:

$$ f'(1) \ge 0 \implies 2(1) + a \ge 0 \implies a \ge -2 $$

  1. Least Value: The least value of $a$ is $-2$.

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Points

1. The Quadratic Monotonicity Rule

For $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ ($a > 0$): - Strictly decreasing on $\left(-\infty, -\frac{b}{2a}\right]$ - Strictly increasing on $\left[-\frac{b}{2a}, \infty\right)$ (Vertex occurs at $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$, where tangent is horizontal).

2. The Linear Fractional (Möbius) Shortcut

For rational functions of the form:

$$ f(x) = \frac{ax + b}{cx + d} $$

$$ f'(x) = \frac{(cx + d)(a) - (ax + b)(c)}{(cx + d)^2} = \frac{ad - bc}{(cx + d)^2} $$

  • The denominator $(cx + d)^2 > 0$ everywhere in the domain.
  • Golden Rule: The sign of $f'(x)$ is entirely determined by the determinant $\Delta = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = ad - bc$:
  • If $ad - bc > 0 \implies f(x)$ is strictly increasing on every interval in its domain.
  • If $ad - bc < 0 \implies f(x)$ is strictly decreasing on every interval in its domain.

3. Quick Derivative Sign Inspection (Wavy Curve / Sign Table Method)

  1. Differentiate and write $f'(x)$ in completely factored form:

$$ f'(x) = k \cdot (x - r_1)^{m_1} (x - r_2)^{m_2} \dots (x - r_n)^{m_n} $$

  1. Plot roots $r_1, r_2, \dots$ on the real line.
  2. Start from the rightmost region: if the leading coefficient is positive, the sign is positive ($+$).
  3. Alternate signs when passing an odd power root; keep the same sign when passing an even power root.

Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE Standard Model / Exemplar] Find all local extrema for $f(x) = (x - 2)^{2/3}(2x + 1)$.

Solution:

Step 1: Compute $f'(x)$ using Product Rule

$$ f(x) = (x - 2)^{2/3}(2x + 1) $$

$$ f'(x) = \frac{2}{3}(x - 2)^{-1/3}(2x + 1) + (x - 2)^{2/3} \cdot 2 $$

$$ f'(x) = \frac{2(2x + 1) + 6(x - 2)}{3(x - 2)^{1/3}} = \frac{4x + 2 + 6x - 12}{3(x - 2)^{1/3}} = \frac{10x - 10}{3(x - 2)^{1/3}} = \frac{10(x - 1)}{3(x - 2)^{1/3}} $$

Step 2: Identify Critical Points * $f'(x) = 0 \implies 10(x - 1) = 0 \implies x = 1$ * $f'(x)$ does not exist when $(x - 2)^{1/3} = 0 \implies x = 2$ Both $x = 1$ and $x = 2$ belong to the domain $\mathbb{R}$.

Step 3: First Derivative Sign Analysis * Interval $(-\infty, 1)$: Choose $x = 0 \implies f'(0) = \frac{10(-1)}{3(-2)^{1/3}} = \frac{-}{ -} > 0$ * Interval $(1, 2)$: Choose $x = 1.5 \implies f'(1.5) = \frac{10(0.5)}{3(-0.5)^{1/3}} = \frac{+}{-} < 0$ * Interval $(2, \infty)$: Choose $x = 3 \implies f'(3) = \frac{10(2)}{3(1)^{1/3}} = \frac{+}{+} > 0$

Step 4: Conclusion * At $x = 1$: $f'$ changes from $+$ to $-$ $\implies$ Local Maximum at $x = 1$.

$$ f(1) = (1 - 2)^{2/3}(2(1) + 1) = (-1)^{2/3}(3) = 1 \cdot 3 = 3 $$

  • At $x = 2$: $f'$ changes from $-$ to $+$ $\implies$ Local Minimum at $x = 2$.

$$ f(2) = 0 $$

💡 Example 2: Trigonometric Extrema
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2020, 4 Marks] Find the points of local maxima and minima of $f(x) = \sin 2x - x$ in the interval $\left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right]$.

Solution:

Step 1: Differentiate

$$ f'(x) = 2\cos 2x - 1 $$

Step 2: Find Stationary Points

$$ f'(x) = 0 \implies 2\cos 2x - 1 = 0 \implies \cos 2x = \frac{1}{2} $$

Given $x \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right] \implies 2x \in [-\pi, \pi]$:

$$ 2x = -\frac{\pi}{3} \quad \text{or} \quad 2x = \frac{\pi}{3} $$

$$ x = -\frac{\pi}{6}, \quad x = \frac{\pi}{6} $$

Step 3: Second Derivative Test

$$ f''(x) = \frac{d}{dx}(2\cos 2x - 1) = -4\sin 2x $$

  • At $x = \frac{\pi}{6}$:

$$ f''\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = -4\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = -4\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = -2\sqrt{3} < 0 $$

Therefore, $x = \frac{\pi}{6}$ is a point of Local Maximum.

$$ f\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} - \frac{\pi}{6} $$

  • At $x = -\frac{\pi}{6}$:

$$ f''\left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = -4\sin\left(-\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = 4\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) = 2\sqrt{3} > 0 $$

Therefore, $x = -\frac{\pi}{6}$ is a point of Local Minimum.

$$ f\left(-\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} + \frac{\pi}{6} $$

💡 Example 3: Applied Optimization (Mensuration)
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2023, 5 Marks] Show that the height of the cylinder of maximum volume that can be inscribed in a sphere of radius $R$ is $\frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}}$. Also find the maximum volume.

Solution:

           .---------.
        .-'     |     '-.
      .'     r  |        '.
     /       <--+-->       \
    |        |  |   |       |
    |--------+--O---+-------|  Sphere Radius = R
    |        |  | x |       |
     \       <--+-->       /
      '.        |        .'
        '-.     |     .-'
           '---------'

Step 1: Set up Variables & Constraint Let the center of the sphere be the origin $O$. Let the cylinder have radius $r$ and height $h$. Let $x$ be the distance from $O$ to the upper circular base.

$$ \implies h = 2x, \quad \text{where } 0 < x < R $$

From the right triangle in the sphere:

$$ r^2 + x^2 = R^2 \implies r^2 = R^2 - x^2 $$

Step 2: Formulate Objective Function The volume $V$ of the cylinder is:

$$ V = \pi r^2 h = \pi (R^2 - x^2)(2x) = 2\pi (R^2 x - x^3) $$

Step 3: Differentiate with respect to $x$

$$ \frac{dV}{dx} = 2\pi(R^2 - 3x^2) $$

For maximum volume, set $\frac{dV}{dx} = 0$:

$$ 2\pi(R^2 - 3x^2) = 0 \implies 3x^2 = R^2 \implies x = \frac{R}{\sqrt{3}} \quad (\text{since } x > 0) $$

Step 4: Confirm Maximum using Second Derivative

$$ \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} = 2\pi(0 - 6x) = -12\pi x $$

At $x = \frac{R}{\sqrt{3}}$:

$$ \left.\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}\right|_{x = \frac{R}{\sqrt{3}}} = -12\pi\left(\frac{R}{\sqrt{3}}\right) = -4\sqrt{3}\pi R < 0 \quad (\text{since } R > 0) $$

By the Second Derivative Test, $V$ is maximum at $x = \frac{R}{\sqrt{3}}$.

Step 5: Compute Required Values * Height of cylinder:

$$ h = 2x = \frac{2R}{\sqrt{3}} $$

  • Maximum Volume:

$$ V_{\max} = 2\pi\left(R^2\left(\frac{R}{\sqrt{3}}\right) - \left(\frac{R}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^3\right) = 2\pi R^3\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} - \frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{4\pi R^3}{3\sqrt{3}} $$

💡 Example 4: Board Application Problem
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2024, 3 Marks] Find the dimensions of a rectangle of perimeter $36\text{ cm}$ which will sweep out a cylinder of maximum volume when revolved about one of its sides.

Solution:

Let the sides of the rectangle be $x$ and $y$.

$$ \text{Perimeter } 2(x + y) = 36 \implies x + y = 18 \implies y = 18 - x \quad (0 < x < 18) $$

Revolving about the side of length $y$: * Cylinder radius $r = x$ * Cylinder height $h = y = 18 - x$

Volume $V$:

$$ V(x) = \pi r^2 h = \pi x^2 (18 - x) = \pi (18x^2 - x^3) $$

$$ \frac{dV}{dx} = \pi(36x - 3x^2) = 3\pi x(12 - x) $$

Setting $\frac{dV}{dx} = 0$ for $x \in (0, 18)$:

$$ x = 12\text{ cm} $$

Confirming extremum:

$$ \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} = \pi(36 - 6x) $$

At $x = 12$:

$$ \left.\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}\right|_{x = 12} = \pi(36 - 72) = -36\pi < 0 \implies \text{Maximum} $$

Dimensions of the rectangle:

$$ x = 12\text{ cm}, \quad y = 18 - 12 = 6\text{ cm} $$

💡 Example 5: Case Study / Optimization
Solved Example ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Must-Know

[CBSE 2025 Sample Question Paper, 4 Marks] An open rectangular box with a square base is to be made from $48\text{ m}^2$ of sheet metal. Determine the dimensions so that the volume is maximum.

Solution:

Let the side of the square base be $x$ meters and height be $y$ meters ($x > 0, y > 0$).

Surface Area of Open Box:

$$ S = x^2 + 4xy = 48 \implies 4xy = 48 - x^2 \implies y = \frac{48 - x^2}{4x} $$

Volume:

$$ V = x^2 y = x^2\left(\frac{48 - x^2}{4x}\right) = \frac{x(48 - x^2)}{4} = 12x - \frac{x^3}{4} $$

First Derivative:

$$ \frac{dV}{dx} = 12 - \frac{3x^2}{4} $$

Setting $\frac{dV}{dx} = 0$:

$$ \frac{3x^2}{4} = 12 \implies x^2 = 16 \implies x = 4\text{ m} \quad (\text{rejecting } x = -4) $$

Second Derivative:

$$ \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} = -\frac{6x}{4} = -\frac{3x}{2} $$

At $x = 4$:

$$ \left.\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}\right|_{x = 4} = -\frac{3(4)}{2} = -6 < 0 \implies \text{Maximum} $$

Dimensions:

$$ x = 4\text{ m}, \quad y = \frac{48 - 4^2}{4(4)} = \frac{32}{16} = 2\text{ m} $$

$$ \text{Base } = 4\text{ m} \times 4\text{ m}, \quad \text{Height } = 2\text{ m} $$

1. Monotonic Transformation Trick

If $g(u)$ is a strictly increasing function (e.g., $g(u) = u^2$ for $u > 0$, or $g(u) = \ln u$), then $f(x)$ and $g(f(x))$ achieve extrema at the exact same values of $x$.

For positive variables:

$$ \frac{a + b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab} $$

Problem: An open box with a square base is to be made out of a given quantity of cardboard of area $c^2$ square units. Show that the maximum volume of the box is $\frac{c^3}{6\sqrt{3}}$ cubic units.

  • Solution:
  • Let the side of the square base be $x$ and the height of the open box be $y$ ($x, y > 0$).
  • The box is open at the top. Its surface area consists of 1 square base and 4 vertical faces:

$$ S = x^2 + 4xy = c^2 \implies y = \frac{c^2 - x^2}{4x} $$

  1. The volume $V$ of the box is:

$$ V = x^2 y = x^2 \left(\frac{c^2 - x^2}{4x}\right) = \frac{1}{4}(c^2 x - x^3) $$

  1. Differentiating with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{dV}{dx} = \frac{1}{4}(c^2 - 3x^2) $$

 Setting $\frac{dV}{dx} = 0$:

$$ c^2 - 3x^2 = 0 \implies x^2 = \frac{c^2}{3} \implies x = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3}} \quad (\because x > 0) $$

  1. Second derivative check:

$$ \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} = \frac{1}{4}(0 - 6x) = -\frac{3x}{2} $$

 At $x = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3}}$:

$$ \left. \frac{d^2V}{dx^2} \right|_{x = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3}}} = -\frac{3c}{2\sqrt{3}} < 0 \quad (\because c > 0) $$

 Therefore, $V$ is maximum at $x = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3}}$.
  1. Maximum volume calculation:

$$ V_{\max} = \frac{1}{4}\left(c^2 \cdot \frac{c}{\sqrt{3}} - \left(\frac{c}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^3\right) = \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{c^3}{\sqrt{3}} - \frac{c^3}{3\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{2c^3}{3\sqrt{3}}\right) = \frac{c^3}{6\sqrt{3}} $$

$$ \text{Hence Proved.} $$

Problem: Find the point on the curve $y^2 = 4x$ which is nearest to the point $(2, -8)$.

  • Solution:
  • Let $P(x, y)$ be an arbitrary point on the parabola $y^2 = 4x$. Express $x$ in terms of $y$: $x = \frac{y^2}{4}$.

$$ f(y) = \frac{y^4}{16} - y^2 + 4 + y^2 + 16y + 64 = \frac{y^4}{16} + 16y + 68 $$

  1. Differentiating with respect to $y$:

$$ f'(y) = \frac{4y^3}{16} + 16 = \frac{y^3}{4} + 16 $$

 Setting $f'(y) = 0$:

$$ \frac{y^3}{4} + 16 = 0 \implies y^3 = -64 \implies y = -4 $$

  1. Second derivative:

$$ f''(y) = \frac{3y^2}{4} $$

 At $y = -4$:

$$ f''(-4) = \frac{3(-4)^2}{4} = 12 > 0 $$

 Thus, the distance is minimized at $y = -4$.

$$ x = \frac{(-4)^2}{4} = \frac{16}{4} = 4 $$

The function $f(x) = x^2 e^{-x}$ is strictly increasing in the interval: - (A) $(-\infty, \infty)$ - (B) $(-2, 0)$ - (C) $(2, \infty)$ - (D) $(0, 2)$

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution:

  1. Differentiate Using Product Rule: $$f'(x) = \dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2) \cdot e^{-x} + x^2 \cdot \dfrac{d}{dx}(e^{-x})$$ $$f'(x) = 2x e^{-x} + x^2 (-e^{-x}) = x e^{-x} (2 - x)$$
  2. Condition for Strictly Increasing: $$f'(x) > 0 \implies x e^{-x} (2 - x) > 0$$ Since $e^{-x} > 0$ for all real $x$, divide by $e^{-x}$: $$x (2 - x) > 0 \implies x (x - 2) < 0$$
  3. Sign Analysis: The roots are $x = 0$ and $x = 2$. By the wavy-curve rule, $x(x - 2) < 0$ for: $$x \in (0, 2)$$

Correct Option: (D) $(0, 2)$

CBSE Marking Scheme Breakdown:

  • Computing $f'(x) = xe^{-x}(2 - x)$: [½ Mark]
  • Solving inequality $x(2 - x) > 0$ to get $(0, 2)$ and selecting (D): [½ Mark]

Topic: Monotonicity of Cubic Polynomial

Find the intervals in which the function $f(x) = 2x^3 - 9x^2 + 12x + 15$ is: 1. Strictly increasing 2. Strictly decreasing

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution:

  1. Differentiate $f(x)$: $$f'(x) = 6x^2 - 18x + 12 = 6(x^2 - 3x + 2) = 6(x - 1)(x - 2)$$ [½ Mark]
  2. Find Critical Points: Set $f'(x) = 0 \implies 6(x - 1)(x - 2) = 0 \implies x = 1, x = 2$. These points divide the real line into three disjoint intervals: $(-\infty, 1)$, $(1, 2)$, and $(2, \infty)$. [½ Mark]
  3. Sign of $f'(x)$:
  4. On $(-\infty, 1)$: choose $x = 0 \implies f'(0) = 6(-1)(-2) = 12 > 0$. Strictly increasing.
  5. On $(1, 2)$: choose $x = 1.5 \implies f'(1.5) = 6(0.5)(-0.5) = -1.5 < 0$. Strictly decreasing.
  6. On $(2, \infty)$: choose $x = 3 \implies f'(3) = 6(2)(1) = 12 > 0$. Strictly increasing.
  7. Conclusion:
  8. (i) Strictly increasing: $(-\infty, 1) \cup (2, \infty)$ [½ Mark]
  9. (ii) Strictly decreasing: $(1, 2)$ [½ Mark]

Topic: Local Maximum of Logarithmic Quotient

Find the local maximum value of the function $f(x) = \dfrac{\log x}{x}$ for $x > 0$.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution:

  1. First Derivative by Quotient Rule: $$f'(x) = \dfrac{x \cdot \frac{1}{x} - \log x \cdot 1}{x^2} = \dfrac{1 - \log x}{x^2}$$ [½ Mark]
  2. Critical Point: Set $f'(x) = 0 \implies 1 - \log x = 0 \implies \log x = 1 \implies x = e$. [½ Mark]
  3. First Derivative Test / Nature of Critical Point:
  4. For $x < e$: $\log x < 1 \implies 1 - \log x > 0 \implies f'(x) > 0$.
  5. For $x > e$: $\log x > 1 \implies 1 - \log x < 0 \implies f'(x) < 0$. Since $f'(x)$ changes sign from positive to negative as $x$ increases through $e$, $x = e$ is a point of local maximum. [½ Mark]
  6. Local Maximum Value: $$f(e) = \dfrac{\log e}{e} = \dfrac{1}{e}$$ [½ Mark]

  1. Geometric Formulation: Let $x$ be the distance of the foot of the ladder from the wall, and $y$ be the height of the top of the ladder on the wall. By the Pythagorean theorem: $$x^2 + y^2 = 5^2 = 25$$ [½ Mark]

Case Context: Architectural Optimization of Acoustic Chamber

An architect designs an open-top rectangular acoustic testing chamber with a square base of side $x\text{ meters}$ and vertical height $y\text{ meters}$. To satisfy acoustic vibration damping standards, the internal volume must be exactly $108\text{ m}^3$.

Sub-questions: 1. Express the total surface area $S$ of the open box as an algebraic function in terms of $x$ only. [1 Mark] 2. Find the critical dimension $x$ for which the surface area is stationary. [1 Mark] 3. (i) Using the second derivative test, show that the surface area is minimum at this critical dimension, and find the minimum surface area. [2 Marks]
OR
(ii) If acoustic soundproofing panels cost ₹$250/\text{m}^2$, compute the minimum expenditure needed to line the chamber. [2 Marks]

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution:

  1. Express Surface Area Function $S(x)$: Volume of chamber: $V = x^2 y = 108 \implies y = \dfrac{108}{x^2}$. Total surface area of open chamber (bottom base $+$ $4$ vertical walls): $$S = x^2 + 4xy = x^2 + 4x\left(\dfrac{108}{x^2}\right) = x^2 + \dfrac{432}{x}$$ [1 Mark]

  2. Find Critical Point: Differentiate $S$ with respect to $x$: $$\dfrac{dS}{dx} = 2x - \dfrac{432}{x^2}$$ Set $\dfrac{dS}{dx} = 0 \implies 2x = \dfrac{432}{x^2} \implies 2x^3 = 432 \implies x^3 = 216 \implies x = 6\text{ m}$. [1 Mark]

  3. (i) Second Derivative Test & Minimum Area: $$\dfrac{d^2S}{dx^2} = \dfrac{d}{dx}\left(2x - 432x^{-2}\right) = 2 + \dfrac{864}{x^3}$$ At $x = 6$: $$\left.\dfrac{d^2S}{dx^2}\right|_{x = 6} = 2 + \dfrac{864}{216} = 2 + 4 = 6 > 0$$ Since $\dfrac{d^2S}{dx^2} > 0$, $S$ is minimum at $x = 6\text{ m}$.

  4. Optimal Height: $y = \dfrac{108}{6^2} = \dfrac{108}{36} = 3\text{ m}$.
  5. Minimum Surface Area: $$S_{\min} = 6^2 + \dfrac{432}{6} = 36 + 72 = 108\text{ m}^2$$ [2 Marks]

OR

(ii) Minimum Cost Calculation: $$\text{Total Cost} = S_{\min} \times \text{Rate} = 108\text{ m}^2 \times ₹250/\text{m}^2 = ₹27,000$$ [2 Marks]

Question 9 [CBSE 2023 / 2020 Delhi/AI, 5 Marks] ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ [Must-Know / Evergreen]

Topic: Maximum Volume of Cone with Given Slant Height

Show that the semi-vertical angle of the cone of maximum volume and of given slant height $l$ is $\tan^{-1}\sqrt{2}$.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution:

  1. Geometric Setup: Let $l$ be the given constant slant height of the cone. Let $\alpha$ be the semi-vertical angle of the cone, where $0 < \alpha < \dfrac{\pi}{2}$.
  2. Radius of base: $r = l\sin\alpha$

  3. Height of cone: $h = l\cos\alpha$ [1 Mark]

  4. Volume Function in Terms of $\alpha$: $$V = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi (l\sin\alpha)^2 (l\cos\alpha) = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi l^3 \sin^2\alpha \cos\alpha$$ [1 Mark]

  5. Differentiate with Respect to $\alpha$: $$\dfrac{dV}{d\alpha} = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi l^3 \left[ \dfrac{d}{d\alpha}(\sin^2\alpha) \cos\alpha + \sin^2\alpha \dfrac{d}{d\alpha}(\cos\alpha) \right]$$ $$\dfrac{dV}{d\alpha} = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi l^3 \left[ 2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha \cdot \cos\alpha + \sin^2\alpha (-\sin\alpha) \right]$$ $$\dfrac{dV}{d\alpha} = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi l^3 \sin\alpha \left( 2\cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha \right)$$ [1 Mark]

  6. Critical Point: Set $\dfrac{dV}{d\alpha} = 0$. Since $\alpha \in \left(0, \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)$, $\sin\alpha \neq 0$. $$2\cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha = 0 \implies \sin^2\alpha = 2\cos^2\alpha$$ Divide by $\cos^2\alpha$: $$\tan^2\alpha = 2 \implies \tan\alpha = \sqrt{2} \implies \alpha = \tan^{-1}\sqrt{2}$$ [1 Mark]

  7. Second Derivative Test for Maximality: Differentiating $\dfrac{dV}{d\alpha}$: $$\dfrac{d^2V}{d\alpha^2} = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi l^3 \left[ \cos\alpha(2\cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha) + \sin\alpha(-4\cos\alpha\sin\alpha - 2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha) \right]$$ $$\dfrac{d^2V}{d\alpha^2} = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi l^3 \left[ \cos\alpha(2\cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha) - 6\sin^2\alpha\cos\alpha \right]$$ At $\tan^2\alpha = 2$ (where $2\cos^2\alpha - \sin^2\alpha = 0$): $$\left.\dfrac{d^2V}{d\alpha^2}\right|_{\alpha = \tan^{-1}\sqrt{2}} = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi l^3 \left[ 0 - 6\sin^2\alpha\cos\alpha \right] = -2\pi l^3 \sin^2\alpha\cos\alpha < 0$$ Since $\dfrac{d^2V}{d\alpha^2} < 0$, the volume $V$ is maximum when $\alpha = \tan^{-1}\sqrt{2}$. Hence proved. [1 Mark]

Topic: Minimizing Surface Area of Box with Square Base

Show that the surface area of a closed cuboid with a square base and a given volume $V$ is minimum when it is a cube.

📝 View CBSE Model Solution & Step-Marking Scheme ▼

Step-by-Step Model Solution:

  1. Define Variables & Volume Relation: Let the side of the square base be $x$ and the height of the cuboid be $y$. Volume $V$ is given as constant: $$V = x^2 y \implies y = \dfrac{V}{x^2}$$ [1 Mark]

  2. Formulate Total Surface Area $S$: A closed cuboid has $2$ square faces (top and bottom) and $4$ rectangular vertical faces: $$S = 2x^2 + 4xy = 2x^2 + 4x\left(\dfrac{V}{x^2}\right) = 2x^2 + \dfrac{4V}{x}$$ [1 Mark]

  3. First Derivative & Critical Point: $$\dfrac{dS}{dx} = 4x - \dfrac{4V}{x^2}$$ Set $\dfrac{dS}{dx} = 0 \implies 4x = \dfrac{4V}{x^2} \implies 4x^3 = 4V \implies x^3 = V \implies x = V^{1/3}$ [1 Mark]

  4. Second Derivative Test: $$\dfrac{d^2S}{dx^2} = \dfrac{d}{dx}\left(4x - 4Vx^{-2}\right) = 4 + \dfrac{8V}{x^3}$$ Substitute $x^3 = V$: $$\left.\dfrac{d^2S}{dx^2}\right|_{x^3 = V} = 4 + \dfrac{8V}{V} = 4 + 8 = 12 > 0$$ Since $\dfrac{d^2S}{dx^2} > 0$, the surface area $S$ is strictly minimum when $x^3 = V$. [1 Mark]

  5. Prove Cuboid is a Cube: Substitute $x^3 = V$ into the relation for height $y$: $$y = \dfrac{V}{x^2} = \dfrac{x^3}{x^2} = x$$ Since base side $x$ equals height $y$ ($x = y$), all edges of the cuboid are equal. Therefore, the closed cuboid of minimum surface area is a cube. Hence proved. [1 Mark]

1. Speed Hacks & Fast Solving Shortcuts

1.1 Fast Derivatives & Objective Shortcuts

  • Logarithmic Derivative Mental Trick: For product/quotient expressions $y = \prod f_i(x)^{\alpha_i}$, differentiate instantaneously using:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = y \sum \alpha_i \frac{f_i'(x)}{f_i(x)} $$

Example: $y = \frac{(x-1)\sqrt{x+2}}{(2x+3)^3} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = y\left[\frac{1}{x-1} + \frac{1}{2(x+2)} - \frac{6}{2x+3}\right]$. Skip the tedious multi-line product-quotient expansions.

  • Implicit Differentiation Shortcut: For any implicit equation $F(x, y) = 0$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{F_x}{F_y} = -\frac{\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}} $$

Use this to cross-check in 5 seconds before writing out the formal step-by-step differentiation.

  • Monotonicity via Discriminant on Quadratics: If $f'(x) = Ax^2 + Bx + C$:
  • Strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R} \iff A > 0 \text{ and } \Delta = B^2 - 4AC \le 0$
  • Strictly decreasing on $\mathbb{R} \iff A < 0 \text{ and } \Delta = B^2 - 4AC \le 0$ (Board Note: Mentioning $\Delta \le 0$ immediately validates that $f'(x)$ never changes sign).

1.3 Objective Optimization Hack (Function Substitution)

  • Squaring the Objective Function: When minimizing/maximizing distance $D = \sqrt{(x-x_0)^2 + (y-y_0)^2}$ or area containing roots, always optimize $Z = D^2$.

$$ \frac{dZ}{dx} = 2D\frac{dD}{dx} \implies \frac{dZ}{dx} = 0 \iff \frac{dD}{dx} = 0 \quad (\text{since } D > 0) $$

This avoids evaluating derivatives of square roots. Mention: "Since $Z = D^2$ and $D > 0$, $D$ is maximum/minimum when $Z$ is maximum/minimum."

2. Examiner Traps & Common Board Evaluation Mistakes

2.1 The Specific Pitfalls Where Students Lose $\frac{1}{2}$ to $1$ Mark

  1. Forgetting to Test Sign of $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$ (The Incomplete Second Derivative Trap):
  2. The Mistake: Finding the critical point (e.g., $r = \sqrt{S/6\pi}$) and writing "hence volume is maximum" without finding $\frac{d^2V}{dr^2}$.

  3. Penalty: $-1$ full mark. You must evaluate $\left.\frac{d^2V}{dr^2}\right|_{r=r_0}$, substitute $r_0$, and explicitly write: "< 0, therefore by Second Derivative Test, V is maximum at r = ...".

  4. Open vs. Closed Intervals in Increasing/Decreasing:

  5. NCERT convention:
    • A function $f$ is strictly increasing on an open interval $(a, b)$ if $f'(x) > 0$ for all $x \in (a, b)$.
    • When stating intervals on which a continuous function is increasing, NCERT writes closed intervals $[a, b]$ if $f'(x) \ge 0$ on $(a, b)$ and $f$ is continuous at $a$ and $b$.

  6. The Trap: Writing $(0, \pi/2]$ where the boundary point is undefined or makes $f'(x)$ explode. If $f'(x)$ is undefined at a point, you cannot include it in the interval. Always specify open intervals for strict monotonicity to avoid losing $\frac{1}{2}$ mark unless continuous up to endpoints.

  7. Rate of Change Signs (Leaking/Decreasing Quantities):

  8. If water leaks out of a conical tank at $5\text{ cm}^3/\text{s}$, writing $\frac{dV}{dt} = 5$ instead of $\frac{dV}{dt} = -5\text{ cm}^3/\text{s}$ results in an erroneous negative radius rate or sign error.

  9. Penalty: $-1$ mark for wrong equation setup.

  10. Omitting Units in Final Answers:

  11. An answer written as 12 instead of 12 cm/s or 48 instead of 48 cm²/s incurs an automatic $\frac{1}{2}$ mark deduction.

  12. Chain Rule Lapse in Rate of Measure:

  13. For spherical balloon volume $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$:

$$ \frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2 \frac{\mathbf{dr}}{\mathbf{dt}} $$

  • Many students write $\frac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2$, conflating $\frac{dV}{dr}$ with $\frac{dV}{dt}$.

  • Extraneous Critical Points in Geometry:

  • Discarding negative roots (e.g., $x = -2$ for length/radius) must be explicitly justified: "Since dimension $x > 0$, $x = -2$ is rejected." Skipping this justification costs $\frac{1}{2}$ mark.

3. Golden Points to Remember (NCERT Theorems & High-Yield Truths)

  1. Critical Points Definition: A point $c \in \text{Dom}(f)$ is a critical point if:

$$ f'(c) = 0 \quad \text{OR} \quad f'(c) \text{ does not exist} $$

(Example: $f(x) = |x|$ has a critical point at $x = 0$ because $f'(0)$ does not exist; $f(x) = x^{2/3}$ has a critical point at $x = 0$).

  1. First Derivative Test Condition:
  2. At $x = c$, if $f'(x)$ changes sign from positive to negative as $x$ increases through $c \implies$ Local Maximum.
  3. If $f'(x)$ changes sign from negative to positive $\implies$ Local Minimum.

  4. If $f'(x)$ does not change sign $\implies$ Point of Inflection (e.g., $f(x) = x^3$ at $x = 0$).

  5. Absolute (Global) Maxima/Minima on a Closed Interval $[a, b]$:

  6. Do NOT use the Second Derivative Test for absolute extrema on $[a, b]$.

  7. Algorithm:

    1. Find all critical points $c_i \in (a, b)$ where $f'(c_i) = 0$ or undefined.
    2. Evaluate $f(x)$ at all $c_i$ AND at endpoints $x = a$ and $x = b$.
    3. $\text{Absolute Maximum} = \max \{f(a), f(b), f(c_i)\}$, $\text{Absolute Minimum} = \min \{f(a), f(b), f(c_i)\}$.

  8. Strict Monotonicity vs. Stationary Intervals:

  9. If $f'(x) \ge 0$ on $(a, b)$ and $f'(x) = 0$ only at isolated discrete points (like $f(x) = x^3 \implies f'(0) = 0$), $f(x)$ is still strictly increasing.
  10. If $f'(x) = 0$ over a non-degenerate interval, it is purely non-decreasing, not strictly increasing.

1. Pedagogical Theory & Mathematical Intuition

1.1 The Geometric Interpretation of the Derivative (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Consider a real-valued function $y = f(x)$ defined and continuous on an open interval $I$, and let it be differentiable at an interior point $x_0 \in I$.

Let $P(x_0, y_0)$ be a fixed point on the curve where $y_0 = f(x_0)$. Choose a neighboring point $Q(x_0 + \Delta x, f(x_0 + \Delta x))$ on the curve.

The line passing through $P$ and $Q$ is a secant line to the curve. The slope of the secant line $PQ$, denoted by $m_{\text{sec}}$, is:

$$ m_{\text{sec}} = \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{(x_0 + \Delta x) - x_0} = \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} $$

As $Q \to P$ along the curve, $\Delta x \to 0$. If the limit of $m_{\text{sec}}$ exists as $\Delta x \to 0$, the limiting position of the secant line $PQ$ becomes the tangent line to the curve at point $P$.

$$ \lim_{Q \to P} m_{\text{sec}} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x} = f'(x_0) = \left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right|_{(x_0, y_0)} $$

Core Definition (Tangent Line):
The tangent line to the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $P(x_0, y_0)$ is the straight line passing through $P$ with slope equal to the derivative of the function evaluated at $x_0$, i.e., $m_T = f'(x_0) = \left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right|_{(x_0, y_0)}$.

1.2 Definition of the Normal Line (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Core Definition (Normal Line):
The normal line to a curve $y = f(x)$ at a given point $P(x_0, y_0)$ is the straight line passing through $P$ that is perpendicular to the tangent line at $P$.

From coordinate geometry, if two non-vertical lines with slopes $m_1$ and $m_2$ are perpendicular, then:

$$ m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1 \implies m_2 = -\frac{1}{m_1} $$

Thus, if $m_T = \left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right|_{(x_0, y_0)} \neq 0$, the slope of the normal $m_N$ is:

$$ m_N = -\frac{1}{\left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right|_{(x_0, y_0)}} = -\left. \frac{dx}{dy} \right|_{(x_0, y_0)} $$

1.4 Angle of Intersection of Two Curves (⭐⭐⭐)

Definition: The angle of intersection between two intersecting curves $y = f(x)$ and $y = g(x)$ at their point of intersection $P(x_1, y_1)$ is defined as the acute angle $\theta$ between their respective tangents at $P$.

If $m_1 = f'(x_1)$ and $m_2 = g'(x_1)$, then:

$$ \tan \theta = \left| \frac{m_1 - m_2}{1 + m_1 m_2} \right| $$

  • Orthogonal Curves Condition: The two curves cut each other orthogonally (at right angles) if and only if:

$$ m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1 $$

  • Tangential Curves Condition: The curves touch each other at $P$ if and only if:

$$ m_1 = m_2 $$

2. Step-by-Step Mathematical Proofs & Derivations

Proof 2.1: Derivation of the Equation of Tangent and Normal (⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement

Let $y = f(x)$ be a differentiable curve on an interval $I$, and let $P(x_0, y_0)$ be a point on the curve.
Prove that: 1. The equation of the tangent at $P$ is:

$$ y - y_0 = f'(x_0)(x - x_0) $$

  1. If $f'(x_0) \neq 0$, the equation of the normal at $P$ is:

$$ y - y_0 = -\frac{1}{f'(x_0)}(x - x_0) $$

Proof

Step 1: Equation of Tangent * Given: A curve $y = f(x)$ differentiable at $x = x_0$, and a point $P(x_0, y_0)$ lying on it ($y_0 = f(x_0)$). * By definition of the derivative as the slope of the tangent line:

$$ m_T = \left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right|_{(x_0, y_0)} = f'(x_0) $$

  • The point-slope form of a straight line passing through $(x_0, y_0)$ with slope $m$ is given by:

$$ y - y_0 = m(x - x_0) $$

  • Substituting $m = m_T = f'(x_0)$:

$$ y - y_0 = f'(x_0)(x - x_0) $$

Step 2: Equation of Normal * Given: The normal line is perpendicular to the tangent line at $P(x_0, y_0)$. * Let the slope of the normal be $m_N$. By the condition of perpendicularity for non-vertical lines:

$$ m_T \cdot m_N = -1 $$

  • Since $m_T = f'(x_0) \neq 0$:

$$ m_N = -\frac{1}{m_T} = -\frac{1}{f'(x_0)} $$

  • Using point-slope form for the normal line passing through $P(x_0, y_0)$ with slope $m_N$:

$$ y - y_0 = -\frac{1}{f'(x_0)}(x - x_0) $$

  • Alternatively expressed as:

$$ (y - y_0)f'(x_0) + (x - x_0) = 0 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Proof 2.2: Tangents for Parametric Curves (⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Statement

Let a curve be given parametrically by $x = f(t)$ and $y = g(t)$, where $f$ and $g$ are differentiable functions of a parameter $t$. Prove that the slope of the tangent at $t = t_0$ (where $f'(t_0) \neq 0$) is:

$$ \left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right|_{t=t_0} = \frac{g'(t_0)}{f'(t_0)} $$

Proof

  • Given: $x = f(t)$ and $y = g(t)$.
  • By the Chain Rule of differential calculus:

$$ \frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx} \cdot \frac{dx}{dt} $$

  • Provided $\frac{dx}{dt} = f'(t) \neq 0$, dividing both sides by $\frac{dx}{dt}$ yields:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\frac{dy}{dt}}{\frac{dx}{dt}} = \frac{g'(t)}{f'(t)} $$

  • Evaluating at $t = t_0$:

$$ m_T = \left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right|_{t=t_0} = \frac{g'(t_0)}{f'(t_0)} $$

  • The coordinates of the point of contact are $(x_0, y_0) = (f(t_0), g(t_0))$.
  • Therefore, the equation of the tangent is:

$$ y - g(t_0) = \frac{g'(t_0)}{f'(t_0)} \left( x - f(t_0) \right) $$

$$ g'(t_0)(x - f(t_0)) - f'(t_0)(y - g(t_0)) = 0 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

3. High-Yield Solved Examples & Recent 5-Year Board PYQs (2020–2025)

Example 3.1: Parametric Normal at Specific Parameter [CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]

Problem: Find the equation of the normal to the curve $x = a \cos^3 \theta$, $y = a \sin^3 \theta$ at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$.

Solution: 1. Find point of contact $(x_0, y_0)$ at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$:

$$ x_0 = a \cos^3\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = a \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^3 = \frac{a}{2\sqrt{2}} $$

$$ y_0 = a \sin^3\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = a \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^3 = \frac{a}{2\sqrt{2}} $$

  1. Compute derivatives with respect to $\theta$:

$$ \frac{dx}{d\theta} = 3a \cos^2 \theta (-\sin \theta) = -3a \cos^2 \theta \sin \theta $$

$$ \frac{dy}{d\theta} = 3a \sin^2 \theta (\cos \theta) = 3a \sin^2 \theta \cos \theta $$

  1. Compute $\frac{dy}{dx}$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\frac{dy}{d\theta}}{\frac{dx}{d\theta}} = \frac{3a \sin^2 \theta \cos \theta}{-3a \cos^2 \theta \sin \theta} = -\tan \theta $$

  1. Evaluate slope of tangent and normal at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$:

$$ m_T = \left. \frac{dy}{dx} \right|_{\theta = \pi/4} = -\tan\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = -1 $$

$$ m_N = -\frac{1}{m_T} = -\frac{1}{-1} = 1 $$

  1. Write equation of the normal:

$$ y - y_0 = m_N (x - x_0) $$

$$ y - \frac{a}{2\sqrt{2}} = 1 \cdot \left(x - \frac{a}{2\sqrt{2}}\right) $$

$$ y - x = 0 \implies x - y = 0 $$

Example 3.2: Tangent Parallel to a Given Line [CBSE 2023, 3 Marks]

Problem: Find the points on the curve $y = x^3 - 11x + 5$ at which the tangent is parallel to the line $y = x - 11$. Hence, find the equation of such tangent(s).

Solution: 1. Identify the required slope: The given line is $y = x - 11$, which has slope $m = 1$. Since the tangent is parallel to this line:

$$ m_T = \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 $$

  1. Differentiate the curve:

$$ y = x^3 - 11x + 5 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = 3x^2 - 11 $$

  1. Set $\frac{dy}{dx} = 1$ and solve for $x$:

$$ 3x^2 - 11 = 1 \implies 3x^2 = 12 \implies x^2 = 4 \implies x = \pm 2 $$

  1. Find corresponding $y$-coordinates on the curve:
  2. For $x = 2$:

$$ y = (2)^3 - 11(2) + 5 = 8 - 22 + 5 = -9 \implies P_1(2, -9) $$

  • For $x = -2$:

$$ y = (-2)^3 - 11(-2) + 5 = -8 + 22 + 5 = 19 \implies P_2(-2, 19) $$

  1. Write equations of the tangents:
  2. At $P_1(2, -9)$ with $m = 1$:

$$ y - (-9) = 1(x - 2) \implies y + 9 = x - 2 \implies x - y - 11 = 0 $$

 *(Note: This is identical to the line $y = x - 11$ itself; check whether the problem asks for points or distinct lines. Both $P_1$ and $P_2$ are valid points on the curve).*
  • At $P_2(-2, 19)$ with $m = 1$:

$$ y - 19 = 1(x - (-2)) \implies y - 19 = x + 2 \implies x - y + 21 = 0 $$

Example 3.3: Orthogonality of Curves [CBSE 2022 Term-2 / 2020, 5 Marks]

Problem: Prove that the curves $x = y^2$ and $xy = k$ cut at right angles if $8k^2 = 1$.

Solution: 1. Find point of intersection $P(x, y)$: Given equations:

$$ (1) \quad x = y^2 $$

$$ (2) \quad xy = k $$

Substitute (1) into (2):

$$ (y^2)y = k \implies y^3 = k \implies y = k^{1/3} $$

Then:

$$ x = y^2 = (k^{1/3})^2 = k^{2/3} $$

Point of intersection is $P\left(k^{2/3}, k^{1/3}\right)$.

  1. Find slope $m_1$ of tangent to $x = y^2$: Differentiating with respect to $x$:

$$ 1 = 2y \frac{dy}{dx} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2y} $$

At point $P$:

$$ m_1 = \frac{1}{2 k^{1/3}} $$

  1. Find slope $m_2$ of tangent to $xy = k$: Differentiating with respect to $x$ using product rule:

$$ x \frac{dy}{dx} + y(1) = 0 \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{y}{x} $$

At point $P$:

$$ m_2 = -\frac{k^{1/3}}{k^{2/3}} = -\frac{1}{k^{1/3}} $$

  1. Apply orthogonality condition: Curves intersect at right angles $\iff m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$.

$$ m_1 \cdot m_2 = \left( \frac{1}{2 k^{1/3}} \right) \left( -\frac{1}{k^{1/3}} \right) = -\frac{1}{2 k^{2/3}} $$

Setting $m_1 \cdot m_2 = -1$:

$$ -\frac{1}{2 k^{2/3}} = -1 \implies 2 k^{2/3} = 1 $$

Cubing both sides:

$$ \left(2 k^{2/3}\right)^3 = 1^3 \implies 8 k^2 = 1 $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Example 3.4: Finding Points of Horizontal and Vertical Tangents [CBSE 2021 Comp., 3 Marks]

Problem: Find the points on the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{16} = 1$ at which the tangents are: (i) parallel to the $x$-axis, (ii) parallel to the $y$-axis.

Solution: 1. Differentiate implicitly with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{2x}{9} + \frac{2y}{16}\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies \frac{y}{8}\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{9} \implies \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{16x}{9y} $$

  1. Case (i): Tangent parallel to $x$-axis:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \implies -\frac{16x}{9y} = 0 \implies x = 0 \quad (y \neq 0) $$

Substitute $x = 0$ into the ellipse equation:

$$ \frac{0}{9} + \frac{y^2}{16} = 1 \implies y^2 = 16 \implies y = \pm 4 $$

Points: $(0, 4)$ and $(0, -4)$.

  1. Case (ii): Tangent parallel to $y$-axis: The tangent is vertical, so $\frac{dx}{dy} = 0$ (or slope is undefined):

$$ \frac{dx}{dy} = -\frac{9y}{16x} = 0 \implies y = 0 \quad (x \neq 0) $$

Substitute $y = 0$ into the ellipse equation:

$$ \frac{x^2}{9} + 0 = 1 \implies x^2 = 9 \implies x = \pm 3 $$

Points: $(3, 0)$ and $(-3, 0)$.

4. Examiner Traps & Common Student Pitfalls

Trap 4.1: Substituting the Given Line Equation instead of the Curve Equation

  • The Error: When a problem states "Tangent is parallel to line $L$", students compute $x$ from $\frac{dy}{dx} = m_L$, but then plug $x$ into the equation of line $L$ instead of the equation of the original curve to find $y$.
  • The Rule: The point of contact $(x_0, y_0)$ must lie on the curve. Always substitute $x_0$ back into $y = f(x)$.

Trap 4.2: Handling Vertical Tangents ($m_T \to \infty$)

  • The Error: Writing $\frac{dy}{dx} = \infty$ and attempting to use point-slope form: $y - y_0 = \infty(x - x_0)$, leading to algebraic deadlock or division by zero.
  • The Rule: If the denominator of $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is $0$, express the slope in reciprocal form: $\frac{dx}{dy} = 0$.
  • Equation of vertical tangent: $x = x_0$
  • Equation of corresponding normal (horizontal): $y = y_0$

Trap 4.3: Failing to Verify Point Validity

  • The Error: For curves defined on restricted domains (e.g., $y = \sqrt{4 - x^2}$ or curves with fractions/logarithms), students fail to discard extraneous values of $x$ generated when solving $\frac{dy}{dx} = m$.
  • The Rule: Always verify that the computed $(x_0, y_0)$ satisfies the curve's original domain and equation.

Trap 4.4: Sign Errors in Parametric Derivatives

  • The Error: In parametric forms like $x = a(1 - \cos \theta)$, $y = a(\theta + \sin \theta)$, students evaluate $m_N$ by directly taking $-\frac{dy/d\theta}{dx/d\theta}$ instead of the proper inverted negative reciprocal:

$$ m_N = -\frac{1}{dy/dx} = -\frac{dx/d\theta}{dy/d\theta} $$

5. Speed Hacks & Golden Examination Takeaways

5.1 The "$T = 0$" Shortcut for Any Second-Degree Curve

For any general second-degree conic $S \equiv ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0$, the tangent at point $(x_0, y_0)$ lying on the curve can be written directly without differentiating by the standard transformation: * $x^2 \to x \cdot x_0$ * $y^2 \to y \cdot y_0$ * $2xy \to x y_0 + x_0 y$ * $2x \to x + x_0$ * $2y \to y + y_0$

$$ \text{Tangent } T: a x x_0 + h(x y_0 + x_0 y) + b y y_0 + g(x + x_0) + f(y + y_0) + c = 0 $$

(Tip: In board exams, always show $\frac{dy}{dx}$ explicitly for method marks, but use $T = 0$ to verify your final equation in under 5 seconds).

5.2 Implicit Derivative Fast Formula

For any implicit curve $F(x, y) = 0$:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{\frac{\partial F}{\partial x}}{\frac{\partial F}{\partial y}} $$

Differentiate $F$ treating $y$ as constant, divided by derivative of $F$ treating $x$ as constant, with a negative sign in front.

1. PEDAGOGICAL THEORY & MATHEMATICAL INTUITION

Priority Tag: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Core Foundation / High CBSE Board Frequency)

The differential calculus approach to approximation leverages the local linearity of differentiable functions. On an infinitesimal scale, every smooth curve resembles its tangent line. We extend this local tangent approximation to estimate finite small changes in dependent variables.

1.1 Core Definitions & Terminology (NCERT Framework)

Let $y = f(x)$ be a real-valued function defined on an open interval $I \subseteq \mathbb{R}$. Let $x \in I$ be a fixed reference point.

  1. Increment in Independent Variable ($\Delta x$): A small positive or negative change in $x$.

  2. Differential of the Independent Variable ($dx$): Defined identically to its increment:

$$ dx = \Delta x $$

  1. Increment in Dependent Variable ($\Delta y$): The exact change produced in $y$ corresponding to $\Delta x$:

$$ \Delta y = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) $$

  1. Differential of the Dependent Variable ($dy$): The linear principal part of the increment $\Delta y$, defined as:

$$ dy = f'(x)\,dx = \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)\Delta x $$

  1. Fundamental Relation of Approximation: When $\Delta x$ is small ($\Delta x \to 0$), the actual increment $\Delta y$ is approximated by the differential $dy$:

$$ \Delta y \approx dy $$

$$ f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) \approx f'(x)\,\Delta x $$

$$ \implies f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x $$

1.2 Geometric Interpretation

Consider the Cartesian plane with the curve $y = f(x)$:

  • Let $P(x, y)$ and $Q(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y)$ be two neighboring points on the curve $y = f(x)$.
  • Draw the tangent line to the curve at point $P(x, y)$. The slope of this tangent line is:

$$ m = \tan\theta = f'(x) $$

  • Let the vertical line through $x + \Delta x$ intersect:
  • The horizontal line through $P$ at $R(x + \Delta x, y)$.
  • The curve at $Q(x + \Delta x, y + \Delta y)$.
  • The tangent line at $T(x + \Delta x, y + dy)$.

From the right-angled triangle $\Delta PRT$:

$$ \tan\theta = \frac{RT}{PR} = \frac{RT}{\Delta x} $$

$$ RT = \tan\theta \cdot \Delta x = f'(x)\,\Delta x = dy $$

Meanwhile, the total vertical displacement from $P$ to the curve at $Q$ is:

$$ RQ = \Delta y = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) $$

The difference between the actual change and the linear approximation is:

$$ TQ = RQ - RT = \Delta y - dy $$

As $\Delta x \to 0$, $Q \to P$, the secant line $PQ$ approaches the tangent line $PT$, causing the error $TQ \to 0$ faster than $\Delta x$. Thus:

$$ \Delta y \approx dy \quad \text{for small } \Delta x $$

1.3 Types of Errors (Star Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐)

Let $x$ denote the measured value of a physical quantity, and let $\Delta x = dx$ denote the error committed in measuring $x$.

Relative Error $\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}$ Dimensionless ratio measuring error per unit baseline quantity.
Percentage Error $\left(\dfrac{\Delta x}{x} \times 100\right)\%$ Expresses relative error on a centesimal scale.

Error Propagation in a Derived Quantity $y = f(x)$:

  • Absolute Error in $y$:

$$ dy = f'(x)\,\Delta x $$

  • Relative Error in $y$:

$$ \frac{dy}{y} = \frac{f'(x)\,\Delta x}{f(x)} $$

  • Percentage Error in $y$:

$$ \frac{dy}{y} \times 100 = \left(\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}\Delta x\right) \times 100 $$

2. COMPLETE STEP-BY-STEP MATHEMATICAL PROOFS & DERIVATIONS

Theorem 2.1: The Fundamental Approximation Formula

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Let $f: I \to \mathbb{R}$ be differentiable at $x \in I$. If $\Delta x$ is a small increment in $x$, then:

$$ f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x $$

Given: $f(x)$ is differentiable at $x$, which implies that the derivative limit exists:

$$ f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} $$

To Prove:

$$ f(x + \Delta x) = f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x + \varepsilon \cdot \Delta x, \quad \text{where } \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \varepsilon = 0 $$

which yields the first-order approximation $f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x$.

Proof: Define the difference quotient function for $\Delta x \neq 0$:

$$ g(\Delta x) = \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} $$

By the definition of derivative:

$$ \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} g(\Delta x) = f'(x) $$

Define the error term $\varepsilon(\Delta x)$ as:

$$ \varepsilon = \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} - f'(x) $$

Taking the limit as $\Delta x \to 0$ on both sides:

$$ \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \varepsilon = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \left[ \frac{f(x + \Delta x) - f(x)}{\Delta x} - f'(x) \right] = f'(x) - f'(x) = 0 $$

Multiplying both sides by $\Delta x$:

$$ \varepsilon \cdot \Delta x = f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) - f'(x)\,\Delta x $$

Rearranging terms:

$$ f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) = f'(x)\,\Delta x + \varepsilon \cdot \Delta x $$

$$ f(x + \Delta x) = f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x + \varepsilon \cdot \Delta x $$

Since $\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \varepsilon = 0$, the term $\varepsilon \cdot \Delta x$ is an infinitesimal of order higher than $\Delta x$ (i.e., $o(\Delta x)$).

Neglecting $\varepsilon \cdot \Delta x$ for small values of $\Delta x$:

$$ f(x + \Delta x) - f(x) \approx f'(x)\,\Delta x $$

$$ \implies f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + f'(x)\,\Delta x $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

Theorem 2.2: Power Rule for Percentage Error Propagation

Priority: ⭐⭐⭐⭐

Statement: Let $y = k \cdot x^n$, where $k$ is a non-zero real constant and $n \in \mathbb{Q}$. The percentage error in $y$ is approximately $n$ times the percentage error in $x$:

$$ \left(\frac{\Delta y}{y} \times 100\right) \approx n \left(\frac{\Delta x}{x} \times 100\right) $$

Given: $y = k x^n$, with increment $\Delta x$ in $x$ and induced increment $\Delta y$ in $y$.

To Prove:

$$ \frac{dy}{y} = n \frac{dx}{x} $$

Proof: Taking the natural logarithm on both sides of $y = k x^n$:

$$ \ln y = \ln(k \cdot x^n) = \ln k + n \ln x $$

Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$:

$$ \frac{d}{dx}(\ln y) = \frac{d}{dx}(\ln k + n \ln x) $$

$$ \frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = 0 + n \cdot \frac{1}{x} $$

$$ \frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{n}{x} $$

Multiplying both sides by the differential $dx = \Delta x$:

$$ \frac{1}{y} \left(\frac{dy}{dx} dx\right) = n \frac{dx}{x} $$

$$ \frac{dy}{y} = n \frac{dx}{x} $$

Multiplying both sides by 100 to convert into percentage error:

$$ \left(\frac{dy}{y} \times 100\right) = n \left(\frac{dx}{x} \times 100\right) $$

Since $\Delta y \approx dy$ and $\Delta x = dx$:

$$ \left(\frac{\Delta y}{y} \times 100\right) \approx n \left(\frac{\Delta x}{x} \times 100\right) $$

$$ \text{Q.E.D.} $$

3. HIGH-YIELD EXAMPLES & 5-YEAR PYQS (2020–2025)

Example 3.1: Fractional Power Root Approximation

[CBSE 2024, 3 Marks]
Question: Using differentials, find the approximate value of $(25.3)^{1/2}$ correct to 3 decimal places.

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Define the Function and Reference Points: Let $y = f(x) = x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}$. Decompose $25.3$ into a base value $x$ whose square root is an integer and a remainder $\Delta x$:

$$ x = 25, \quad \Delta x = +0.3 $$

$$ x + \Delta x = 25.3 $$

  1. Evaluate the Base Function:

$$ y = f(25) = \sqrt{25} = 5 $$

  1. Compute the First Derivative:

$$ \frac{dy}{dx} = f'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}(x^{1/2}) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}} $$

At $x = 25$:

$$ f'(25) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{25}} = \frac{1}{2 \times 5} = \frac{1}{10} = 0.1 $$

  1. Calculate Differential $dy$:

$$ dy = f'(x)\,\Delta x = (0.1)(0.3) = 0.03 $$

  1. Apply the Approximation Formula:

$$ f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + dy $$

$$ (25.3)^{1/2} \approx 5 + 0.03 = 5.030 $$

Final Answer: $(25.3)^{1/2} \approx 5.030$

Example 3.2: Radical Evaluation with Negative Increment

[CBSE 2023, 3 Marks]
Question: Using differentials, evaluate $(0.999)^{1/10}$ up to 3 decimal places.

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Identify the Function: Let $y = f(x) = x^{1/10}$.

  2. Choose Reference Points: Choose $x = 1$, as $1^{1/10} = 1$.

$$ \Delta x = 0.999 - 1 = -0.001 $$

  1. Compute Derivative at Base Point:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{1}{10} x^{\frac{1}{10} - 1} = \frac{1}{10 x^{9/10}} $$

At $x = 1$:

$$ f'(1) = \frac{1}{10 (1)^{9/10}} = \frac{1}{10} = 0.1 $$

  1. Compute $dy$:

$$ dy = f'(x)\,\Delta x = (0.1)(-0.001) = -0.0001 $$

  1. Combine to Find Approximate Value:

$$ f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + dy $$

$$ (0.999)^{1/10} \approx 1 + (-0.0001) = 0.9999 \approx 1.000 $$

To 4 decimal places, it is $0.9999$; rounding off to 3 decimal places gives:

$$ (0.999)^{1/10} \approx 0.9999 \text{ (or } 1.000 \text{ to 3 decimal places)} $$

Example 3.3: Error Propagation in Sphere Volume

[CBSE 2022 Term-II, 3 Marks]
Question: If the radius of a sphere is measured as $9\text{ cm}$ with an error of $0.03\text{ cm}$, find the approximate error in calculating its volume.

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Given Data: Radius $r = 9\text{ cm}$. Error in radius $\Delta r = dr = 0.03\text{ cm}$.

  2. Formulate the Governing Relation: Volume of a sphere:

$$ V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 $$

  1. Differentiate with Respect to $r$:

$$ \frac{dV}{dr} = \frac{4}{3}\pi \left(3r^2\right) = 4\pi r^2 $$

  1. Calculate Differential $dV$:

$$ dV = \left(\frac{dV}{dr}\right) \Delta r = 4\pi r^2\,\Delta r $$

  1. Substitute Numerical Values:

$$ dV = 4\pi (9)^2 \times 0.03 $$

$$ dV = 4\pi (81) \times 0.03 = 324\pi \times 0.03 = 9.72\pi\text{ cm}^3 $$

(Optional numerical expansion: $9.72 \times 3.1416 \approx 30.536\text{ cm}^3$)

Final Answer: Approximate error in volume is $9.72\pi\text{ cm}^3$.

Example 3.4: Solid Geometry & Percentage Error Propagation

[CBSE 2020, 4 Marks]
Question: Find the approximate percentage error in calculating the volume of a right circular cylinder if an error of $+2\%$ is made in measuring its radius and $-1\%$ in measuring its height.

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Establish the Function:

$$ V = \pi r^2 h $$

  1. Given Percentage Errors:

$$ \frac{\Delta r}{r} \times 100 = 2\% \implies \frac{dr}{r} = 0.02 $$

$$ \frac{\Delta h}{h} \times 100 = -1\% \implies \frac{dh}{h} = -0.01 $$

  1. Take Logarithmic Differentiation:

$$ \ln V = \ln(\pi r^2 h) = \ln\pi + 2\ln r + \ln h $$

  1. Compute Total Differential:

$$ \frac{dV}{V} = 0 + 2\frac{dr}{r} + \frac{dh}{h} $$

  1. Convert to Percentage Error:

$$ \left(\frac{dV}{V} \times 100\right) = 2\left(\frac{dr}{r} \times 100\right) + \left(\frac{dh}{h} \times 100\right) $$

$$ \left(\frac{dV}{V} \times 100\right) = 2(+2\%) + (-1\%) = 4\% - 1\% = 3\% $$

Final Answer: The approximate percentage error in the calculated volume is $+3\%$.

Example 3.5: Multi-term Polynomial Value Estimation

[CBSE 2021 Comp., 2 Marks]
Question: If $f(x) = 3x^2 + 15x + 5$, then find the approximate value of $f(3.02)$ using differentials.

Step-by-step Solution:

  1. Partition Arguments:

$$ x = 3, \quad \Delta x = 0.02 $$

  1. Calculate $f(x)$ at $x = 3$:

$$ f(3) = 3(3)^2 + 15(3) + 5 = 3(9) + 45 + 5 = 27 + 50 = 77 $$

  1. Compute Derivative $f'(x)$:

$$ f'(x) = \frac{d}{dx}(3x^2 + 15x + 5) = 6x + 15 $$

$$ f'(3) = 6(3) + 15 = 18 + 15 = 33 $$

  1. Compute Differential $dy$:

$$ \Delta y \approx f'(3)\,\Delta x = 33 \times 0.02 = 0.66 $$

  1. Sum Terms:

$$ f(3.02) \approx f(3) + \Delta y = 77 + 0.66 = 77.66 $$

Final Answer: $f(3.02) \approx 77.66$

4. EXAMINER TRAPS & COMMON MISTAKES

Trap 4.1: Sign Neglect in Negative Increments

  • The Pitfall: When evaluating $(26.8)^{1/3}$, choosing $x = 27$ means $\Delta x = 26.8 - 27 = -0.2$. Students often write $\Delta x = 0.2$ and add instead of subtract.
  • Correction Protocol: Always explicitly calculate:

$$ \Delta x = \text{Target} - \text{Base} $$

Trap 4.2: Angle Units in Trigonometric Approximations

  • The Pitfall: Finding $\cos(61^\circ)$ with $x = 60^\circ$ and $\Delta x = 1^\circ$. Writing:

$$ dy = -\sin(60^\circ) \times 1 = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \quad \mathbf{[INCORRECT!]} $$

  • Correction Protocol: Derivatives of trigonometric functions are derived using limits where angles are measured in radians. Therefore, $\Delta x$ must always be converted to radians:

$$ \Delta x = 1^\circ = 1 \times \frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{\pi}{180}\text{ radians} $$

$$ dy = -\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) \times \frac{\pi}{180} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}\pi}{360} $$

Trap 4.3: Base Selection Error

  • The Pitfall: For $(82)^{1/4}$, selecting $x = 100$ or $x = 80$ instead of $x = 81 = 3^4$.
  • Correction Protocol: Always choose a base $x$ such that $x^{1/n}$ yields an exact integer and $|\Delta x|$ is minimal.

Trap 4.4: Conflating "Percentage Error in $y$" with "Absolute Error in $y$"

  • The Pitfall: A question asks for the percentage error in surface area $S$, but the student only computes $dS$ and stops.
  • Correction Protocol:
  • Absolute error: $dS$
  • Relative error: $\dfrac{dS}{S}$
  • Percentage error: $\dfrac{dS}{S} \times 100$

5. SPEED HACKS & GOLDEN POINTS

5.1 The General Root Speed Formula

For any root of the form $(a^n \pm b)^{1/n}$ where $b \ll a^n$:

$$ (a^n \pm b)^{1/n} \approx a \pm \frac{b}{n \cdot a^{n-1}} $$

Direct Verification:

  • Square Root ($n = 2$):

$$ \sqrt{a^2 \pm b} \approx a \pm \frac{b}{2a} $$

Example: $\sqrt{25.3} = \sqrt{5^2 + 0.3} \approx 5 + \frac{0.3}{2(5)} = 5 + 0.03 = 5.03$ (Solved in 5 seconds).

  • Cube Root ($n = 3$):

$$ \sqrt[3]{a^3 \pm b} \approx a \pm \frac{b}{3a^2} $$

Example: $\sqrt[3]{26} = \sqrt[3]{3^3 - 1} \approx 3 - \frac{1}{3(3)^2} = 3 - \frac{1}{27} \approx 3 - 0.0370 = 2.963$.

5.2 Golden Points Table for Mensuration Error Problems

When linear dimension $x$ carries a small percentage error of $k\%$:

Solid Geometry Metric Power Dependence on $x$ Resulting Percentage Error
Perimeter / Circumference $\propto x^1$ $1 \times k\% = k\%$
Surface Area (Sphere, Cube) $\propto x^2$ $2 \times k\% = 2k\%$
Volume (Sphere, Cube) $\propto x^3$ $3 \times k\% = 3k\%$

Rule of Thumb: For formulas of the type $P = C \cdot x_1^{a} x_2^{b} x_3^{-c}$, the percentage error is:

$$ \%\text{ Error in } P \approx a(\%\text{ in } x_1) + b(\%\text{ in } x_2) - c(\%\text{ in } x_3) $$

5.3 Quick Reference Mnemonic: "D-B-D-S" Algorithm

When solving approximation problems in board exams, follow these four steps: 1. D — Define: State $y = f(x)$ clearly. 2. B — Base & Delta: Split target into $x$ (exact power) and $\Delta x$ (deviation). 3. D — Derivative: Compute $f'(x)$ and determine $dy = f'(x)\,\Delta x$. 4. S — Sum: Assemble final result $f(x + \Delta x) \approx f(x) + dy$.
